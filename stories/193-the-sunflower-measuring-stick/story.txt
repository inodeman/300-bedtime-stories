The sunflower stood taller than Paz's shoulder, its face turned toward the late light, yellow petals thick and a little dusty. Oscar held a smooth stick almost as tall as the fence. The garden smelled like warm soil and the green of tomato leaves nearby. A bee worked the brown center of the flower and did not glance at them. They stood a polite step back, where they could see everything and touch nothing that the bee needed. "If we measure it," Paz said, "we can greet it every day and know if it grew while we slept."

Oscar liked that kind of greeting. He set the stick in the soil beside the stem, not too close, so roots would not be bothered. Grandpa had said he would press the stick down if they held it steady. He did, one firm push, and the stick stayed. "No climbing the fence to look from above," Grandpa said mildly. "Your eyes are already at a good height." Paz tied a loose green string around the stick at the height of the flower's face. The string ends dangled like grass. The petals made a dry, papery sound when the breeze turned them.

That evening the sun sat low, and the sunflower's shadow stretched across the path, long as a story. Oscar's eyes widened. "It grew a whole extra Oscar," he said. Paz walked along the shadow and shook her head. "Shadows are big talkers," she said. "They stretch when the light is tired." Still, they marked the shadow's tip with a pebble, because curiosity is a kind of nature-looking too. The pebble was cool. The path held the day's heat. They went in for supper with dirt on their knees and a question folded in their pockets.

Morning smelled like wet leaves, though no one had watered yet. Dew beaded the sunflower's fuzzy stem. Oscar ran to the pebble, then stopped and laughed at himself. The shadow was short and fat, sitting close to the plant like a sleepy cat. "It shrank," he said, astonished. Paz touched the green string. It was still where they had tied it, right at yesterday's flower face. The real plant had not leapt or shrunk. The light had done the dancing. She felt relief and wonder in the same breath. Nature was honest if you asked it the right question.

"We measure the stem, not the shadow," she said. Grandpa brought a second string and showed them how to hold it level from the stick to the plant without bending the stem. "I will stand at the stick. You tell me when the string meets the flower's chin," he said. Oscar watched the string like a serious engineer of gardens. "There," he said. The flower's face was a thumb higher than the green knot. Not a huge change. A true one. Paz moved the knot, her fingers careful among the little stem hairs that tickled and did not hurt. The new knot smelled like the garden because the string did.

They made a habit of noticing while they measured. The leaves were rough on one side and smoother on the other, like a cat's tongue and then like cloth. The soil at the roots was darker where shade stayed. A second bee arrived, gold dust on its legs, and they gave it the whole flower without waving. Oscar heard a rustle and found a ladybug on the stick, round as a berry, and he let it walk off onto a leaf. Paz sniffed her fingers and found the sharp green smell of sunflower. Each day the knot moved a little, or sometimes not at all, and both answers were welcome.

On the third morning a new leaf had uncurled, small and bright. They did not pull it to see better. They looked from the side. Grandpa said plants like to open in their own time, the way bread does. Oscar wrote the changes on a card with pictures: a short line, a taller line, a bee, a leaf. Paz added the words "real height" so they would not chase shadows again. The card lived in a jar by the door, safe from rain. The stick in the garden wore its green knot like a quiet medal.

They ended that day sitting on the step, knees dusty, watching the flower turn a little more toward the west. The bee had gone home. The petals glowed at the edges. Oscar said, "It did not need to be taller than a shadow to be wonderful." Paz agreed. The soil under her nails was a badge she would wash off soon, but not before she liked it for a minute. Nature had given them a slow story, and they had learned how to read it without tugging.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the garden card in its jar. The sunflower outside was a dark friendly shape against the last blue of the sky. Oscar and Paz could not see the knot, but they knew it was there, a small true mark beside a living stem. The lantern light stayed indoors, warm and steady, while the plant kept its own quiet growing in the cool night air.
