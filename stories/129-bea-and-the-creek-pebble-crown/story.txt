At the shady bend the creek slowed down, as if it wanted people to see its stones. Bea crouched with a tin cup of water and a cloth bag that was already heavy with pebbles. She was making a crown, not to wear for long, just to set on a flat rock and admire. The best stones were small, oval, and shiny when wet: one like a drop of tea, one like a moon, one with a white ring. Uncle sat on a log and mended a sandal strap. "Leave the big rocks for the creek's furniture," he said. Bea agreed. She only wanted the jewels.

A visiting child came down the path with an empty tin that knocked against a knee. The child looked at Bea's bag and then at the water, hopeful and a little unsure. "Are all the shiny ones taken?" the child asked. Bea glanced at her bag. It was not all of them. The creek still held plenty. But the ones she had picked were the ones that had taken the most looking. She could say the bend was busy. She could keep the moon stone under her palm. The tin in the child's hand made a lonely sound.

Bea dipped a pebble and watched it wake into color. "I was building a crown," she said. "It was going to be only mine." She heard how tight that sounded. The creek did not hoard. It showed a stone and then another. She loosened her fingers. "We can divide the jewels. You take a turn picking from my bag, and I will pick from the creek again. We will each make a pattern." The child's face opened. "I am Sol," Sol said. "My tin was for berries, but the berries are gone, and stones are better guests anyway."

They sat knee to knee on the dry part of the bank. Bea set out her stones in a line. Sol chose second, then Bea, then Sol, the way they had seen people share orange slices. The moon stone went to Sol because Sol's eyes stayed on it the longest. Bea felt a pinch and then a lightness. She found a new stone in the shallows, gray with a gold freckle, and it fit her pattern as if it had been waiting to be the replacement. Uncle looked up from the strap and nodded once, the kind of nod that does not interrupt.

Bea's pattern became a spiral. Sol's became a path leading to the moon stone at the center. They poured cup-water over both so the colors would stay awake. The creek added its spoon-click song. A dragonfly checked their work and left, satisfied. "Your crown is a spiral now," Sol said. "Spirals can be crowns that lie down." Bea laughed. "Lying-down crowns are easier on heads." They did not stack the stones high. They let them rest. Sharing had changed the plan, and the new plan was wide enough for two tins.

When a stone rolled back toward the water, they let it go if it seemed eager. "The creek keeps a share too," Bea said. That idea made the game feel fair all the way down. Sol offered a berry-tin story about a picnic, and Bea offered the gold-freckle stone's finding place, a pocket behind a mossy rock. They traded stories the way they had traded pebbles. Uncle finished the strap and dipped his feet, sighing at the cool. The bend smelled like wet leaves and clean tin.

Before leaving, Bea helped Sol wrap the moon stone in a leaf so it would not chip in the tin. Sol helped Bea tie her bag so the spiral could be rebuilt on the porch. "Come back if the creek invents new jewels," Sol said. "I will save you a turn," Bea answered, and she meant a real turn, not a leftover. They walked as far as the fork together. The bags bumped softly, a shared rhythm. Bea's hands were empty of the moon and full of the gold freckle, which was a good trade for a heart.

At home she laid the spiral on the step and showed Uncle how the freckle stone sat where the moon had been. "It belongs," she said. "So did the sharing." She told Mama about Sol's tin and the lying-down crown. The pinch was gone. In its place was a picture of two patterns shining under creek water. The problem had been a heavy bag and a lonely tin. Opening the bag had made the bend kinder and the colors brighter.

Bea and Sol raced leaves, not stones, so the jewels could rest. They chose two leaves the same size and set them in the slow water above the bend. Bea's leaf spun. Sol's leaf went straight, proud as a little boat. They did not call a winner. The creek could keep the score. When the leaves caught on a twig, the children freed them with a grass stem and let them go on. Uncle's humming wrapped around the game. Bea noticed that sharing the stones had made sharing the rest feel easy, like a path already worn smooth.

They rinsed their hands in the cup of water, not in the deeper creek, and dried them on the sunny side of the log. Bea's bag was lighter and her spiral plan was richer. Sol tapped the moon stone through the leaf wrap and smiled at the sound, a tiny tok. "It will remember the bend," Sol said. Bea nodded. She would remember the empty tin that had not stayed empty. The shade smelled like wet bark and mint. A bright thread of sun lay on the water, and both children looked at it as if it were a third pattern, one nobody had to carry home.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the spiral of pebbles. Bea touched the gold freckle and thought of the moon stone wrapped in a leaf, safe in another tin. The creek, she knew, was still slowing at the bend, showing jewels to whoever would share the looking. She listened to the house and let the stones rest. Sleep came like clear water over smooth rock.
