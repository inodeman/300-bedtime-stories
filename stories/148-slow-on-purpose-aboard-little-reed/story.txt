Keiko stepped onto the canal boat while it was still hugging the dock, one hand in Grandpa's and the other on the rail. The boat was named Little Reed, painted green as a cucumber, and it was going out for a very short loop with Ms. Fenn at the tiller. They would leave the dock, glide past three willow clumps, and come back to the same ropes. Keiko wore an orange vest because that was the boat's ordinary rule, like a bicycle helmet on the lane. "Do we go fast?" she asked. Ms. Fenn smiled. "We go as fast as a duck who is not in a hurry. Fast would wrinkle the water, and then you would miss it."

The rope was loosened by Ms. Fenn, not by Keiko. The boat slid into the canal with a sound like a hand smoothing a sheet. Keiko sat on the middle bench, feet flat, sketchbook closed on her knees. She wanted the engine to growl and the banks to blur. Instead the banks stayed readable. A bicycle on the towpath was quicker than they were, and the rider waved without racing them. "We are being passed by a basket," Keiko said. Grandpa chuckled. "Let the basket win. We are here to notice."

Keiko tried. At first noticing felt slow, like waiting for a kettle. Then a dragonfly stopped in the air beside her elbow, its wings a glitter that did not need speed. "Oh," she said, and did not grab. The dragonfly left when it was ready. In the reeds, seed heads tapped one another with a dry whisper. A fish shadow, not a fish she needed to catch, crossed a patch of sunken light. Keiko opened her sketchbook. "If I only look when things zoom, I miss the zoomless things," she said. Grandpa passed her a short pencil. "Draw the whisper if you can."

She drew reeds as straight letters and the dragonfly as a pause. Ms. Fenn pointed with her chin, both hands still on the tiller. "Water lilies ahead, the white ones. They do not perform. They just float." The lilies came alongside like quiet plates. One held a bead of water in its middle. Keiko counted petals without picking. "Seven that I can see, and more tucked," she said. A moorhen, round and busy, picked along the muddy edge and did not mind the boat because the boat did not rush it. "Nature is all on a different clock," Keiko said. "I brought my hurry clock by mistake."

"Leave it on the dock next time," Ms. Fenn said. "The canal keeps its own." They passed under a low footbridge they could have walked across in one minute. From the water the bridge was a wooden picture frame. On the path above, a child looked down and waved a paper pinwheel. Keiko waved back with her pencil hand. She added the bridge to the page, and a tiny pinwheel dot. "I am collecting a ride," she said. "Not flowers. Not stones from the bank. Just lines." Grandpa looked at the page. "That is a gentle way to take something home."

The loop bent. Little Reed made a wide, careful turn in a place Ms. Fenn called the kettle, because it was round and calm. No bump. No splash on their knees. Keiko saw their dock again, small at first, with the same red fender and the same coil of rope. She was almost sorry. Then she noticed the willow leaves showing their pale undersides, which she had not drawn on the way out. "The way back is a second look," she said, and sketched a pale leaf. A duck convoy, four birds and a lot of dignity, let the boat go first. Keiko whispered thank you, which made Grandpa's eyes crinkle.

At the dock, Ms. Fenn settled the ropes. Keiko stayed seated until she was told the step was ready. She climbed to the boards that did not move. The orange vest came off and was hung on its peg for the next rider. "Was that too slow?" Ms. Fenn asked. Keiko shook her head. "It was the right slow. I saw a bead of water living on a flower. I would have missed it if we had hurried past the lilies." She showed the page. Ms. Fenn nodded as if the drawing were a proper log of the trip.

They walked home along the lane, sketchbook closed against a surprise breeze. Keiko listened to the canal even after she could not see it. "The reeds are still talking," she said. Grandpa said, "They will be there after supper, and tomorrow, if we keep the water kind." Keiko thought about not throwing anything, not even a crumb parade, because Ms. Fenn had said crumbs were better in a tin than in a duck's surprise dinner. She patted her pocket where the pencil rode, a small tool for loving a place.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the open sketchbook. Keiko added the date and the words "slow on purpose." Grandpa set a cup of mint water beside the page, not on it. "The canal lent you pictures," he said. "You gave them back as lines." Keiko watched the glow sit on the white lily she had drawn and felt the boat's gentle rock still hiding in her shoulders, a quiet memory of nature going at its own wise pace.
