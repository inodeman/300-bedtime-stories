Pema’s fingers itched to turn pages. The photo album on Grandpa Kamal’s lap looked full of secrets, and secrets felt faster when you flipped. “I can find the best picture if I go quickly,” she said. Her knees bounced. The feeling was impatience, bright and bossy. Grandpa Kamal kept one gentle hand on the page they had already opened. It showed a rainy Sunday and a blanket on the kitchen floor. “This one has a long story,” he said. “If we run past it, the story stays inside the paper. I would like to tell it. Can your patience sit with us?”

Pema made a face that was half groan and half smile. “Patience is wiggly today,” she said. “I will try. I might need a job for my hands.” Grandpa Kamal offered her the ribbon bookmark. “Hold this. When the story is done, you move it. That is the only flip.” Pema held the ribbon as if it were a kite string. Her other hand sat on her knee. She looked at the photo again, slower. She saw cups, a blanket, wooden spoons standing like little tent poles, and rain on the window. “Why are the spoons in the blanket?” she asked. The question gave her impatience somewhere useful to go.

“That was an indoor picnic,” Grandpa Kamal said. “We had planned the balcony, but the sky wanted a bath. Your grandma said we could move the picnic instead of canceling the joy. We spread the blanket. We used spoons to hold the corners down so nobody tripped. We ate sandwiches and listened to the rain as if it were a guest drummer.” Pema could almost hear it. Her bounce slowed. She had thought the best picture would be the brightest one. This one was gray and gold and funny. “If I had flipped, I would have missed the spoon tent,” she said. Grandpa Kamal nodded. “Speed is not the same as seeing.”

They stayed. Pema asked who sat where. She asked whether the sandwiches were cut in triangles. They were. She asked if anyone felt grumpy about the rain. “A little, at first,” Grandpa Kamal said. “Then the blanket made a new plan, and the grumpy feeling had to share the room with giggles.” Pema understood. Her own impatience was sharing the room with interest now. She traced the edge of the photo without peeling it. The ribbon stayed still in her other hand. She felt proud of that stillness. It was a small muscle she had not known she could use.

When the story ended, Grandpa Kamal lifted his hand. “Your turn to move the ribbon,” he said. Pema did, one page only. The next photo was a balcony tomato, round and red. She wanted to race to the page after that, because a corner of color peeked out. She named it. “Impatience is back,” she said. “It sees the peek.” Grandpa Kamal waited. Pema put the ribbon down on the tomato page like a promise. “Tell me one slow thing about this tomato,” she said. He told her it took all summer, and that a child had checked it every morning as if hurry could paint it red. Pema laughed. “That child was impatient like me.” “That child was me,” Grandpa Kamal said, eyes twinkling. The album suddenly felt like a mirror with kind glass.

They made a game called One and Then. One picture. Then the story. Then the ribbon. Pema kept score of stories, not of pages. By the fourth picture she noticed she could remember details. The indoor picnic. The slow tomato. A wooden spoon band in a kitchen that looked like theirs. A letter on a table addressed in careful letters to another town. If she had flipped like wind, those details would have blurred into a smear of faces. Instead they stacked in her mind like plates, each one whole. Patience, she decided, was a way of collecting, not a way of doing nothing.

She asked to stop while she still wanted more, which felt like a new kind of strength. “I want to keep some for next time,” she said. “If I see them all now, I might not hear them.” Grandpa Kamal closed the album with care and let her hug it. The hug was not a flip. It was a thank-you. Pema’s knees were quiet. Impatience had not been scolded away. It had been given a ribbon and a turn. She liked herself this way, wiggly and also able to stay. She told Grandpa Kamal the indoor picnic story back to him, almost whole, spoon tent and all. He clapped softly. “You kept it,” he said. “That is what patience is for.” Pema tied the ribbon in a neat bow around the album so the place would not get lost. The bow was a promise to come back without rushing.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the closed album and the ribbon that marked their place. Pema watched the light and practiced one slow breath, a tiny rehearsal for the next page. The best picture, she understood, was the one she stayed with long enough to hear.
