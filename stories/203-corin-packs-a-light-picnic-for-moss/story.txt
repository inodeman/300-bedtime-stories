Corin spread a blanket on the kitchen floor and pretended it was the meadow. "Picnic," Corin said. "We need everything." Cups, a heavy melon, three jars, a full water jug, and a loaf all tried to climb into the basket at once. The basket bulged. From the open doorway Moss the donkey watched with calm dark eyes and flicked one ear, which was his way of being interested.

Moss liked helping. He liked the lane. He liked standing in shade while friends laughed nearby. He did not like a basket that tugged. The grown-up set a hand on the bulging lid. "Moss can carry a light picnic," the grown-up said. "Light is the important word. If we pack as a team, the help will feel good from the first step to the last."

Corin called, "Team meeting!" Selma came in from the step, and they sat in a circle with Moss's basket in the middle like a quiet guest. Corin lifted the melon. "Too much," Corin said. Selma lifted the big jug. "We can take one small bottle and refill it at the pump." They chose apple slices in a little tin, carrot sticks, a folded cloth, two cups, and a thin wedge of cheese. The loaf stayed home for later toast. Each thing was named out loud before it went in. Naming made the choices feel fair.

They tested the weight the kind way. Corin held the basket. Selma held it. The grown-up held it. Then they held the empty basket and compared. "It should feel like a tote of folded towels, not like a stone," the grown-up said. Corin took out one extra cup. Selma took out a jar of pickles nobody had really wanted. Now the basket sat neatly. Moss sniffed it, blew a soft breath, and stood still while the grown-up settled the strap across the padded band. The strap lay flat. Nothing swung. Moss could turn his head freely.

They walked the lane together. Corin stayed on one side. Selma stayed on the other. Nobody tugged the lead. Moss chose a steady pace, and the children matched it. When a rabbit hopped across far ahead, they all paused so Moss could look. Bees worked the clover at a distance, and Corin only pointed, not chasing the sight closer. "Teamwork is also walking at the same speed," Selma said. Corin nodded. The meadow smelled like warm grass and a little mint.

Under the picnic oak they unpacked as a team too. Corin spread the cloth. Selma set the cups. The grown-up lifted the basket off and rubbed Moss's shoulder with a gentle palm. Moss had a loose rope clipped to a shady loop, long enough to shift his feet and reach his water, and he could see his friends the whole time. He ate his own small measure of hay that had been brought in a separate light net, not the children's cheese. Apple slices were for people today. Everyone agreed that out loud, so no one would be confused.

After lunch they packed crumbs back into the tin. Nothing was left to sour in the grass. Moss carried the same light basket home, even lighter now. Corin felt the good tired feeling of a plan that had room for everyone. The jars and the melon could wait for a table, where tables are the right carriers.

Halfway along the lane they stopped because Corin thought of one more thing: a heavy book of leaf pictures. Selma held out both hands. "Team check," she said. "Does the book help the picnic, or does it only help a maybe?" Corin laughed and admitted it was a maybe. The book went back to the porch chair. Moss flicked an ear as if he approved of second thoughts. They walked on, lighter in more than one way. Corin began a counting song of steps, Selma matched it, and the grown-up hummed the quiet parts so the lane stayed a shared rhythm.

At the oak, before anyone ate, they asked with a look and a loose strap whether the band still felt fair. The grown-up slid a finger beneath it. There was space. Moss sighed the long sigh of a donkey who is content. Corin whispered, "Thank you for carrying the light part." Teamwork, Corin understood, kept going after the packing was done. It was also the thank-you, the shade, the water set where Moss could reach it, and the choice to leave a beloved book at home.

Home again, they wiped the tin and hung the hay net to air. Corin retold the team check so Maude, who had arrived with a question about jars, could hear the rule in a fresh voice. "If it makes the basket sigh, it stays on the shelf," Corin said. Maude grinned and promised to remember. Moss stood easy in his shelter, strap off, ears resting. The picnic had been a circle of small choices, and every choice had kept the circle kind.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as Moss was brushed and thanked. Corin wrote a tiny list and pinned it by the door: small bottle, apple tin, cloth, two cups, hay net. "Next picnic, we already know how to be a team," Corin said. Moss blinked slowly in the shade, comfortable and unhurried, which was the finest review a donkey could give.
