Esther warmed her hands around the round barley pillow and then set it near the window lamp, close but not touching the shade. The pillow was Grandpa's favorite for the reading chair. He was still on the balcony, listening to crickets and naming stars under his breath. His slippers sat by the door, cool from the floor. Esther looked at them and made a kindness plan. She would have comfort ready before he came in, the way a lamp is ready before anyone asks the room to shine.

The pot of hot milk clicked softly as it cooled. Paper stars hung in a row on the glass. A cardboard telescope waited on the quilt, which Esther had already folded over the chair so the seat would not feel bare. She moved the slippers into the pool of lamplight and turned them so the openings faced the balcony door. "Toes first," she told them, as if they were polite animals. Then she fluffed the barley pillow, smoothing a dent from the afternoon, and placed it exactly where Grandpa's back liked to land.

She did not call him yet. Kindness, she thought, could finish its work without a trumpet. She poured milk into his tall cup and her short one, leaving his on a napkin so the heat would not bother the wood. She set a spoon in case he wanted to stir. She checked the glow-in-the-dark stickers on the windowsill and pressed down a corner that had peeled, because a peeled sticker might stick to his sleeve. Small fixes counted. The moon looked in, round and approving, though Esther knew the moon approved of everyone in its quiet way.

Grandpa's voice drifted through the door. "The spoon is bright tonight." Esther smiled. He meant the Big Dipper. She lifted the cardboard tube and stepped onto the balcony in her own slippers, staying near him. "I warmed your chair," she said. "You can finish the spoon first." Grandpa's face opened into a glad look. "You did that without being asked. That is a kindness with both shoes on." They looked through the tube together. He let her find the last star in the handle. She let him have the long look at the moon.

When they came in, Grandpa saw the slippers in the lamplight and laughed a soft laugh. "They look toasted." He slid his feet in and sighed the sigh of a person whose toes have come home. Then he sat, and the barley pillow met his back. "Esther, this is exactly the shape of rest." She felt a glow in her cheeks that was better than praise stickers. She handed him the tall cup. The milk was still nicely hot. He blew across it and thanked her twice, once for the warmth and once for the noticing.

Esther sat on the rug with her short cup and the quilt over her knees. She told him she had almost only warmed the pillow, and then she had seen the slippers and the peeling sticker and the spoon. "Kindness kept finding one more little thing," she said. Grandpa nodded. "It often travels in a line, like stars. You followed the line." They listened to the crickets. The garden was safe and busy with music. Esther taped a new paper star to the string because the row had a gap, and gaps were something she liked to mend.

Before bed she moved the barley pillow back a finger's width so it would not overheat near the lamp, and she set Grandpa's book beside it, closed, with a paper star as a bookmark. She did not do every job in the house. She did the jobs that would meet him kindly when he reached for them. Her own slippers she placed under her bed. The cardboard tube went into the basket. The cups she rinsed until they squeaked, a tiny cheerful sound.

Grandpa called from the chair, "The room feels looked after." Esther held those words the way she had held the warm pillow, carefully and with pleasure. She stood on the balcony for three breaths, smelling the night and saying goodnight to the crickets. Then she came in and found the lamp still doing its golden work. Being kind had not emptied her. It had filled the evening up.

Esther carried a small folded cloth to the balcony and offered it to Grandpa for his cool fingers before he came inside. "Hold this a moment," she said. "It has been near the lamp." He wrapped his hands and sighed in a happy way. They listened to one more cricket song together, not rushing the kindness indoors to be the only kindness. Esther pointed out a paper star that had twisted backward on its string. Grandpa held the cloth. She turned the star to face the moon again. Two small comforts, side by side, made the doorway feel like a welcome. When they stepped in, the barley pillow and the warm slippers were waiting, and the cloth joined them on the chair arm, a little trio of care.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the barley pillow and the waiting book. Grandpa's slippers rested in the last of the warm light. Esther slipped under the quilt, glad she had prepared comfort before it was requested, and the crickets kept their gentle watch outside.
