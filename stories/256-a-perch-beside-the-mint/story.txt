Laila heard a bright chirp and stopped with her brush in the air. A small brown bird sat on the fence, chest puffing, song tumbling out like beads poured on a plate. Marco froze too, green paint shining on his bristles. They were outside with Auntie, finishing a birdhouse whose walls Auntie had already joined. The children had been painting leaves along the eaves, not because a bird needs painted leaves, but because the garden had so many real ones that their hands wanted to answer. "Listen," Laila whispered. The chirp came again, then a softer churr, like a question.

They did not run at the bird. Nature, Laila had learned, sometimes steps closer if you become a quieter part of the yard. They set the brushes on a lid. The paint smell was sharp, so they moved a few steps away, toward the mint, which smelled cool and sweet when Marco pinched a leaf and passed it to Laila just to share the scent. The bird flicked its tail, considered them with a bead-bright eye, and flew to the bush. "It likes the middle height," Marco said. "Not the hot top of the fence for long. Not the ground." Laila nodded. "Then the house should sit at middle height, in shade, with a clear hop to the bush."

Auntie listened and smiled. "That is good noticing. I will hold the house. You two tell me when the doorway feels right." She lifted the little wooden house, already sturdy, screwdriver resting in her pocket unused for now because the walls were done. The children did not reach for tools. They stood back and used their eyes. "A hand lower," Laila said. "A breath toward the mint, so the doorway is not staring at the noisy gate," Marco added. Auntie shifted. A breeze moved the mint and the painted leaves on the eaves at the same time, real and pretend leaves agreeing. "There," both children said together, then laughed because the togetherness surprised them.

Auntie fixed the house to the post she had prepared, while Laila and Marco held the water dish and the seed dish like treasures. They placed the water in a lid a short hop away, filling it from a jar until it trembled. They sprinkled seeds, not a mountain of them, just a sprinkle, so nothing would spoil. Marco brushed a fallen petal out of the dish. Laila wiped a drip of green paint off the perch with a damp cloth, a small cleanup that mattered, because a sticky perch would not feel kind to little feet. "Perch beside the mint," she said. "That can be its address."

They sat on the step and let the yard be the yard. Ants drew a line in the dust, busy and harmless if you let them work. A butterfly, pale as paper, wobble-flew over the clover and did not land. The brown bird did not move in at once. It sang from the bush, nearer than before. Marco whispered the rhythm, not the meaning, because he did not pretend to speak bird. "Chirp, chirp, churr. Like our brushes when they tap the lid." Laila smiled. "Maybe it is telling the garden its name for this hour." They watched a cloud shade the house and then slide off. The blue of the roof, painted yesterday, deepened and then brightened again.

When the bird hopped to the fence and then, after a long look, to the roof, both children held their breath in a happy way, not a frightened way. Tiny claws ticked on the wood. The bird did not go inside. It bent, peered at the round door, and flew back to the bush with a seed from the dish shining in its beak for a moment before the beak closed. "It took a sample," Marco said, awed and quiet. "The house can be a maybe. The seed is a yes." Laila felt gladness spread, warm as sun on her knees. She did not shout. Shouting would have undone the careful quiet. She squeezed Marco's sleeve once.

They finished the painted leaves with smaller strokes, now that the bird had gone higher into the bush. Each leaf was a little different, because real leaves are. Auntie said, "You matched the place to the creature. That is nature work as much as paint work." Laila cleaned the brushes until the water ran clear. Marco tipped the dirty water into a scrub bucket, not into the bird's drink. They looked back once. The house stood at middle height, mint swaying, water shining, a perch clean and dry. No one said it was the greatest birdhouse ever built. Marco said, "I hope the shade stays kind." Laila said, "I hope we keep listening."

Indoors they drew the bird on a card, a brown oval, a bright eye, a note of the rhythm written as chirp chirp churr. The card went on the fridge so the noticing would not fade. Their fingers smelled like mint and a hint of paint. Outside the window the little roof showed among leaves, a made thing sitting politely inside a living place.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as the yard turned blue, and the birdhouse became a calm shadow beside the mint. Laila and Marco watched from their chairs, hands still, eyes kind. The house was finished. The perch was ready. Nature would choose the rest, and they had made room for that choice.
