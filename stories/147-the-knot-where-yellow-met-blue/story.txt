Jo arrived on the common with a diamond kite the color of a ripe pear and a string wound neatly on a wooden winder. The grass was dry. The wind was friendly. Mama spread a rug near the path, not under the kites. Ren was already there with a blue kite shaped like a simple fish, and Ren's father stood by the bicycle rack. "Same sky?" Jo called. "Same sky," Ren called back. They ran a few light steps, not toward each other, and both kites rose. The pear diamond wagged. The fish kite curved. For a while the only job was smiling.

Then the strings drew a quiet cross in the air and stayed together. It was not a tumble. It was a tangle, high enough to see and low enough to reach if they were patient. Jo felt a tug and tugged back. The knot hopped tighter. "Hey," Ren said, not angry, only surprised. "Your string is holding my string." Jo's cheeks warmed. "I did not aim. The wind introduced them." Mama lifted a hand. "Stop pulling. Teamwork starts when the pulling stops." Both children froze with their winders lifted, arms a little tired, kites nodding like they were listening.

Ren's father jogged over, calm as a walk. "We will make a square of helpers," he said. "Jo, you hold your winder still. Ren, you do the same. Grown-ups will steady the kites if they dip. Nobody yanks, and nobody lets go all at once." Jo liked plans that had jobs in them. "What is my second job?" "Walk toward Ren when I say, a little at a time, so the strings go slack," Mama said. "Slack string is honest string. It shows you the knot." Jo nodded. The pear kite dipped, and Mama caught the frame gently at the edge of the grass, keeping it off the path.

They walked closer. The knot slid down from the sky into reach, a small nest of yellow and blue. Jo wanted to pick at it fast. Ren wanted that too. Their fingers bumped. "One at a time," Ren said. "You loosen the yellow loop. I will hold the blue so it does not cinch." Jo breathed and worked the loop as if it were a shoelace on a sleepy foot. The knot gave a tiny sigh of string and loosened. "Your turn," Jo said. Ren eased the blue free. A twisted bit remained. They did that bit together, counting under their breath. "One, two, rest. One, two, rest."

When the strings parted, nobody cheered so loud that the kites startled. They grinned. "The knot did not win," Jo said. "It just needed four people and no hero tugs." Ren laughed. "I almost became a hero tug. I am glad I did not." They checked the lines for snags. Jo's string had a fuzzy patch. Ren offered a bit of smooth tape from a pocket kit. "For the fuzzy part, so it will not catch again," Ren said. Jo held the string straight while Ren pressed the tape. It was a small repair, and it felt like a handshake.

They launched again with a new rule they made up together. Jo would keep to the left half of the sky above the rug. Ren would keep to the right. If a kite wandered, they would both walk, not pull. The pear diamond climbed. The fish kite swam. Their strings were parallel as roads. Mama and Ren's father sat and shared a tin of cucumber slices. "Look at that teamwork," Mama said. "It looks like ordinary flying, which means it is working." Jo heard and stood a little taller without stepping into Ren's half.

A smaller child pointed and asked how the kites did not bump. Jo knelt so their eyes were level. "We made a plan, and we stopped when the plan needed fixing," Jo said. "You can fly too, over there, where the sky is still empty." The child and a grown-up took the far edge. Three kites then, no knots. Jo felt the common grow wider in a friendly way. Teamwork, it seemed, could include people who had not even held your string.

When the wind softened, they wound the lines in matching rhythms so the string would not bite itself. Jo's arms had the good tiredness of a game finished well. "Same sky tomorrow?" Ren asked. "If the weather is kind and our grown-ups say yes," Jo said. They bumped winders like cups. The bicycles ticked home along the lane, kites rolled in soft sleeves, and the rug left no litter behind because everyone had looked once more at the grass.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Jo hung the pear kite where it could rest flat and not crease. The taped place on the string showed if you looked closely. Jo did not mind. "That fuzzy bit is where we shared the fix," Jo told Mama. Mama nodded. "Teams leave kind marks sometimes." Jo wound the last loose end and set the winder on the sill. Outside, the common was only a dark green thought away, and the glow made the kite's fabric look ready for another careful sky.
