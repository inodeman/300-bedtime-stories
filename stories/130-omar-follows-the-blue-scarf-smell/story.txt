The night market was a river of color. Lanterns hung so low Omar could see the seeds of light inside the paper. Papa held a coin purse and a scrap of paper that said blue scarf, tea stall, painted kettle. "We will walk there together," Papa said, "but I want you to practice noticing, so the market becomes a map in your head." Omar liked maps. He also liked the idea of a scarf the color of deep evening. They passed drums, oranges, and a person folding fans. Every stall waved.

At the tea stall the kettle was painted with white leaves. The scarf lay folded beside cups, blue as a quiet lake. The seller, a man with steam on his glasses, said he would wrap it after he poured a line of cups. "Walk one small circle and come back," Papa told Omar. "I will stay by the kettle. Use your eyes and your nose. Wonder is a tool." Omar set off, sure he could not get lost in one circle. Then a puppet show clapped, a bell rang, and a dog of a very polite size trotted past with a tassel. Omar turned twice. The kettle was gone from his view. The lane looked new in every direction.

His stomach tightened in a practical way, not a storm. He stopped where he was, beside a basket of cinnamon sticks. Wonder, Papa had said. Omar closed his eyes for one breath and opened them ready to collect clues. He smelled cinnamon, sharp and sweet. He heard the puppet crowd laughing in a burst behind him, so that way was back, not forward. He saw a string of square lanterns, then a string of round ones. The tea stall had been under round ones. He remembered steam, and a clink of small cups, and the white leaves on the kettle.

He followed the round lanterns. A child sold paper pinwheels that turned when people passed. Omar paused, astonished by a pinwheel painted like a fish, then remembered the lake-blue scarf and walked on. He was allowed to notice and still move. The air changed from cinnamon to mint and then to something toasted. Tea. He was close. A woman hummed a three-note tune. He had heard that hum near the kettle, under the other sounds. He followed it the way a person follows a ribbon through grass.

The painted kettle appeared between shoulders, white leaves shining. Steam wrote a curl and erased it. Papa was still there, calm, wrapping nothing yet because the seller had just finished the cups. "You circled wider than a circle," Papa said, not sharp, only true. Omar grinned. "I used the round lanterns and the hum and the tea smell. I saw a fish pinwheel and did not stay too long." The seller nodded. "That is how regulars are born. They learn the market's sentences." He placed the blue scarf in paper the color of cream.

Omar held the parcel. It was light and smelled faintly of tea. He looked back down the lane and saw it differently now: not a muddle, but a row of clues waiting to be read. The square lanterns meant spice. The round ones meant the tea bend. The puppet laugh was a bright landmark. Wonder had not been a delay. It had been the way home to Papa's side. He touched the paper and felt the scarf's fold, a soft ladder under his fingers.

They drank a small cup of tea that tasted like honey and leaves. Omar blew across it and watched the surface wrinkle. "I want to remember the kettle leaves," he said. The seller let him look as long as he liked. Omar traced the shape in the air: a leaf, a vein, a curve. Papa paid and tucked the receipt into the purse. The market's river moved around their still island. Omar felt tall enough to be a map.

On the walk home he narrated the clues so he would not drop them. Round lanterns, toasted smell, three-note hum, white leaves, blue lake folded in cream paper. Papa added one more. "And a boy who stopped and looked instead of spinning." Omar liked being that boy. The problem had been a lane that all seemed the same and a parcel he might have missed. Noticing had brought it back. At home he set the wrapped scarf on the chair where Mama would find it in the morning, a surprise they had planned together. He told her only that the market had spoken in smells. She raised an eyebrow and smiled. Omar washed tea from his lips and stood by the window, still hearing the pinwheel's soft tick. The day had been wide and then, suddenly, readable.

Omar stood still beside Papa and named the kettle's leaves out loud: one, two, three, a cluster like a small hand. The seller listened, pleased, and turned the kettle so a new leaf showed. Steam beaded on the painted veins and made them shine. Omar did not touch the hot metal. Wonder could stand a step back and still see. He heard the three-note hum again and found it was the seller, not a hidden radio. That discovery felt like a lantern turning on. The market was made of people as much as of paper and spice.

Walking on, he kept the cream parcel against his chest when the lane grew busy, and he watched feet and lantern strings with the same care. A drum tapped a pattern like rain on a tin roof. Omar matched two steps to the tap and then let the rhythm go so he would not miss the corner. Papa asked what the tea had tasted like in one word. Omar said, "Leaves." Then he added, "And honey, if honey is allowed to be a second word." Papa said two words were fair after a wide circle. The blue scarf waited inside its paper, a quiet lake folding itself toward home.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the cream paper. Omar pictured white leaves and a blue fold sleeping inside. Somewhere the kettle still steamed under round lanterns. He let the three-note hum finish its sentence in his head. Wonder had been the path, and the path had worked. He rested his forehead near the cool glass for a moment, then turned toward bed, full of maps.
