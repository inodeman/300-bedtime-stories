Gus held a cardboard tube up to the light and peered through it like a sailor with a very short telescope. Dust danced in the bright circle. The tube smelled like dry paper and the crayons that had once lived inside it. Neri stood on the porch below, a basket at her feet, and looked up without standing on anything wobbly. The window box was only a little above her reach if she stretched, but they were not going to stretch and drop things. They were going to build a slide for notes, a gentle slant along the wall.

"Papa said the tube can be taped if he presses the high part," Gus called, voice bouncing a little in the cardboard. "We can test with paper, not with anything heavy." Neri patted the basket. It was lined with a folded scarf so a landing would be soft. "I want to send you the thank-you for the blue chalk," she said. "My legs can walk it over, but a slide would feel like a tiny parade." A bee inspected the window-box flowers and left, uninterested in paper. The wall was warm under Gus's hand.

Papa brought wide tape and a calm face. "Fingers away from the sticky side while I anchor the top," he said. They nodded. He fixed the tube so it leaned like a relaxed arm, from the window box down to the basket, steep enough to slide and gentle enough not to spit a note onto the step. Gus decorated the outside with a wavy line that looked like a road. Neri drew a small envelope near the bottom. The marker smelled sharp and then sweet as it dried. They were inventing a post office the size of a smile.

The first note was a square of paper that said, "Thank you for the chalk. The sky I drew is because of you." Neri folded it once. Gus let it go at the top. It whispered, hurried, and stopped. The basket stayed empty. Neri looked up the tube and saw the note stuck at a bend where yesterday's bump had dented the cardboard. "It found a pocket," she said. Gus felt a pinch of disappointment and then a better feeling, the inventing feeling. "If it can get stuck, it can get unstuck," he said. "We look, then we change one thing."

Papa eased the tube free at the dent, just enough. "Tell me what you see," he said, and he kept his hands on the tape so it would not spring. Gus peered. "The wall of the tube caves in," he said. "The note thinks the cave is a chair." Neri laughed. "We can invite it to keep walking." They pressed the dent out from the inside with a wooden spoon handle that Papa held, slow and even, until the tube was round again. Then they added a wider paper mouth at the top, a little funnel taped smoothly, so the next note would enter without catching a corner. The funnel looked like a flower made of brown paper.

They tested with a blank scrap. It slid. It sighed. It landed in the scarf with a soft tick. Neri clapped without a big noise, hands meeting like two pages. Gus sent the real thank-you. This time the words traveled the whole way, a pale flash, and arrived face up, as if the note wanted to be read. Neri lifted it, smelled the faint marker, and read it aloud even though she had written it. Gus heard his own kindness coming back up to him, which is a strange and lovely loop. The chalk sky, still upstairs on his table, seemed to approve.

They sent three more: a drawing of the bee, a joke about a tube that wanted to be a flute, and a blank one they pulled back up on a string so they could practice returns. The string was Papa's idea, tied loosely, so a note could be guided if it ever hesitated. Nothing pinched. The basket caught every landing. Neri's porch smelled like the flowers overhead and warm cardboard. Gus rested his chin on the windowsill and felt the satisfaction of a thing that had failed once and then learned, which is what creative things do when someone is kind to them.

Before supper they sent one last note that said, "The slide works. Your turn tomorrow." Neri wrote her answer on the back and they pulled it up carefully, the string humming. The answer said, "I will send a cloud to go with your sky." Gus pinned both papers to the cork square in his room. The tube stayed on the wall, a quiet invention, ready and round, its dent only a memory in their hands.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the cork square, lighting the thank-you and the cloud note. Gus looked down at the porch basket, a dark little boat of scarf, and Neri looked up at his lit window. The cardboard tube held a stripe of gold along its side. Creativity had given their words a gentle road, and the road was ready for tomorrow's small parade of paper.
