Sami drew the picnic map at the kitchen table with a green pencil and a serious tongue at the corner of his mouth. The common was close. They only needed to find the oak that had a flat root like a bench, on the side near the letter box path. Papa packed sandwiches. Sami added arrows, a duck that was really a scribble, and a big circle for the oak. He colored the circle on the left of the page. Something in his memory itched. He thought the oak might be on the right when you entered from the lane. He pressed the pencil down anyway. Left looked balanced. Balanced was not the same as true.

They walked to the common with the map in a clip. The entrance arch of two hedges was exactly where Sami had drawn it. Good. The path split around a wide lawn. Papa paused. "Left or right, mapmaker?" Sami looked at his circle. His stomach made a small honest thump. If he said left, they would walk a few calm minutes and then have to turn. If he said the truth now, the map would be wrong in public. Nobody was far. The lane was still at their heels. "Papa," he said, "I think I put the oak on the easy side of the paper, not the real side. I noticed at home and I did not fix it. That was a wobble."

Papa crouched so the map was between them. "Thank you for telling me before our feet did extra work. A wobble named is a wobble shrinking." He did not sigh a sharp sigh. He turned the paper around. "What if the oak wants to be here?" Sami studied the hedges. A bright scarf someone had tied on a far rail, and the letter box path's gravel, matched the right-hand side. "Yes. I knew that gravel sound. I drew the quiet side because the pencil was already there." He felt lighter and a little embarrassed, which was a bearable mix. "I should have said it at the table."

"You said it when it still mattered," Papa answered. "Honesty is not only for after a mix-up grows legs." They took the right-hand path. Bees worked the clover. A kite lay rolled beside a rug where a family waved. In a short, unhurried time the oak appeared, flat root and all, with shade that looked poured. Sami checked the map and laughed. "The circle is on the wrong side, but our feet are on the right one." Papa set the blanket on the root's friendly edge, not on any flowers. "Then the circle can be corrected by a person, which is what circles hope for."

They ate sandwiches with the map weighted by a cup. Sami erased the old circle, blowing the dust off the blanket, and drew a new one on the true side. He added a tiny note: "I almost left this wrong." Papa read it and nodded. "That note is braver than a perfect map." A younger child from the kite rug wandered close with a parent and asked if the oak was the picnic oak. Sami showed the fixed drawing. "It is, if you come from the hedge and take the gravel side. I had it backward at first. I said so." The parent thanked him for the clear directions. Sami felt the embarrassment change jobs and become usefulness.

After lunch he walked the left path only a little, with Papa, just to see what his first drawing would have done. It led to a pretty patch of grass and the wrong tree, a beech with no flat root. They were never out of sight of the oak blanket. "See?" Papa said. "Pretty, and not the plan. If we had been stubborn, we might have picnicked in a puzzled way." Sami grinned. "Puzzled picnics are not bad, but promised oaks are better." They returned and shook crumbs into the tin. Honesty, Sami thought, was a kind of turning the paper around before the day got wrinkly.

On the way home he told the story to the rhythm of his steps. "I knew. I hid it in a tiny way. I told. We turned. We found the root." Papa added, "And you fixed the map so the next walker gets the true side." Sami held the clip so the wind would not edit his work. "Next time I will say the itch while the pencil can still change its mind." Papa said that sounded like a good friendship with facts. The lane greeted them with the same cracks as always. Home was exactly where the map had started, which made Sami proud of a small loop done honestly.

He set the corrected map on the table and looked once more for sneaky easy sides. The duck scribble was silly and labeled silly, so it did not pretend to be a duck pond. The oak was right. The arrows matched the walk their feet had just finished. "True is calmer than tidy," he said. Papa put the sandwiches' tin in the sink. "Tidy can come along after true." Sami smiled at that order. It felt like a rule he could keep in a pocket.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the green pencil lines. The new circle seemed to sit more comfortably than the old one ever had. Sami did not hide the pale dent of the first mark. He let it stay. "That dent is the wobble," he told Papa. "The dark circle is the truth." Papa touched the sill. "Both can live on one map if the truth is the one you follow." The glow stayed, quiet as a promise to speak up sooner next time.
