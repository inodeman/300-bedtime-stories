Omar cupped a sprouting acorn in his palm and felt the tiny root tickle his skin, a pale finger looking for a home. He had found a small cluster of them beside the hollow tree library, where deep leaf-litter had kept them damp. Some sprouts were crowded, elbows of roots tangling, none of them unkind, just too close to grow into proper oaks. Omar lined up clean acorn caps on a flat root. "You can each have a cap of moss," he told them. "A little cup, a little drink, and then a place with enough light and enough friends, but not a pile-up."

A gentle fox came to inspect the row, nose damp, manners good. Omar showed how he pressed a bed of wet moss into each cap, soft as a pillow, then set one sprout so the root pointed down and the green tip pointed toward the sky. "Nature already knows the direction," he said. "I am only giving the direction some room." The fox fetched an extra cap from the moss and set it at the end of the line, ready for a sprout Omar had almost missed. "Good eyes," Omar said. "A team of two is a fine planting crew, even if one of us has a tail."

They carried the caps in a shallow oak-bark tray, walking slowly past the ferns so no root would jostle loose. At the sunny edge near the picnic clearing, the soil looked thirsty and bright. Omar shook his head. "Too much noon for babies this small." In the deep hollow behind the biggest oak, it was cool and dim. "Too shy of sun," he decided. The fox led him to a gentle middle place, where morning light could reach and afternoon shade arrived like a lid. Omar knelt. The earth smelled rich and a little sweet. "Yes," he said. "This feels like a neighborhood, not a hiding spot and not a bare stage."

He planted the first sprout with its moss cap snug at the surface, so he could still see the tiny green and remember to water it. He spaced the next one two hands away, then the next, imagining future trunks that would not bump noses. The fox paced the gaps and sat in one of them, showing just how much room a body needed. Omar laughed. "If a fox can sit between them now, the oaks can stretch later." He pressed the soil with flat fingers, never a punch, and gave each moss cap a sip from his water leaf. Drops shone like small lanterns even though dusk was still far off.

While they worked, Omar talked about the paper map in his pocket and how he would mark this nursery with tiny tree dots, not so anyone could take the sprouts, but so he could visit with more sips. "Caring is a return trip," he said. The fox blinked yes. They left the crowded patch by the library cleaner than they found it, brushing litter back so the ground would not look scarred. Omar picked up no more acorns than he had cups for. Leaving some to try their luck where they had fallen felt respectful. The wood was allowed to plant some of its own ideas.

They rested on the rope footbridge, legs and paws dangling above ferns, the empty tray between them. Omar's hands were dark with good soil. He did not rush to wash. "This dirt means we showed up," he said. He told the fox a hope, not a hurry, that one day these sprouts would make shade for picnics and leaf-pages and fireflies. The fox leaned on the guide rope and yawned a comfortable yawn. Hope, Omar thought, did not need to poke the sprouts to grow faster. It needed water, space, and a person who remembered the way back.

Before home, they stopped at the water bowl by his mushroom cottage step, and Omar rinsed only the tray, leaving a little soil in the lines as a memory. He drew the tiny tree dots on his paper map while the ink-smell mixed with moss. The fox watched the dots appear and then touched its nose to the middle one, choosing a favorite sprout to ask about next time. "That one did have the curliest root," Omar agreed. "We will check its curl tomorrow and not tug it." He set three spare acorn caps on the sill in case another sprout called for a cup.

The day had been full of small placements, each one a decision about light and room. Omar's back was pleasantly tired. He ate an acorn biscuit on the step and shared the corner without being asked twice. Fireflies woke in the ferns, pricking the dusk with quiet gold. He looked toward the middle place he could not quite see from here and felt calm, the way a map feels calm when the path is drawn true. The sprouts did not need him to hover. They needed him to return. That was a promise he could keep.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the spare acorn caps, empty and waiting, neat as cups in a cupboard. Omar washed his hands at last and watched the soil swirl away, its work already done in the ground. Somewhere a curly root was drinking from a moss pillow. He sat until the lantern made a round moon on the table. Nature, he thought, was not a thing to rearrange for pride. It was a neighborhood of sprouts, shade, and sips, and a fox who knew the exact middle place where morning could find them.
