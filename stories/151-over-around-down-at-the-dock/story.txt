Nabil walked to the canal dock with a small jug of water wrapped in a towel so it would not sweat through his shirt. The boat Little Reed was staying in today, tied fast, with no loop planned. Ms. Fenn was coiling a soft rope that kept slipping its loops, and the coil looked more like a nest than a neat cake. "Morning," Nabil said. "Grandpa said you might like an extra pair of hands that listen. He is on the bench with the newspaper, close enough to see my elbows." Ms. Fenn wiped her brow and smiled. "I like hands that listen even more than hands that hurry. Want to learn the rope's circle?"

Nabil set the jug in the shade first, because kindness could melt if you forgot the practical part. Then he watched. Ms. Fenn made a loop, turned it, and laid it flat. "Over, around, down," she said. "The rope is not a snake. It is a long quiet helper. We give it a bed." Nabil tried. His first loop stood up like a surprised ear. He laughed instead of frowning. "The ear is not the shape." "Not today," Ms. Fenn said. "Try slower. Let the rope rest on the dock between moves." Nabil did. The next loop agreed to lie down. A warmth spread in his chest that was better than being quick.

They built the coil together until it was a tidy cinnamon roll of rope. Nabil's fingers smelled like damp hemp and sunshine. "Is there another job that is small?" he asked. Ms. Fenn pointed at the chalkboard by the ramp. A sleeve had smeared the words. The message was meant to say, "Rides with a grown-up. Ask first. Life vests on pegs." Now it looked like a puzzle. "People can still tell," she said, "but clear writing is a welcome." Nabil liked clear welcomes. He took the chalk, which was short and dusty, and looked at Grandpa. Grandpa nodded from the bench. "Print, do not squeeze," Grandpa called.

Nabil printed each word and then read them aloud to be sure he had not invented a new rule. He drew a tiny vest shape beside the pegs note, because a picture can help a reader who is still learning the words. "Does the vest drawing look bossy?" he asked. Ms. Fenn shook her head. "It looks helpful. Bossy would be a shout. This is a hint." She poured water from Nabil's jug into two cups. "And this is the kindness I did not know I was thirsty for. You brought it before I asked."

They sat on the edge of a crate, not on the boat, and drank. A toddler's sun hat skated along the dock on a breeze and stopped against Nabil's shoe. The toddler was with a father two steps away, already reaching. Nabil did not make a game of keep-away. He picked up the hat, dusted the brim, and held it out. "It found my shoe," he said. "Thank you for not letting it meet the water," the father said. The toddler clapped once, a small happy knock. Nabil felt the dock become a place of several kindnesses, none of them loud, all of them close to home.

Ms. Fenn showed him how to hang the coiled rope on its peg so the next person would not have to undo a tangle. "Helpers leave a place easier than they found it," she said. Nabil looked around for one more easy thing. He saw a paper scrap under the bench, not a treasure, just a flutter that might blow into the canal. He put it in the bin. Grandpa folded the newspaper. "I saw the chalk, the hat, and the bin," he said. "Your hands had a busy kindness." Nabil shrugged, pleased. "They wanted jobs. The jug started it."

Before they left, Nabil checked the chalkboard from a few steps back, the way a visitor would. The letters were straight enough. The vest drawing was a little lopsided and still friendly. "If rain comes, the words will fade," he said. Ms. Fenn nodded. "Then someone kind will write them again. That is how a dock stays welcoming. Not by one perfect morning, but by people who notice." Nabil liked belonging to that long line of noticing. He rinsed the cups with a splash from the jug and set them upside down to dry.

The walk home was shaded by the same trees they always passed. Nabil swung the empty jug carefully. "I could bring mint leaves tomorrow if the pots at home can spare them," he said. Grandpa said, "Ask the pots, and ask your mother, and then yes." Nabil grinned. Kindness, he was learning, was not a single grand gesture. It was a coil, a cup, a hat, a clear sentence, and the good sense to stay where a grown-up could see you.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Nabil washed the chalk dust from his fingers. He could still picture the rope lying down at last. "Over, around, down," he told the sink, and the words sounded like a bedtime for a busy cord. Grandpa set the empty jug by the door for the next small errand. Outside, the canal kept its quiet slap against the dock, and the glow in the glass looked a little like a lamp left on so travelers would know a kind house was near.
