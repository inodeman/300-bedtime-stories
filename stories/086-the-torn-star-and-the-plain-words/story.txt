Imani stopped in the middle of the rug with a torn paper star in her palm. One point was ripped halfway, a pale tongue of paper hanging loose. She had been hopping a silly slipper dance while the window lamp hummed its quiet light, and her elbow had bumped the hanging string. The star had fallen and caught on the chair. Hot milk sat forgotten on the table. Crickets sang outside as if nothing had torn. Imani's cheeks felt warm. She looked at the quilt, which had a deep fold, and for a moment she wanted to tuck the star underneath and say nothing.

Mama was on the balcony, watering the small pot of mint and humming. The cardboard telescope leaned by the door. Glow-in-the-dark stickers dotted the windowsill like patient seeds of light. Imani turned the torn star over. If she hid it, the string would simply look shorter. Mama might think the star had blown into the garden. The idea made Imani's stomach feel twisty, the way a sock feels when it is on wrong. She sat down. She put the star on her knee where she could see it. Hiding was a story that would not fit the room.

"Mama," she called, and her voice shook only a little. "I need to tell you a true thing." Mama came in, drying her fingers, and sat on the quilt without a frown waiting on her face. Imani held out the star. "I was dancing, and I bumped the string. This one ripped. I almost put it under the quilt. I did not. I want to say it plainly." The words felt big in her mouth and smaller, better, once they were out in the lamplight.

Mama nodded slowly. "Thank you for telling me the truth before it got covered up. A torn star is easy to mend. A hidden story is harder." She did not scold. She fetched yellow paper, a dab of paste, and safe scissors. Together they cut a new point. Imani held the pieces still while Mama pressed them. Then Imani took a turn pressing too. The mend showed, a little paler than the rest, and Imani decided the pale place could be a mark of honesty rather than a mistake to be ashamed of.

They hung the star back on the string. It swung once and then settled among the others. Imani breathed out. The twisty feeling left, like a wrinkle smoothed by a warm hand. Mama poured the hot milk into two cups and they drank while looking at the moon. "The moon does not need us to pretend," Mama said. "It just shines. People do better when their words shine plainly too." Imani thought about that. Plain words had brought paste and help instead of a lumpy secret under the quilt.

She still wanted to dance, but she moved the string first, clipping it higher with Mama's hands helping. Then she did three soft hops in her slippers, smaller than before, and stopped before any elbow could wander. They laughed. Through the cardboard tube Imani found the moon and, beside it, the row of three stars Mama called coat buttons. Nothing in the sky was cross with her. The crickets kept time for a calmer dance.

Imani stuck a glow-in-the-dark sticker on the back of the mended point, a tiny dot only the family would know about. "So the true story has a little light on it," she said. Mama smiled. "Truth can glow without being loud." They rinsed the cups. Imani folded the scrap of torn paper into her notebook instead of throwing the evening away. It was a reminder that fixing begins with saying what happened.

On the balcony the mint smelled clean, and the garden was as safe as it had been before the bump. Imani was glad she had called Mama in. The room felt straight again, not because nothing had gone wrong, but because the wrong thing had been told and tended. She lined her slippers under the bed and let her shoulders drop.

After the cups were warm in their hands, Imani asked if they could test the mend with a gentle tug. Mama showed her how to tug the string, not the torn place. The star stayed. Imani's eyes shone. "The truth did not make it worse," she said. "It made a path to the paste." They looked through the cardboard tube together, Imani going first, and she found the moon waiting without any scolding in its light. She told Mama about the hop that had caused the bump, even the silly part where she had sung to her slippers. Mama laughed and sang one line back. Honesty had space for the funny details too. Imani pressed the glow sticker a little flatter so it would not peel, and she promised the window string a calmer dance next time, with elbows tucked like wings at rest.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the mended paper star and its pale new point. The string hung even. Imani climbed under the quilt with a clear heart, and the crickets stitched the night together outside. Honesty had been the paste that held the evening in place.
