Tessa carried a tray of orange peels into the Saturday sun beside the market stalls. The peels were fresh, bright, and full of oil that flashed when the light hit them. Amira spread a clean cloth on the low bench near the neighborhood bakery window. They were not throwing anything away. The peels were waiting for a second life in honey jars, tea cups, and bread. Nature had packed the fruit with scent, and the morning was ready to show how.

"Watch," Tessa said. She set one peel in the shade and one in the sun. They waited, because peels do not hurry, and neither did the girls. After a little while the sunny peel smelled louder. Its color looked deeper. The shady peel stayed cooler, greener in its perfume, like a leaf still thinking.

Amira bent close. "The sun is cooking the smell without a stove," she said. "The air is part of the recipe."

They noticed more. A curl of cinnamon bark on the grocer's board looked like a tiny hillside. When Amira snapped a small dry piece, the inside smelled like warm trees after rain, even though the street was dry and cheerful. She did not snap much. The bark was for jars and buns, and there was a whole bowl of it, plenty for the stall and the bakery. She held it to her ear as a joke and laughed when it did not whisper. "It talks to my nose instead," she said.

Tessa laid peels in a single layer so each one could feel the breeze. The white side faced down. The bright side faced the sky. A market awning flapped. Somewhere a soup pot lid tapped, and the bread ovens inside answered with their steady hum. No cloud crossed the sun. The only traveler was the cinnamon wind itself, moving from stall to stall.

"Let us make a nature card," Amira said. "Not only a baking card. A card about paying attention."

They wrote on a recipe card in clear letters. Step one, set peels in the sun and wait. Step two, smell the shade peel and the sun peel and notice they are relatives, not copies. Step three, add cinnamon bark and listen with your nose. Step four, share the dried curls in honey jars so the table can borrow the morning. They drew a small sun and a small curl. They did not draw a rush.

While the peels dried, they helped at the shared table. Picnic baskets slid underneath. A loaf cooled. Tessa turned the peels once, the way you turn a thought so you can see the other side. The edges had tightened. The scent had gathered. Patience had done what heat in a hurry might have scorched.

They filled three honey jars with a few curls each, not crowding them. The honey took on a sunny halo. One jar stayed at the grocer's stall. One went to the bakery shelf. One sat on the shared table beside cups and bread. Amira twisted a peel over a warm slice. A mist of oil freckled the crumb. Tessa dusted a whisper of cinnamon. They tasted. The bread tasted like standing outside with your face toward the light.

"Nature did the first baking," Tessa said. "Trees and fruit and sun. We did the second."

Amira nodded. "And we did not have to take it all. The bowl of bark is still full. The orange bowl is still full. The day is still making smell."

They propped the nature card where bakers and shoppers could read it. A child-sized breeze lifted the corner, and Tessa set a honey jar on it, gentle as a paperweight. The market kept its cheerful noise. The ovens kept their golden work. The peels on the cloth finished drying in their own time, ready for the next jar, the next loaf, the next curious nose.

When they sat, their fingers smelled like orchards and spice drawers. They did not scrub the scent off right away. It was a souvenir from paying attention. The shared table held bread, honey, and a little dish of peels that looked like orange smiles.

They took the nature card outside again and read it beside the drying cloth. A peel at the edge had curled tighter than the rest, a small orange boat. Tessa turned it over and showed Amira the pale side, soft as cloth, and the bright side, dimpled and glossy. They sniffed both. The pale side smelled gentle. The bright side smelled like a bell. Cinnamon bark, warmed in Amira's palm, added the tree note. They did not rush back in. The market awning flapped. A picnic basket rolled past on a neighbor's arm, full of bread. The girls waved. Then they chose three curls for the shared table and left the rest in the sun, because nature was still finishing its slow bake. The waiting cloth looked like a tiny field. Attention made it feel wide.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the sun-dried curls. Tessa and Amira rested their cheeks on their hands and breathed in. The Saturday light, the fruit, and the cinnamon bark had been the teachers. All they had done was notice, wait, and share the brightness nature had already made.
