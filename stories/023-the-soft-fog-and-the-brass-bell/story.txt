Saffron stood on the lighthouse path with the brass bell rope in sight and a small flutter in her stomach. A bright fog had come in, not dark and not frightening, only white and soft like a scarf pulled gently across the cove. The little wooden boats were still there, but their edges looked penciled instead of painted. Auntie rested a hand on the rail. "The bell tells the boats where the friendly lighthouse is," she said. "Your turn, if you want it." The air smelled like clean wet shells.

Saffron wanted the job and did not want it. The bell was large, golden, and quiet. She had heard it before from the dunes, a round note that made gulls tilt their heads and then carry on with their walks. Up close, the rope looked thick and serious. "What if it is too loud for the tide pools?" she asked. "What if I do it wrong and the sound comes out tangled?" Auntie smiled with her whole face. "A bell only knows one honest word. We let it speak, then we let it rest."

She climbed the two wooden steps to the bell post. Fog beads sat on the rail like tiny cool seeds. Her palms felt dusty from the sandy path. She wiped them on her sweater so she could hold the rope kindly. Below, a gull paced the railing and gave a small encouraging croak, not harsh, more like a neighbor saying go on. Saffron wrapped her hands around the rope. It was cool and a little damp. The bell did not move. "I am brave in small pieces," she whispered, a saying Auntie had given her for feelings that were bigger than one step.

The first pull was too polite. The clapper tapped the brass with a tiny tick, like a spoon on a mug. Saffron laughed a shaky laugh. "That would not guide a teapot across a table," she said. Auntie did not take the rope away. "Try with your whole arms, and stop when the note is round. Courage can be firm without being rough." Saffron set her feet on the boards. She thought of the boats, of people who liked to find the shore even when the shore was wearing fog, and of the lighthouse trying to be a good neighbor in the white air.

She pulled. The rope slid through her hands in a smooth line. The bell swung, and a clear golden sound rolled out over the cove, wide and warm, not angry at all. It touched the fog and made the white feel friendly. Saffron's ribs buzzed with the note in the nicest way. She let the rope rise and pulled once more, the way Auntie counted, two and then rest. A gull answered with a bright call and wheeled so its wings wrote a curve above the water. From the cove, a wooden hull turned its bow a little more surely along the safe bright path.

Saffron's hands tingled. She had thought courage would feel like a drum inside a parade. It felt more like a warm drink after a windy walk. "I did it," she said, and her voice was steady now. Auntie nodded toward the water. "You did, and you stopped at the right time. Even a good sound needs quiet around it so people can hear where they are." They stood together while the last ring thinned into the fog. Saffron watched a rock pool go pale silver at the edge of the stones, the starfish there untroubled, the water only shivering from the far note.

She practiced one more pair of rings when Auntie said the fog was still sitting softly on the channel. Each time, the flutter was smaller, a shell she could set on the rail and leave. Between rings she listened. She heard drips from the rope, the hush of small waves, and the keeper moving inside the lighthouse with a kettle. The bell's shine held tiny beads of fog. Saffron touched the brass lightly when it stilled. It was cool again, ordinary and wonderful. "Thank you," she told it, which made Auntie laugh in the nicest way.

They walked down toward the dune and shared sips of warm barley drink from a flask. Saffron's arms were pleasantly tired, the good kind of tired that means you used them for real. A person in a blue cap waved from the clearest pocket of fog, a simple thank-you wave from a little wooden boat, and Saffron waved back with both hands. "The sound found them," she said. "It was only a bell, but it was my pull." Auntie bumped her shoulder gently. "That is how a lot of brave things look. Not giant. Just done, and done with care."

The lantern by the window glowed softly, a round peach of light inside the fog, as Saffron coiled the end of the rope the way Auntie had shown her. She looked out once more before going in. The cove was still white and quiet, but it was no longer a puzzle she needed to shrink from. The boats had a note to follow, the bell had a voice, and she had lent it her arms. Inside, she warmed her fingers and smiled at the memory of the brass gleam. Courage, she decided, could sound like one clear note and then a kind silence after.
