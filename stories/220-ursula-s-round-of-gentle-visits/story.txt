Ursula woke with a worry that looked like kindness. She wanted to visit every friend before lunch, and she thought a visit did not count unless her hands were full. She pictured a huge bouquet, a heavy basket, a new hat for everyone, a song, and a pebble. By the time she reached the porch, the picture was so crowded she could barely hold the idea of it. The grown-up listened and then asked one question. "What if the gift is a greeting that fits?"

Ursula was not sure a greeting could be enough. She decided to test it before she built a tower of presents. Button was on the greeting stone. She did not rush him. She stood still, smiled, and said, "Quiet hello." Button answered with soft eyes and one wag. That was the whole friendship exchange, and it felt complete. Ursula's hands were empty. Button did not look under them for a hidden treat. He looked content. One worry loosened.

Bramble waited by the open gate, hat on the low peg, not on her head. Ursula did not insist on hats. She said, "Good morning, with or without a brim," and offered her palm to sniff. Bramble blinked the slow blink and leaned her shoulder near Ursula's arm for a moment, then went back to clover. The visit was short because Bramble's friendship was short and sweet in the mornings. Ursula did not stretch it to prove her affection. She thanked the goat and moved on.

At the pond she borrowed Emeka's stillness. The heron stood on one leg. Ursula did not ask for a twirl. She counted three breaths and lifted her hand halfway. The heron turned his head a little, a neighbor's nod, and returned to watching light. "That was a visit," Ursula whispered, surprised. On the clover path she stopped far from the bees, as Flora and Selma had taught, and only said inside her mind, I see your work. She did not need the bees to look up. Friendship with a meadow hum can be a promise not to interrupt.

The chickens were on Rafi's pebble path. Ursula hummed one line of Hawa's song and then let them pass. They did not owe her a performance. Pippin's walk was last, and it was not a walk she took alone. The grown-up came, because a pony walk belonged to that rule. They went only to the first oak, the distance Ioanna trusted. Ursula told Pippin he did not need a bigger gift than company. His ear flicked, noticed, and relaxed. At the oak they turned back, promise kept.

Ursula sat on the porch and counted the visits on her fingers. Dog, goat, heron, bees, chickens, pony. No bouquet had been picked. No basket had grown heavy. No one had been asked to become a different kind of friend for her sake. She laughed at the morning picture of her arms piled high. "I would have been too busy holding things to notice their answers," she said. The grown-up poured water for both of them. "You brought attention," the grown-up said. "Attention is a present that fits in any pocket, even an honest one."

She thought of Penelope's apple, of Maude's voice, of Nabil's tins, of all the ways this home practiced care. Her way today was a round of gentle arrivals and gentle goodbyes. She waved once more toward the meadow, a small wave, not a summons.

Ursula made one last round that was even smaller, a round of looking from the porch and no farther. Button's stone was empty because Button had gone to his bed. Bramble was a soft shape by the gate. The pond was a dark mirror with a heron-shaped quiet that she did not need to check up close. She lifted her hand halfway, the same greeting that had worked all morning, and let it be for everyone at once. No bouquet, no basket, no new hat. The porch was near enough.

She told the grown-up the list from memory, without reading it, to be sure the shapes had stayed in her. Quiet hello. Slow blink. Three breaths. Far hum. One song line. Short walk together. The grown-up said she could visit again tomorrow and still bring the same light gift. Ursula smiled. Friendship did not run out when your hands were empty. It grew there, visit by visit, in the space you leave for a friend to remain themselves.

She folded the list once and slipped it into the notebook Orhan had left on the sill, not to hide it, but to keep it near other careful notes. Friends in this home wrote things down so kindness could be repeated on ordinary days. Ursula did not add a drawing of a giant present. She added a small circle, empty in the middle, and smiled at what the empty space could hold.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as Ursula wrote a list that was not about packing at all. It said: quiet hello, slow blink, three breaths, far hum, one song line, short walk with a grown-up. Friendship, she understood, is learning the shape of someone else's comfort and visiting inside that shape, empty-handed and full of care, ready to leave when the visit is complete.
