Teo wanted to make a surprise that the pond had not seen before. He did not want a toy that would stay and bother the water. He walked the edge where fallen leaves already lay, brown and gold, let go by the trees on purpose. "These have finished being leaves on branches," he told the grown-up. "May they be boats for a little while?" The grown-up said yes, if the boats stayed at the quiet edge, if Teo did not wade in, and if every leaf could fall apart and go back to being a leaf.

Teo chose five leaves that were wide and still bendy. He pinched a corner and tied it with a thin blade of dry grass, not tight enough to matter, just enough to make a cup that could float. His fingers learned the trick on the second leaf. The first one became a scoop. He laughed and set the scoop aside to be a scoop. There was no jury. Making things could include a funny shape that still counted as play.

At the pond he crouched on the path, the way Bastian and Nabil crouched for peas. He did not throw the boats. He lowered the first one until the water took its weight. It turned slowly, a small gold saucer. The pale duck noticed and came near because she was curious in a duck way, not because anyone called her. She nudged the boat with her bill. The boat spun. The duck looked extremely satisfied with the game she had invented. Teo set a second boat a little farther off and let the speckled duck decide. She decided yes. The third duck stayed with a patch of floating oats someone had shared earlier and let the boats be other people's fun. Teo felt glad that play was optional.

Alba arrived with Bramble, whose sun hat was on the peg at home today. "She likes a choice about hats," Alba said. Teo held up a ring he had twisted from soft fallen grass. "It is a tiny crown that is also a joke," he said. "It should fall if she shakes." They offered it on open palms. Bramble sniffed, accepted the ring between her ears, took two grand steps, and shook. The ring dropped. She nosed it, which meant the joke was a success, and she did not ask for it again. Alba clapped softly. "A hat with an ending," she said. "That is creative and kind."

Teo gathered nothing that was still growing. He did not strip the reeds. He did not pick the clover Selma had left for the bees. When a boat soaked through and became a leaf again, he let it drift to the reeds and stay there. The scoop leaf came home only because he wanted to show its funny shape, and he would return it to the compost afterward so the garden could use it. Creativity, he thought, is a visit, not a taking-away.

He sat with Alba and named the boats: Gold Saucer, Slow Spin, and The One the Third Duck Ignored, which was an honest name. They did not need the heron to join. The heron remained a tall patience at the far side, and the children kept their voices low enough to be good neighbors. Ducks finished their game and swam off in bright lines.

Teo rinsed his hands. A grass seed stuck to his wrist like a tiny reminder of the ring. He flicked it back onto the soil.

Teo carried the funny scoop leaf to the compost and told it thank you for being a good mistake. The pond still held one gold saucer that had not yet gone soft. He did not wade out to fetch it. An ending can happen without an audience. Alba hung nothing new on Bramble's peg, because the grass ring had done its short job and gone back to the ground. They agreed that tomorrow's making could be a song or a drawing if the leaves were too crispy to fold.

He showed the grown-up his sketch before the lines could be forgotten. Gold Saucer. Slow Spin. The boat a duck ignored. A crown with an exit. The grown-up said those were excellent titles for gentle inventions. Teo set the pencil down while the idea was still kind, which is a skill as real as folding. Outside, the water kept whatever it wanted and returned the rest to the reeds, exactly as promised.

He listened at the open window for ducks and heard only water against reeds, a soft settled sound. The game was over, and the pond was not worse for it. Teo liked endings that did not leave a mess for someone else to solve. He put his sketch under the smooth stone Flora sometimes used, so the breeze could not carry the sketch away before morning, even though the real boats were free to go wherever water took them.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as he sketched the three boats and the grass crown before the memory of their shapes melted, which was allowed, because the real ones were meant to melt too. Making, he decided, can be wonderful for an hour and gentle forever, if you build with what has already fallen and let the pond have the last word.
