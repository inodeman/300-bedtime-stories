Soren set two small cups on the ceramic studio sill, where the window looked over the plaza, the fountain, and the far bridge. The cups were cream clay with plum-purple rims, made for a shared drink when the juice was ready, not for a fancy show. His friend's cup was smooth. His own cup had a shallow dent where his thumb had pressed too hard while the clay was soft. If he turned that side toward the wall, the dent became a secret. He turned it toward the wall. Then he turned it back. Secrets on cups were still secrets.

His friend climbed the last step, a little breathless, and smiled at the pair. "They look like they belong together," the friend said. "We can sit at the window and watch the lamps come on at the market." Soren touched the dent with a clean finger. The clay was still workable, leather-hard at the edges and kinder in the middle. "Mine has a dent," he said. "I made it. I was going to hide that side. I do not want to hand you a cup and pretend it is the same as yours when it is not." His voice shook once and then steadied. The truth felt like setting a cup down instead of holding it in the air.

The friend did not laugh. The friend looked closely, the way a friend looks when they mean to understand. "Thank you for telling me," the friend said. "A dent is not a broken friendship. It is a mark. We can keep it, or we can try again while the clay allows. What do you want?" Soren had thought he wanted hiding. Now, with the words already spoken, he wanted the cup to be honest and also comfortable to hold. "I want to smooth it, with you here, so if I rush I can stop." The potter, nearby, nodded and set down the walnut sponge and a misting bottle. "Mist, wait one chime, then press from the inside with two fingers, slow as the ferry."

They misted the dent until it shone like a river pebble. The clock tower gave one kind note, and they waited through it without filling the quiet with jokes that would dodge the feeling. Soren's friend held the cup steady on the board. Soren pressed from the inside, not a jab, a patient palm. The dent rose. A shadow of it remained. "Again," he said, "lighter." The second press made the wall even. He checked with his eyes at the rim's level. "It matches your cup now," he said. His friend turned both cups so their handles faced the same way, ready to sit side by side. "They match because you told the truth and tried again," the friend said. "Not because the first try was perfect."

Soren felt something unclench behind his ribs. Friendship, he thought, was not a smooth cup appearing by magic. It was a person who stayed at the board while you fixed what you had not wanted seen. He rinsed his fingers. "If I dent it again, I will say so before I turn it to the wall." His friend grinned. "And I will say if I dent mine. We can have a dent club if we must, but we will not have a hiding club." They both laughed, soft enough for the clay. The potter set a tiny tray of plum slices, not juice yet, just the smell of later, and said the cups could dry together on the sill.

They sat on the bench by the window and looked out. The fountain held its wooden lily. The bridge wore its line of wooden fish. The book barge showed a square of light. The market lamps were beginning, persimmon-round and kind. Soren named none of this as a lesson. He only felt that the town was full of small repairs: a scarf retied, a saucer leveled, a seed freed, a pebble swept. His repair was a cup, and it counted. His friend bumped a shoulder against his, a brief friendly hello. "The rims look like plums," the friend said. "Good," said Soren. "Plums can be a little uneven and still sweet. I am glad this one is smooth."

He checked the cup once more, not from worry, from care. The place where the dent had been was warm from his hand and already cooling into the right curve. He set it so the handle was easy for his friend to see, nothing turned away. Honesty had made the sill more comfortable than hiding ever could. The potter hummed. The misting bottle caught a last bit of daylight. Soren said, "Stay with me until they are dry enough to leave?" His friend said yes. Staying was the easiest gift and the one he needed.

Outside, the early evening stayed gentle. They needed to be true in a small way, and Soren had practiced. The cups would be there, cream and plum, two handles facing the room. He rested his hands in his lap so he would not fuss the clay. His friend watched the plaza and did not rush him. That quiet was a kind of room they made for each other.

The lantern by the window glowed softly, lighting the two cups and the place where a thumb dent had been told and smoothed away. Soren and his friend sat in that gold and did not turn either cup toward the wall. Telling the truth kindly, and trying again together, had made the pair ready to share. The town's lamps answered the lantern, one small light at a time, and the sill held everything they had mended.
