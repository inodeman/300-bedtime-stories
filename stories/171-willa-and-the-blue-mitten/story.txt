Willa's lantern painted a small apricot circle on the footpath. The town lantern walk moved at the speed of conversation, which was slow. Papa walked beside her. Low garden walls held late flowers, and the flowers held drops of evening damp that shone when the lanterns passed. Somewhere a concertina breathed a tune like a polite accordion that had decided not to show off. Willa was watching the light on the stones when she saw a blot of blue that was not a flower.

It was a mitten, wool, with a white star stitched on the cuff. It sat on the wall as if it had climbed up to wait. Willa stopped. "Someone's hand will miss that," she said. Papa nodded. "If we leave it, the owner may come back. If we carry it, we must carry it openly so it can be recognized." Willa chose to carry it in her free hand, star showing, not stuffed into a pocket. The wool was a little cool and very soft. She did not squeeze it into a ball. She let it keep a hand shape, which felt respectful.

They walked on, and Willa lifted the mitten slightly whenever people passed. "Is this yours?" she asked, in a voice that was clear and not sharp. A man checked his pockets and shook his head kindly. A musician with the concertina showed two green gloves. "Not mine," she said, "but bless the asking." Willa's arm grew faintly tired. She switched the mitten to the lantern hand for a moment, carefully, so the paper would not brush the wool, then switched back. Papa offered to hold the lantern. "You found it," he said. "You can be the one to give it home."

They passed the flower wall again where a second blue thing turned out to be a painted stone, not wool. Willa laughed under her breath and did not feel silly. "Checking is free," Papa said. She asked two more walkers, and both thanked her for the care even though the star was not theirs. One offered a mint leaf from a pocket tin. Willa took it and let it cool her tongue while the mitten warmed in her other hand. The concertina's tune seemed to stitch along beside them, in and out, like the white star's thread. She held the mitten high enough to be seen and low enough not to wave it like a flag.

Near the square, the walk widened. Tables held pear slices and small cups of warm milk with honey. Willa did not set the mitten down near the food. She kept it in sight. Mrs. Pell stood by the milk table rubbing one bare hand and holding a lantern with the other. Her second hand was pink from the air. Willa's heart made a hopeful hop. She stepped close. "Mrs. Pell, does this star belong to you?" Mrs. Pell looked, and her face became a smile. "Oh, it does. I set it on the wall when I fixed my lace, and then the walk carried me off. I have been telling my fingers a story to keep them warm."

Willa held the mitten open so Mrs. Pell could slide her hand in. The wool went on with a soft whisper. "Better," Mrs. Pell said at once. "The star was a gift from my sister. Losing the warmth was small. Losing the star would have followed me home." She asked the server for an extra small cup and passed it to Willa. The milk was sweet and calm. Honey lingered on Willa's lip. "Thank you for keeping the star visible," Mrs. Pell said. "A pocket would have hidden the very clue." Willa felt the sentence settle like a blanket. She had guessed right about not hiding it.

They stood together while the concertina finished a phrase. Mrs. Pell flexed her mittened fingers and laughed quietly at how quickly comfort returns. Papa found Willa a pear slice. It was juicy and floral. Juice tried to run toward her sleeve, and she caught it with a napkin. "Will you walk the rest with us, or with your own group?" Willa asked. Mrs. Pell pointed to her cousin at the next table. "There they are. I will go with them, now that my pair is a pair again." She showed the other mitten, already on, a match without a star. "This one stayed loyal. This one took a little journey."

Willa walked her to the cousin so the return would be complete. The cousin thanked her too, and the thanks were simple, which Willa preferred. No one made a fuss that would turn kindness into a show. The lanterns bobbed. The path home curved by the same garden wall. The place where the mitten had waited was empty stone again, shining a little in Papa's light. "A wall can be a good shelf," Willa said, "and still not a home." Papa agreed. They did not need to find anything else. One return was a whole evening's good work.

Mrs. Pell told the short story of the star while they finished the milk. Her sister had stitched it on a rainy afternoon, using thread that was almost silver. "I was to have a pair of stars," she said, "and the second skein ran out. So one mitten is ordinary and one is a little sky. I like them better that way." Willa listened with both hands around her cup. She understood keeping something ordinary and something special side by side. When the cup was empty she rinsed it and set it gently in the washing tub, where it chimed once against another cup and then went quiet.

At home Willa washed the honey from her fingers and told the star story while the kettle cooled. She set her own mittens, both present, side by side on the radiator where they would be warm for morning. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and she looked out at the quiet garden wall down the lane, hoping every lost soft thing had a walker patient enough to carry it in the open.
