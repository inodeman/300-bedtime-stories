Sunday pancakes were wanted by three cousins at once, and the blue bowl was only big enough for one spoon to stir. Malik held the spoon. Joy held the spoon too, from the other side. Una stood between them with the milk and a hopeful face. Nobody was angry, but everybody felt a tug. The tug’s name was mine-first. It made elbows sticky and voices a little higher than kindness preferred. The pan waited. The berries waited. The morning did not need a pulling match. It needed a team.

Grandma Noelle clapped once, softly, the way a conductor might if the conductor were a grandmother in an apron. “Name the jobs,” she said. “A pancake breakfast is a parade of jobs, not a prize for one pair of hands.” Malik loosened his grip. Joy did too. Una set the milk down so she could think. They looked around the kitchen as if the jobs might be hiding like spoons. In a way, they were. Stirring was a job. Pouring was a job. Setting plates was a job. Washing berries was a job. Watching bubbles was a job. Carrying the plate was a job. Saying ready was a job.

Malik took a big breath. “I feel mine-first,” he said. “I want the spoon because stirring looks important.” Joy said, “I feel the same, and I also want the first pancake, which is silly because the first one is often the practice one.” Una laughed. “I feel left out of the spoon and loud in my head.” Grandma Noelle nodded at each feeling as if checking them off a kind list. “Good naming. Now trade importance around. Every job feeds the table.” The mine-first feeling had to share a bench with that idea.

They made a chart on a scrap of paper. Malik chose berries, and he chose them with his whole attention, picking out leaves and giving each berry a rinse. Joy chose plates, cups, and a napkin folded like a little boat for each seat, including Grandma Noelle’s. Una chose the stir, and she stirred in circles that did not splash. Then they rotated, because teamwork can mean turns. Joy watched the pan and called bubbles. Malik poured three careful circles. Una became the ready-caller, which meant she said “Flip” only when the edges looked gold. Nobody corrected her with a grab. They trusted the chart.

The first pancake was a little pale. They did not groan at Una. “Practice pancake,” Joy said, remembering her own words. They ate it in three bites, one each, standing up, laughing at the shared imperfection. The next pancakes were better. Malik’s pours grew rounder. Joy’s bubble calls grew confident. Una’s flip was steady. Mine-first tried to return when the prettiest pancake, the cloud-shaped one, landed on the stack. All three eyes went to it. Then Malik said, “Table job. We put it where everyone can see, and we cut it.” The cloud became shared weather. The tug let go.

They served Grandma Noelle first, because she had given them the parade idea, and then they served each other. “Berries for the stirrer,” Malik said, even though he was not stirring anymore. “A boat napkin for the pourer,” Joy said. Una added, “A middle piece of cloud for the bubble watcher.” The sentences were a game and also true thanks. The kitchen sounded like forks and pleased hums. Sunday felt wide. Three cousins had entered wanting one spoon. They had become a crew with many spoons, many jobs, and one breakfast.

After the last bite, they washed up in the same spirit. Malik washed, Joy rinsed, Una dried. When the blue bowl was clean, they taped the job chart inside the cupboard. Una drew three small figures holding different tools. None of the figures was bigger. Joy wrote, We feed the table together. Malik added the cloud pancake with three arrows pointing to three chairs. They stepped back and admired the chart as if it were a medal they had all won, which, in a way, it was. Grandma Noelle asked them to teach her the chart as if she had not suggested it. They did, with serious voices and bright eyes. Malik pointed to berries. Joy pointed to napkins. Una pointed to bubbles. Then they pointed to the arrows on the cloud pancake and said together, “Nobody’s job is smaller.” The sentence surprised them by arriving in unison. They laughed and said it again, on purpose, like a family band with words instead of pots. Mine-first peeked in, heard the unison, and could not find a place to sit. The Sunday kitchen belonged to the crew. They even saved the practice pancake story to tell at the next visit, so teamwork would have a memory, not only a meal. Una said they should leave a wooden spoon on the chart as a real pointer. They did, balanced gently, and it looked like a tiny flag of we-can-do-this.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the taped chart and the empty plates. Malik, Joy, and Una stood in its warm pool and felt the mine-first feeling far away, full and friendly instead. Teamwork had turned one spoon into a Sunday that belonged to every chair.
