Rosa could smell the honey before she saw the jars. It was warm and floral, like clover after sun. The tasting table stood under a row of lanterns at the town's evening festival, and the line was real but gentle. People held small wooden spoons. No one reached across. Mr. Ade, who kept the jars, moved as if the honey itself had asked for calm. Rosa's feet wanted to skip ahead. Her hands stayed at her sides because Mama's look was kind and clear.

"There is a turn for you," Mama said. "Turns are how a table stays friendly." Rosa took her place behind a man in a gray cap. She could see three jars: pale, gold, and a deeper amber. Cards said clover, orchard, and late flower. She read them silently. Reading helped the waiting have a shape. A fiddle nearby played a tune with long bows. Rosa breathed with the bows. The smell grew sweeter whenever Mr. Ade lifted a lid, then softer when the lid settled.

The gray cap moved forward. Rosa moved one step. She watched how he dipped only the tip of the spoon, tasted, and closed his eyes. He did not take a second dip from the same jar. "One taste keeps the jar for many," he told her, noticing her attention. "You can try all three if you use a new spoon." Rosa nodded. "I can wait for the new spoon." She liked knowing the rule. Rules, here, were a way of sharing. Her stomach murmured and then settled, as if it had heard the plan.

While she waited, a stack of clean spoons threatened to slide. Rosa put two fingers on the stack until Mr. Ade nodded that she could straighten it. She lined the handles the same way, a little fan of wood. "Good hands," he said. "The line goes smoother when the spoons behave." Rosa felt useful without losing her place. Mama held their cups of warm water. The cups sent up a faint steam. Rosa took a sip when Mama offered, so her mouth would be ready and not impatient. The water tasted like the cup, clean and warm. She counted three slow breaths and found the line had moved a friendly inch closer to the warm, glowing jars on the little wooden table.

Her turn arrived like a lantern being lit, not sudden, just next. Mr. Ade handed her a spoon. "Clover first," he said. "It is the quiet one." Rosa dipped the tip. The honey touched her tongue, smooth and flowery, not too strong. She let it melt. She did not rush to the gold. She dropped the spoon into the wash bowl and took a fresh one for the orchard jar. That honey tasted rounder, with a hint of fruit. The late flower was deeper, like evening itself. She closed her eyes for the last one, the way the gray cap had done.

"They are different rooms in the same house," she said. Mr. Ade looked delighted. "That is a taster's sentence." He did not offer extra dips, and Rosa did not ask. The wanting had changed into satisfaction. She stepped aside so the next person could stand where the smell was strongest. Her fingers were a little sticky. Mama had a damp cloth ready, lemon-scented. Rosa wiped each fingertip until the stickiness became only a memory. The cloth was cool. The lanterns made the jars glow like small captured suns.

They sat on a nearby bench and listened to the fiddle. Rosa told Mama which room of the house she would visit again another day. "The late flower," she said. "It tasted like waiting felt, rich at the end." Mama smoothed the top of her hair in a brief, everyday way and handed her a plain cracker. The cracker was salty. Salt and the memory of honey made a good pair. Rosa did not return to the table for more. She watched other faces learn the three rooms and felt glad the jars were still generous.

Mr. Ade waved when the line eased. "Thank you for the spoons," he called softly. Rosa waved back. She had come hungry for a taste and had left with a pace. That seemed a fair trade. On the walk home, lanterns passed them going the other way, people content and unhurried. Rosa matched their speed. Her tongue still knew the late flower. She described it once more so she would not forget.

A small song started in the line, barely above a hum, passed from the gray cap to Rosa without anyone deciding it out loud. She hummed two notes and let the next person have the third. The fiddle, hearing them, softened so the hum could live. Rosa felt the wait become a circle instead of a fence. Her toes uncurled in her shoes. When a spoon handle pointed the wrong way, she turned it gently, the way she had lined the stack. Order, she saw, was a kindness you could do with fingers while your heart practiced patience.

After the three tastes, Rosa held the damp cloth a moment longer and noticed a seed of wax on the table from a lantern overhead. She did not pick at the jars. She told Mr. Ade about the wax so the next spoons would stay clean. He lifted the lantern a finger's width and the dripping stopped. "You waited, and you also watched," he said. Rosa blushed in a pleased, quiet way. Watching had been something to do with the time, better than tapping her foot. The gold jar caught the raised light and looked like a little lantern itself.

At home she set her cup in the sink and washed the last hint of stickiness away. Mama put the cracker tin on the shelf. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and Rosa watched the light as if it were a jar of evening honey she did not need to open, because she had already had her turn.
