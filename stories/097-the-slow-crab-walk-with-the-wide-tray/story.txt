Asha stood with the wicker tray and discovered it was wider than her arms. Spoons chimed. Two cups slid toward the edge. "I need another pair of hands," she said. Uncle was folding the quilt into a picnic square on the rug. He looked up at once. "Then this is a team night. You take that side of the tray. I will take this side. We will walk like a slow crab to the blanket." Asha laughed and matched his grip. Together the tray behaved. Hot milk stayed inside the pitcher. The window lamp watched their careful feet in bedtime slippers.

They were building an indoor night picnic with the balcony door open, so the moon and the crickets could attend without anyone getting chilly. Paper stars already hung in the window. A cardboard telescope waited on a cushion like a guest. Glow-in-the-dark stickers sat in a dish, ready to mark each person's napkin. Asha had thought she might do every job and then present the picnic as a surprise. The tray had taught her faster than a speech. Some good evenings are built by passing things.

"Next the quilt corners," Uncle said. Asha took the two near the door. Uncle took the two near the chair. They lifted, flapped once, and let the quilt settle without a big billow. Asha smoothed her side. Uncle smoothed his. The rug disappeared under a soft field. Asha set the slippers at the edge so toes would have a dock. Uncle placed the telescope in the center, handle toward the door, so either person could reach it. "Center is for sharing," Asha said. "You remembered."

They poured milk as a team too. Asha tilted the pitcher. Uncle held both cups steady and said, "A little more, a little stop." Not a drop ran away. Asha felt the pleasure of a job that fit her hands because another person held the other half. They carried no pride that shoved. They carried the picnic. On the balcony they took turns aiming the tube, Asha naming the moon, Uncle naming the Big Dipper's spoon. When a breeze nudged the door, Asha held it while Uncle moved a cup away from the cool air. Trade and notice, notice and trade.

A cricket started a loud measure. Asha wanted to listen, and she also wanted the stickers placed. Uncle said, "You listen for us both. I will press a sticker on each napkin. Then you can tell me if the song has a repeat." Teamwork could divide a minute, not only a heavy tray. Asha counted three repeats and reported them. Uncle reported two glowing napkin moons. They compared notes and found the night had room for music and craft at the same time. Asha taped a paper star to the tray's handle so their tool would look like part of the celebration.

They ate nothing fancy, only a few plain biscuits broken into friendly pieces, and they sipped until the cups were light. Asha collected crumbs in one palm. Uncle brought the little broom from the kitchen corner. Sweep, hold the pan, tip, done. Neither of them did the whole cleanup alone. Asha liked the way the room returned to calm because they had planned the return together. She rinsed the cups. He dried. The wicker tray, empty now, went back to the shelf side by side with their efforts.

Before bed Asha stepped onto the balcony with Uncle and they thanked the garden for the music, which made them both smile at the politeness. The moon did not need thanks, but it was nice to offer. Asha said, "I thought a picnic was one person's show. It is better as a duet." Uncle nodded. "Duet is a good word for hands that agree." They came in, folded the quilt just enough to sit until yawns arrived, and set the cardboard tube in its basket. The stickers glowed faintly on the napkins they had left to dry.

Asha put her slippers under the edge of the rug and looked at the wide tray on the shelf. It no longer looked too big. It looked like a reason to ask for help and to offer help back. The crickets kept the beat. She felt the cozy tiredness that follows good work shared.

Asha and Uncle tried the telescope as a passing game. She held the far end steady on a cushion. He turned the near end toward the moon. Then they switched jobs without putting the tube down, hands trading places like a careful dance. The moon stayed in view the whole time. "We did not drop the picture," Asha said, delighted. Uncle said that was a fine picture of teamwork too. They marked the cushion with a glow sticker so the passing spot would be easy to find another night. The crickets seemed to cheer in their thin voices. Asha's arms were a little tired in the best way, the way that comes from carrying a good plan with somebody else.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the empty wicker tray and the folded picnic quilt. Asha crawled into bed with teamwork still warm in her hands. The moon stayed at the open door's edge, and the house held the quiet music of a job done together.
