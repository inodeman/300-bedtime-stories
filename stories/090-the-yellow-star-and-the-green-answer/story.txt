Malik tied a yellow paper star to a long string and let it rest against the window. Across the small garden, his friend's house showed green curtains and a lamp already lit. They had made a plan at snack time. When the moon was up, Malik would lift the yellow star, and his friend would lift a green one, so each of them would know the other was there, safe and thinking of friendship. Crickets warmed up their song in the bushes. Malik's slippers patted the floor. Hot milk cooled just enough in his favorite cup.

Grandma stood beside him with the quilt over her shoulders. "Friendship can wave without a loud voice," she said. Malik nodded. He did not need to shout across the night. He lifted the yellow star high, where the window lamp could shine through the paper. For a moment nothing moved in the other window. Malik kept the star steady. Waiting was part of the hello. Then a green paper star rose, slowly, like a leaf that had learned to float. Malik laughed a bright, small laugh. "There."

He swung his star once to the left and once to the right, the signal they had chosen. The green star copied him. Left. Right. It was a conversation made of color. Malik felt his friend close, even with the garden between them. The moon watched from above the balcony like a shared night-light that belonged to both houses. "We are looking at the same moon," Malik said. Grandma answered, "That is one of friendship's good tricks. Two people, one sky."

They took turns with the cardboard telescope. Malik found the moon first and described the flour-colored patches so Grandma could see them without the tube. Then she found the Big Dipper and told him the spoon story. He repeated it in a whisper, imagining his friend hearing a spoon story too, maybe in different words. He stuck a glow-in-the-dark sticker on the back of the yellow star, so if the lamps grew dim the signal would still have a gentle green edge. "A friend should be able to find you," he said.

Malik poured a little hot milk into a second cup and set it on the sill, not because his friend could drink it from across the garden, but because saving a place felt kind. Grandma suggested they sip their own cups while the stars stayed up. The milk was sweet. Between sips, Malik checked the green star. It was still there, patient and cheerful. He pressed his palm to the glass. Far away, a small palm pressed the other window. The glass was cool. The feeling was warm.

They did not stay up past bedtime. Friendship could keep its promise and still honor sleep. Malik lowered his star halfway, the sign for "I am heading to bed, and I am glad we waved." The green star lowered halfway too. Then both stars rested against their windows, not hidden, just quiet. Malik folded the string so it would not tangle and left the star where morning light would find it. He wanted his friend to see, at breakfast, that the yellow hello had stayed.

On the balcony he took one last breath of garden air. The crickets sounded like tiny applause. He told Grandma the best part was not winning a game, because there was no game. The best part was the moment the green star rose and he knew he had been remembered. Grandma tucked the quilt around him in the chair for a minute. "Being remembered is a glow you do not have to charge," she said. Malik liked that sentence so much he repeated it to his cup.

He rinsed the cups, put the cardboard tube in its basket, and lined his slippers up like two friends standing together. The paper stars on the string above the sill clicked softly. Malik looked once more at the green curtains. A lamp still glowed there, steady and kind. He felt full, the way a room feels full when someone you love has just laughed, even if the laugh arrived across a garden.

Malik stepped onto the balcony with Grandma and held the yellow star where the garden could see it too. The breeze made the paper point turn. Across the way, the green star turned as well, and both children, each on a safe balcony, seemed to share one slow dance. Malik did not lean over the rail. He kept his slippers planted and his free hand on Grandma's sleeve. "We can be close without crossing," he said. Grandma agreed. They named one cricket song as a shared count and then went in, because friendship also knows when to say goodnight. Malik set an extra paper star on the sill for morning, a small gift his friend would see in daylight, proof that the wave had been real.

The lantern by the window glowed softly behind the yellow paper star. Across the way, a green shape kept its own small post. The moon laid the same silver on both balconies. Malik climbed into bed with friendship tucked in beside his quilt, and the crickets carried the rest of the conversation through the night.
