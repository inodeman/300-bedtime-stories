Farah walked onto the bridge with a basket of smooth pegs for the bridge mender, her grown-up the mender themselves today, which made the job feel official and calm. Along the rail, at a height for children and also a little above Farah's easy reach, hung a line of painted wooden fish. Orange, silver, and plum, they all faced the market end of the bridge so small walkers would know which side stayed widest for feet. Farah stopped. Three fish near the middle faced the hill instead. "They turned around," she said. "The line looks confused."

The mender set down a tool roll. "The breeze loosened their ties. If the fish disagree, a little walker may weave." A small neighbor, younger than Farah and holding a grown-up's hand on the other side, pointed. "That plum one looks at the clouds," the child said. Farah smiled. "We can make them look at the market again. I cannot reach the cord alone." The mender nodded. "Then it is a three-person job. I will steady the cord. Farah will turn the fish. The neighbor will tell us when the nose points the same way as the others. No one climbs the rail."

They began with the orange fish. The mender held the cord still so it would not swing toward the water. Farah stood on her toes on the walking planks, both feet flat enough to be safe, and turned the wooden body until its painted eye looked toward the fruit stalls. "Is the nose right?" she asked. "Almost," said the neighbor. "A little more toward the lamps." Farah nudged it. "There," said the neighbor, proud to be the eyes. The mender tightened the tie with a simple knot. The orange fish stayed.

The silver fish was stuck half-turned, its ribbon twisted. Farah did not pull hard. "It needs untwisting first," she said. The mender pinched the ribbon open. Farah rotated the fish slowly so the paint would not scrape the rail. The neighbor watched like a captain of direction. "Now it matches," the child said. Farah felt the good click of a team when each person has one job and no one grabs all the jobs. Her arms were tired in a pleasant way. She shook them out and laughed. "One more. The plum one that likes clouds."

The plum fish had a tiny chip of paint on its tail, old and smooth, not a new hurt. They turned it last, carefully, so the chip did not catch. When its nose faced the market, the whole line made sense again: a school of wooden fish swimming the same way as the walkers who wanted the wide side. Farah stepped back. "They agree," she said. The neighbor clapped once, softly, because the bridge was a place for soft claps. The mender said, "I could not watch the noses and hold the cord and tie the knots all at once. Thank you both."

A family with a small child came from the orchard side. The child touched the first fish and then followed the noses, staying on the wide plank without weaving. Farah felt something unknot in her own chest. Teamwork had not been a loud game. It had been a cord held, a fish turned, a voice saying almost and there. She put the unused pegs back in the basket in a neat row. The river below moved slowly, showing no interest in the fish, which was correct, because these fish belonged to the rail and the walkers.

They checked every other fish, not assuming the rest were fine. All the others faced the market. Farah liked that they checked. "Guessing would be a second mix-up," she said. The mender agreed. The neighbor's grown-up thanked them, and the neighbor said, "I can be the pointer again if the breeze gets ideas." Farah said yes. The bridge felt wider when the markers told one clear story.

Farah walked to the market end herself, following the wooden school, and the clock tower chimed kindly behind her as if to bless the knots. She thought of how easy it was to walk when signs agreed. The painted eyes were only dots, but they looked friendly, all attending the same gentle direction. Her basket felt lighter. The work had been shared, and the path had become simple again.

Farah and the neighbor walked the line one last time from the hill end, naming colors as they went: orange, silver, plum, and again. The names made a little song that matched their steps. "If a fish turns, we will not argue with it," Farah said. "We will ask for hands." The neighbor promised to be the pointer. The mender, walking behind, said the bridge liked visitors who left it clearer than they found it. Farah liked that job. It did not need a loud voice. It needed eyes that noticed a nose pointing at clouds.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the bridge shed window at the market end, where spare ribbons hung in colors of orange, silver, and plum. Farah hung the tool basket on its peg. Turning the fish together had made the walking side clear for the next small feet. She looked back along the line, and every wooden nose pointed the same welcoming way.
