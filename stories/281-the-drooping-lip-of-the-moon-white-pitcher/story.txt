Yara stood at the ceramic studio window, where the light was the color of warm milk and the air smelled like rain on dry stone. On the board sat a moon-white cream pitcher no bigger than a soup bowl. She was making it for the flower cups at the plaza fountain, so a grown-up could give dusty petals a little water without soaking the tiles. The belly of the pitcher was round and calm. The handle fit her hand. Only the pouring lip would not behave. It drooped like a sleepy spoon. "If I pour now," she said, "the water will run toward my thumb."

The potter looked over from the wheel, wiping a sponge the size of a walnut. "Then we will not pour yet," the potter said. "Show me how you shape the lip." Yara pinched the clay upward with quick fingers, the way she pulled a ribbon when she wanted a bow right now. The lip rose, nodded, and leaned down again. Her shoulders drew in. "The clay is tired of me," she said. "I have already tried four times." The studio was quiet enough that she could hear a brush ticking in a water jar. She wanted the pitcher finished before the sun left the sill.

"Clay is not tired," the potter said, in a voice as even as a slow wheel. "Soft things remember a hurry. We will mist the lip, cover the belly, and wait through three kind chimes from the clock tower. Then you may try with two fingers and no tug." Yara's knees wanted to bounce. Waiting felt like a wide room with nothing to hold. Still, she nodded, because the drooping lip was not going to become a pouring lip just because she wished it. She set the blue misting bottle where she could see it.

She misted the lip until it looked like a river pebble, dark and cool, and laid a damp cloth over the pitcher's belly so it would not dry while they waited. Far away the clock tower chimed, low and friendly, like a spoon on a cup. Yara counted one. She put both feet flat. A stripe of sun crept a finger's width across the sill. She counted two and peeked once. The clay still shone, not dusty and not sticky. She almost pinched it. She put her hands in her lap instead and watched a paper boat, tiny as a petal, someone had left on the sill to dry.

When the third chime faded, Yara uncovered the lip. She misted it once more. Then she set two fingers beneath it and her thumb above it, and she stroked outward, slow as the ferry crossing the bright water. The lip rose into a small curve and did not nod. She stroked again, even slower, and the edge grew thin enough to pour and thick enough to hold. "It stayed," she whispered. The potter did not clap or rush. "See how the clay follows a calm hand," the potter said. "Ask it. Do not pull it."

"May I test it with one spoon of water?" Yara asked. "A spoon is a good small test," said the potter. Yara dipped a wooden spoon into a cup, tipped the water into the pitcher, and poured over the basin. The water left the lip in one clear line and landed where she aimed, singing a tiny song against the bowl. Nothing ran toward her elbow. Nothing made a surprise puddle on the tiles. She laughed once, softly, so the soft clay would not feel a bump. Her hands smelled like wet stone and oranges from the soap by the sink.

She set the pitcher on the drying board, out of the hottest square of sun. The potter nodded. "You made room for the clay to change its mind." Yara thought of the fountain flowers, pink and white, waiting in their cups across the plaza. Tomorrow a grown-up would carry this small pitcher over the bridge. Today it was enough that the lip could hold a careful line. She rinsed her hands. The water on her skin felt like the same water that had poured true, cool and simple and going where it was invited.

Yara dried the board and placed the misting bottle back beside the walnut sponge. She looked at the four little marks her earlier pinches had left, already smoothed away. Trying again had not meant starting over from a lump. It had meant waiting until her fingers could be kind. Outside, someone crossed the plaza with a basket of cups, and the clock tower rested between its songs. The pitcher kept its new curve, pale and quiet, as if it had decided to be helpful.

The lantern by the window glowed softly, though the day was not finished, because the studio lamp was lit a little early so wet clay would never sit in a dim corner. Yara touched the sill, not the pitcher, and smiled at the moon-white lip. A short wait and a slower hand had done what four quick pinches could not. She whispered, "Stay just like that," and left the pitcher to dry in the mild air, ready for the fountain flowers when the time was right.
