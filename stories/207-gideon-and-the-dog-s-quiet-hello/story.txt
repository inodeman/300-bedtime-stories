Gideon loved Button, and Button loved the whole idea of hello. The dog's tail was a flag. His feet were springs. When a person appeared, Button welcomed them with a dance that could stir the dust. Gideon thought it was wonderful, because it was love with fur on it. The speckled chickens thought it was a bit too much weather for one yard.

The chickens were not in trouble, and Button was not in trouble. They simply liked different kinds of mornings. The chickens liked to walk to their dust patch as if they were on an important stroll. Button liked to meet them halfway with joy that arrived all at once. Feathers lifted. Toes paused. Button stopped too, puzzled, because he had meant to be friendly. Gideon saw the pause and felt the lesson arrive before anyone's day could spoil.

"We can teach a quiet hello," Gideon said, kneeling so Button could see his calm face. The grown-up nodded from the step. "A quiet hello is still a hello. It tells the truth about love without using quite so much bounce." They chose a flat stone near the porch and called it the greeting stone. Button already knew sit. He knew it the way he knew his water bowl, which is to say, very well when a kind voice asked.

Gideon held a small dog treat on his palm, low and steady. "Sit. Soft eyes. One wag. Wait." Button sat. His eyes were bright. His tail tried to write a long sentence and Gideon smiled. "One wag is the whole poem," he said. They practiced when the chickens were elsewhere, so the lesson stayed peaceful. Sit. Soft eyes. A single sweep of the tail. Then stillness, which was the hardest and the kindest part. Gideon gave the treat and a slow ear rub. Button leaned into the rub as if quiet could be delicious.

Later the chickens came around the corner in their usual line, talking softly to themselves. Gideon felt Button's muscles think about the old dance. "Greeting stone," he said, cheerful but not sharp. Button went to the stone and sat. One wag. Then he waited, mouth closed, pride puffing his chest in a new style. The first hen slowed, looked, and did not raise her feathers. She walked the long way, then the shorter way, until she passed close enough to show that the yard felt ordinary again. The other chickens followed because they chose to.

Gideon did not clap. Clapping might have popped the calm. He whispered, "That was friendship." Button's ears tipped back in a happy, modest way. A chicken pecked near the stone, found nothing she wanted, and moved on without fuss. The space between dog and chickens had become a bridge instead of a gust of wind. Gideon realized his own hellos could use the same pattern. He practiced on the grown-up: a smile, a small wave, no sudden shout from the doorway.

In the afternoon they tried the quiet hello with Bramble at the gate. Bramble liked a calm approach. Button sat. Bramble blinked her slow blink. It was not a grand scene. It was better than grand. It was comfortable. Gideon told Button, "You did not become less yourself. You learned a second way to say I like you." Button's tail agreed with one careful wag and then rested.

They kept the greeting stone swept. Gideon told Flora, who was drawing on the step, that friendship can be studied like bees, from a respectful distance and then, if welcome, a little nearer. Flora added a small dog and three round hens to the corner of her paper.

In the late light Gideon tried the quiet hello on himself, which was a funny and useful practice. When Ursula appeared at the gate with a big wave already starting, he lifted his hand only halfway and said, "Soft hello, if you like." Ursula changed her wave to match, and Button stayed on the stone instead of turning joy into dust. The chickens did not even pause. Gideon felt the yard hold together like a well-set table. Friendship, he thought, is often a matter of size: the right size smile, the right size step, the right size tail. Button sighed, happy, and the sigh was quieter than a bark and just as true.

Gideon swept a little dust from the greeting stone so tomorrow's sit would feel fresh. He told Button the list again, not as a test, more as a bedtime story: sit, soft eyes, one wag, wait. Button's eyes closed halfway at the word wait, which Gideon counted as excellent understanding. The chickens had gone to their roost by their own path. The yard held no leftover fuss, only the shape of a hello that fit. Gideon turned the latch gently and let the evening be as quiet as the lesson.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when the yard grew gold. Button lay with his chin on the greeting stone, chickens dusting nearby as if this had always been the arrangement. Gideon sat between them, not pulling anyone close, just glad to be the friend who had translated hello into a language everyone could share.
