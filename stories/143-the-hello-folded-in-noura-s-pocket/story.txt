Noura climbed the known path to the hill lookout with her words folded small inside her. Cousins were already at the top, visiting from the next village, close enough that Uncle had driven them along the familiar road. Papa walked beside her with a picnic bag that bumped his knee in a friendly way. The path was the one they used on clear Saturdays, with the bent sign and the rosemary bush. Nothing about it was new except Noura's job. She had practiced a hello at home. Now her mouth felt shy. "What if my voice hides?" she asked. Papa said, "Then we will wait on the bench until it peeks out. Courage can be a small clear sound."

From the last turn they could see the wooden rail and the wide, gentle view of roofs, the canal's silver line, and the common like a green handkerchief. People were tiny and safe below. Noura's cousins, Jules and Amira, waved both arms. They were not teasing. They were glad. Noura's stomach gave a little flutter, the kind that comes before a school play with only one line. She touched the paper in her pocket where she had drawn a sun so she would remember to speak. "I know them," she whispered. "I just want my hello to be a real one, not a mumble into my collar."

Papa spread the blanket away from the edge, far from the rail, on the flat grass. "You can say it when you are ready," he said. "No one is timing you. The hill is not going anywhere." Jules held out a triangle of cheese. "We saved you the corner piece," he said. Amira patted the blanket. "Sit by me. I can hear even quiet voices. I practice that with my grandpa." Noura sat. The cheese was salty and good. She chewed and looked at the roofs she could name. "That red one is the post office," she said, and her voice worked for facts. Facts felt easier than hellos.

She tried the hello inside her head. Hello, I am glad you came. Hello, the lookout is my favorite bench. The sentences were smooth in her mind and bumpy in her mouth. A breeze lifted the edge of the blanket, and Papa set a shoe on it. Noura watched a bus, small as a toy, follow the lane. She thought of how the bus driver said the stops clearly so people knew where they were. "Clear is kind," she said aloud, surprising herself. Amira nodded. "Say it like the driver, then. Steady and not huge."

Noura stood up because sitting made her words fold. She faced her cousins, with Papa close and the picnic between them like a soft table. She put her hand on the paper sun in her pocket. "Hello," she said, and this time the word came out round. "I am glad you came. This is the lookout. If you look left you can see the little train shed, and if you look right you can see the common where kites live on windy days." Jules grinned. "That was a whole welcome," he said. "Better than a mumble." Noura's cheeks were warm, but her flutter sat down.

They spent a long, easy time naming what they saw. Amira spotted a canal boat tied at the dock, no bigger than a leaf from up high. Jules found the pale oval of the fair field, empty today, where a tethered balloon sometimes lifted only as high as a grown-up's hat. Noura told them the paths that were for walking and the rail that was for looking, not for climbing. "We stay on the blanket side," she said, and everyone nodded. Being the one who knew the hill made her voice feel useful. Courage, she thought, was not a shout. It was a true sentence you let out.

When the cousins had to leave, Noura walked them to the top of the path where Uncle waited, still in sight of the blanket. "Come again," she said, without looking at her shoes. "I will save the corner piece next time, if Papa says the cheese can wait." Uncle laughed. "We will come while the weather is kind." Noura waved until the car turned the bend she knew by heart. She was not tired from climbing. She was a little tired from being brave, the way arms are tired after carrying a full water jug that was not too heavy.

On the way down, Papa let her lead, because she knew every root. "Your hello reached them," he said. "I saw their faces open." Noura swung the empty picnic bag. "I think my voice was in my pocket with the paper sun," she said. "I had to take it out." Papa nodded. "That is a good place to keep it on ordinary days, and a good place to find it when you need it." The village sounds grew closer, a dog's polite bark, a bicycle bell, somebody laughing on a doorstep.

The lantern by the window glowed softly after they washed the picnic cups. Noura set the paper sun on the sill so the light could shine through it and make a warm circle. "I might say the lookout hello to Grandma on the phone," she said. "Phones are just voices too." Papa smiled. "She would like a clear one." Noura practiced once, not because she had to, but because the words tasted nice now. Hello, I am glad. Outside, the hill was a dark gentle shape, and the lantern kept her courage company until bedtime.
