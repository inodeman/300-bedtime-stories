The oak in the backyard held a treehouse the color of toast, with a little window that winked when the leaves moved. Nora stood at the bottom of the ladder and listened. Pages turned above her. Someone laughed in a small, friendly way. Uncle waited on the grass with a basket of cups. "They saved you a cushion," he said. "The latch sticks on warm days. If you need a hand, you can ask." Nora put her palm on a rung. The wood was smooth from many climbs. Her stomach felt like a balloon that had not decided whether to rise.

She climbed to the third rung, then the fifth. Leaves brushed her shoulder and smelled green and a little dusty, like a book that lived outside. Through the window she saw a neighbor girl sitting cross-legged with a picture book open on her knees. The girl looked up and waved with just her fingers. Nora waved back, but her hand found the latch and stayed there. The metal was warm. She pressed. It did not move. She pressed again, polite and then a bit firmer. The door stayed shut, not angry, only stuck. Nora's cheeks grew warm too.

She could climb down and say the story was probably long. She could pretend she had forgotten a cup. Those ideas were easy and they made her shoulders tight. Uncle's words sat in her ear like a steady hand. Ask. Asking felt bigger than the latch. Nora took a breath that tasted like oak. "Hello," she called, and her voice came out thinner than the one she used at home. She tried once more, closer to the wood. "The latch is stiff. Could someone help from the inside?" The page-turning stopped. That quiet was not empty. It was listening.

The neighbor girl came to the door. Nora could hear her fingers on the wood. "It sticks when the day is dry," the girl said. "I will lift while you press. Ready?" Nora nodded, then remembered nods do not pass through doors. "Ready," she said. They counted, one, two, three, in the same gentle rhythm. Nora pressed the latch. The girl lifted the door a finger's width. There was a small wooden sigh, and the latch gave way. The door opened onto a room that smelled like pencils and sun-warmed planks. Cushions the colors of plums and pears waited on the floor. Nora stepped in, and the balloon in her stomach finally knew it could rest.

"You asked," the girl said, as if that were the bravest page in the book. "I almost did not," Nora answered. She sat on the pear-colored cushion. Her knees bumped the low shelf and a tin of crayons clicked softly. Uncle's voice rose from the grass. "Cups are in the basket when you want them." They lowered a string basket and pulled the cups up, careful so nothing swung hard. Nora poured water from the small jug without spilling, though her hands still remembered the latch. The first sip was cool. A breeze came through the window and turned a paper star that hung from a nail.

They read the picture book together, taking turns with the sentences. The story was about a gardener who planted seeds in a circle and waited to see which color would greet the morning. Nora liked the page where the gardener hummed. When they reached a word she did not know, she pointed instead of skipping. "What is this one?" she asked. The girl sounded it out, slow as pouring. Nora said it after her. The courage from the door seemed to have stayed in the room, small and useful, ready for ordinary puzzles. Outside, a chickadee scolded a twig and then forgot why.

After the book they fixed the latch so the next visitor would not have to wrestle it alone. The girl showed Nora a drop of oil from a tiny bottle kept on the shelf for squeaks. They worked it into the metal and opened and shut the door three times. Each time it moved more kindly. "Asking and oil," Nora said. "Both help." They laughed. She looked down through the leaves at Uncle, who was reading his own book in a chair, trusting her to be up there. She felt tall in a way that had nothing to do with the ladder.

When the light went honey-colored they climbed down, Nora first, the girl waiting so the rungs stayed uncrowded. The grass was cool on Nora's feet at the edge of the blanket. She told Uncle about the stuck latch and the count of three. He nodded as if she had brought him something well made. "You used your voice when your hands needed a partner," he said. Nora carried that sentence home like a smooth stone in her pocket. The oak kept the treehouse in its arms, door closing with a soft, willing click.

At home she washed the dust from her palms and set a paper star, folded on the walk, beside her bed. She could still smell pencils and leaves. The problem had been a stiff latch and a shy mouth, and both had eased because she had spoken up. Mama heard the story and tapped the star so it turned. Nora watched it spin once and settle. She thought of seeds in a circle, waiting, and of a door that opened when two people tried together.

Before they closed the book, Nora and the neighbor girl looked out the little window at the yard below. The grass was dotted with clover, and Uncle's chair made a friendly square of shade. A ribbon someone had tied to a low branch lifted and fell, practicing the wind. Nora rested her chin on her hands. The height felt kindly now that the door was open behind her. "I can come again," she said, not as a question this time. "The cushion will remember you," the girl answered, and the planks seemed to agree.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the paper star. Nora listened to the house and, farther off, to the memory of leaves. She pictured the pear-colored cushion and the chickadee and the latch moving kindly. Her breath matched the slow turn of the star. Courage, she decided, could be as small as a sentence said twice. She let the oak and the evening fold around that thought until her eyes closed.
