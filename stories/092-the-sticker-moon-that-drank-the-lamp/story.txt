Nils pressed a moon-shaped sticker to the window lamp and frowned in a thoughtful way. The sticker stayed pale. He had wanted it to glow the moment it touched the glass. "It is not working," he said. Auntie looked over from the cups of hot milk and smiled with her eyes. "It is working. It is drinking light. Glow-in-the-dark stickers need a quiet wait before they can give brightness back." Nils rocked in his slippers. Waiting felt slow. Crickets outside seemed faster than his sticker. The moon, already glowing for free, hung above the balcony as if to tease him kindly.

Auntie brought the sheet of moon stickers to the table, under the lamp, not on it. "Let them soak up light the way a sponge soaks water," she said. "We can do other cozy jobs while they drink." Nils took a breath. He could be patient if patience had a shape. They laid the quilt on the rug. They lined up paper stars for tomorrow's string. They poured the hot milk and waited for it to cool enough to sip, which was another kind of patience sitting right in the cup. Nils blew gently across the surface. Ripples circled and faded.

"How long?" he asked. Auntie hummed one short song about a cricket who counted to ten. "When the song ends, we will look in a dimmer corner." Nils hummed with her and did not peek early. His toes still wanted to hurry. He gave them a job, tapping one slipper for each note. The song finished. They carried one sticker into the soft shadow beside the bookshelf, away from the brightest lamp. A pale green moon woke in his palm. Nils gasped. "It saved the light." Auntie nodded. "Patience let it keep a little of the lamp to share."

Nils wanted every sticker to have a turn. He did not rush the rest. He left them in the lamplight while he and Auntie stepped onto the balcony with the cardboard telescope. The tube smelled like paper. The real moon did not need charging, and Nils said so. Auntie agreed. "Some lights are born bright. Some lights, like our stickers and our good ideas, need time in the warmth first." They found the Big Dipper. Nils waited until the tube stopped wobbling before he named the bowl. The extra second made the picture clear.

Back inside, the stickers were ready. Nils peeled them slowly so none would tear. He placed a small moon on the telescope, a small moon on the milk tray, and a small moon near the door so the path to the balcony would wear a gentle glow later. He saved one sticker in the sheet. "That one can drink tomorrow," he said. "I do not have to use the patience all up tonight." Auntie looked proud in a quiet way. They drank the milk, which had become perfectly warm, not hot enough to nip and not cool enough to frown at.

Nils still felt a wiggle of hurry when he wanted the crickets to sing a certain song on cue. They did not. He laughed at himself. "They have their own waiting," he said. He sat on the quilt and matched his breath to a slow chirp. In, out, chirp. The room seemed to settle around him. Paper stars rustled. The barley-colored lamp shade glowed. Patience was not an empty cup. It was a cup filling.

He told Auntie the sticker moon was his favorite because he had watched it learn how to shine. She told him she liked the way he had let the milk cool without gulping. They put the cardboard tube in its basket and the sticker sheet in a drawer where morning sun would not bake it too hard. Nils set his slippers together. His body felt ready for bed, which was the last good wait of the night, the wait that turns into sleep.

He looked at the little green moons already keeping their soft posts. They were not loud. They did not need to be. A waited-for glow, he decided, felt friendlier than a glow grabbed in a rush. The garden crickets agreed in their steady way. Nils yawned and let the yawn finish all by itself.

Nils tried one more patient game while the last sticker finished drinking light. He set the cardboard tube on the quilt and promised not to look through it until Auntie had named ten cricket chirps. The chirps came unevenly. Some were quick twins. Some took their time. Nils kept his eye on Auntie's fingers instead of the tube, and the waiting turned into a rhythm he could trust. At ten, he looked and found the moon still faithful, not bored by his slowness. He laughed. "The sky can wait better than I can, so I am learning from it." Auntie poured a splash more milk and they watched a sticker moon on the tray grow surer, greener, and calm.

The lantern by the window glowed softly where the moon stickers had taken their drink of light. A green shimmer waited in the dim corner for anyone who looked. Nils pulled the quilt up, patient and pleased, and the night moved at just the right speed.
