Timo held two wooden knitting needles as if they might slip away like fish, though they were only smooth sticks with yarn beside them. The yarn was blue, soft, and a little fuzzy, smelling faintly of the cedar chest. Agnes sat close, her own scarf project already a small red square in her lap, the needles quiet in her hands. "I have never made a first loop," Timo said. His stomach felt fluttery, the way it felt before a tall slide, except the slide was two sticks and a string. "What if my hands do not learn?" Agnes nodded, respectful of the flutter. "Then we will make the first loop as many times as it needs. Courage can be tiny and still count."

Grandma had set them up at the table and then stepped into the pantry to sort jars, near enough to help, far enough to let Timo try. Agnes did not take his needles. She held up her own and moved slowly. "Yarn in this hand. Needle dips under. Loop steps over. The old loop hops off." Timo tried. The yarn slipped. The loop collapsed into a knot that was not a stitch. He laughed once, shaky. "That was a brave mess." "It was a first try," Agnes said. "Second try is allowed in the same minute." He made the second. It sat on the needle, loose and real. Timo stared at it as if it were a rare beetle that might fly. "I did a loop."

The next loops came like cautious footsteps. Some were fat. One was so tight his fingers ached, and he had to stop and breathe before he wiggled it looser. The blue row grew from a thought into a line. Then a loop dropped off the end and unraveled two hops. Timo's eyes went wide. The flutter came back. He put the needles down, not in defeat, but because courage sometimes sets a thing down before it squeezes too hard. "I am scared I ruined it," he said, voice clear. Agnes leaned in, not grabbing. "You did not ruin it. You dropped it. We can pick it up, or we can start the row again. Which feels brave today, not which looks fanciest?"

Timo thought. Starting over sounded honest and also tiring. Picking up sounded like a new skill, which was new and interesting. "Pick up," he said. "If it fails, we start over after, and that will still be fine." They laddered the stitch with a needle tip, Agnes pointing, Timo doing. The yarn climbed back into its place. The repair was a little snug. It showed. Timo touched it. "A scar of trying," he said, then changed the word, because scar felt too sharp for wool. "A mark of trying. I want to keep it." Agnes smiled. "Good. A mark can be a medal that does not shine on purpose."

They knit on. The needles began to click, uneven music, then steadier. Timo's scarf was narrow, a wrist-width ribbon that would grow into a wonky length for the back of a chair. He did not aim for a giant project. He aimed for finished. Agnes knit her red square beside him so he would not feel watched like a test. Sometimes she told a small story about a button that wanted a coat. Sometimes they were quiet. The quiet helped his hands. Sun warmed the table. A jar in the pantry clinked as Grandma shifted it, a safe sound.

When the blue ribbon was as long as a forearm, Timo bound off, Agnes showing the last loop passing through, Timo pulling the tail. The end was knotted. The scarf curled a little, as new knitting likes to do, and the mark of the picked-up stitch sat in the middle like a darker smile. Timo wrapped it around his own wrist first, just to feel it. It was warm immediately. "I was afraid of the first loop," he said. "The first loop was the door. The rest of the scarf was the path." He held it out to Agnes. "For your reading chair, if you want it. You stayed with me." Agnes took it with two hands. "I do want it. It is wonky in the best, truest way."

They showed Grandma. Timo did not hide the tight spot or the mark. He pointed them out. "Here I yanked. Here I repaired. The beginning is looser than the end, because I learned while I went." Grandma ran a hand along the blue. "That is a complete story in wool," she said. "And you did not abandon it when it dropped." Timo felt pride, a steady warmth, not a trumpet. He did not say he was a natural. He said, "I was a beginner the whole time, and beginners can finish." Agnes set the scarf on the chair arm. It looked cheerful and handmade, which is to say it looked alive.

They wound the leftover yarn and clicked the needle case shut. Timo flexed his fingers. The flutter was gone, replaced by the memory of a loop that had stayed. He knew he could be scared and still dip the needle under. That knowledge felt better than a perfect edge. Agnes poured water. They drank. The blue wool waited on the chair, already doing the job it was made for, which was to be warm and to remind them both that courage often looks like a second try.

The lantern by the window glowed softly along the blue scarf and the red square beside it, two colors keeping company in the evening. Timo and Agnes watched the light catch the mark of the repaired stitch. The scarf was finished. The first loop had been brave. The last knot was calm. Nothing about it needed to pretend to be flawless to be worth keeping.
