Ren liked the bakery at the edge of evening, when the racks were quieter and the air still held sugar. The town lantern walk would pass the door soon. People moved softly outside, paper lights bobbing. Mrs. Pell stood behind the counter with a tray of buns that had not found homes. They were golden, sprinkled with sesame, smelling like butter and warmth. She looked at the tray, then at the door, not upset, only thoughtful.

"They are still good," she said when Ren and Baba came in for a small loaf. "Better shared tonight than left to grow lonely." Ren touched the counter's smooth edge. "We could carry them to the walkers," he said. "Not selling. Offering. A basket and a quiet voice." Mrs. Pell's eyebrows rose in a pleased way. "I was hoping for an extra pair of hands. The buns are a gift if you will help them travel." Baba nodded. "We will walk as far as the square and no faster than a lantern."

Ren lined a basket with a clean cloth. The cloth smelled like soap and a hint of flour. He set the buns in a single layer so they would not squash. Sesame seeds tickled his palms. He made a second layer only when the first was even. Mrs. Pell added a little card: "Please take one. There is enough." Ren liked the sentence. It kept people from hanging back. He washed his hands at the sink, warm water, lemon soap, and dried them so the next bun would not shine with damp.

They stepped into the lane as the first lanterns arrived. Ren held the basket with two hands. "Warm bun?" he asked a walker, voice neighborly. The walker took one, surprised and glad, and the paper of their lantern glowed on the sesame. "It tastes like the bakery's afternoon," the walker said. Ren felt the basket grow lighter by one kindness. He did not press anyone. If a person shook their head, he smiled and moved on. Choice was part of the offer. Baba walked half a step behind, a steady shadow. When a bun shifted, Ren paused and settled it back into the cloth so the offer would look as kind as it tasted.

At the corner, a musician with a small drum had honey on his fingers from a cracker and did not want to touch bread yet. Ren set a bun on a napkin and placed it on the low wall beside him. "For when your hands are free," he said. The drummer thanked him with a softer tap, almost a heartbeat of gratitude. Ren waited to see the bun stay safe, then went on. The evening smelled of wax, sugar, and cool leaves. His arms were busy in a way that felt useful. He rolled his shoulders once, then returned both hands to the basket before the next lantern reached them.

Mrs. Pell met them by the square, apron folded over her arm, curious and calm. "How is the traveling?" she asked. Ren tipped the basket so she could see the smaller pile. "They are finding hands," he said. "One is waiting on a wall for clean fingers." She laughed quietly. "You thought of the honey. That is careful kindness." She took a bun herself, because the giver may also taste. They stood while a flute played a tune no louder than the cloth's rustle. Ren's shoulders relaxed. The job was going well.

He offered the last few near the cider table, where people already had cups and might like something to hold. A woman tore her bun and shared the halves with her friend without being asked. Ren saw the share spread one step further and felt a second warmth. "The basket is teaching," Baba said. "It started with you and it did not stop." Ren kept the card visible until the cloth showed through. Empty was a happy ending for this particular basket.

They brought it back to the bakery. Mrs. Pell shook the cloth outside so sesame seeds returned to the evening air and not to the floor. She paid Ren not in coins but in a bun wrapped for home, which he had hoped for and had not requested. "Helpers eat too," she said. He carried it carefully, as carefully as he had carried the many. The walk home was lit by other people's lanterns and by the bakery window still glowing behind them.

Near the bookshop, Ren paused because the basket handle had begun to press. Baba took one side without taking the whole job. "Shared weight, shared offer," Baba said. Ren's fingers tingled and then felt right again. He practiced the asking sentence once more, softer, until it sounded like an invitation and not a chore. A lantern painted a stripe across the buns, and the sesame looked like tiny tiles. Ren straightened the card when a breeze lifted its corner. The breeze smelled like rain that had decided to wait until morning.

When only three buns remained, Ren counted them against the people still without bread in their hands. He did not do hard sums. He simply looked. Two walkers accepted. The third bun he offered to Mrs. Pell again, and when she had already tasted, he offered it to the guide of the walk, who had been too busy nodding and smiling to eat. The guide's thank you was quiet and full. Ren closed the cloth over the empty center as if tucking in a basket for the night.

In the kitchen Ren unwrapped the bun and split it with Baba, fair down the middle. Butter from their own dish melted into the crumb. Sesame snapped softly. He told the story of the wall, the honey, and the halves. Baba said a town stays gentle when food is helped across the last little distance. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and Ren looked at the empty cloth, folded now, ready for whatever kindness tomorrow might need to carry.
