Zuri stood in the hollow tree library with a sheet of oak bark curled in her hands, wondering why the wood never used just one voice. Outside, the oaks whispered one way, the ferns another, and the moss seemed to speak by staying almost silent. She rolled the bark into a cone, wide at one end and small at the other, and tied it with grass. "A listening cone," she said. "If I put the small end to my ear, maybe the differences will stand up and introduce themselves." A gentle fox sorting leaf-pages paused, intrigued, and followed her to the doorway without knocking a single stack.

Zuri aimed the wide end at a cluster of oak leaves and closed her eyes. The sound rushed in, dry and many-layered, like a crowd of tiny palms rubbing together in a friendly game. She smiled. "That is the oak voice. Busy, but polite." She turned the cone toward the ferns. Their sound was softer, a brush and a drip of leftover dew, green in a way she could not explain except by saying it felt cool on her ears. The fox leaned close, and Zuri turned the cone so the fox could listen too. Its ear filled the small end. The fox's eyes widened, and it sat down very still, which Zuri counted as applause.

They made a game of it. Moss first, and the cone offered almost nothing, a hush with a heartbeat of damp. "Moss is the quiet friend who still shows up," Zuri said. Then the paper map in her pocket, which crackled like a little camp of twigs when she flexed it. Then an acorn cap tapped with a fingernail, a bright tick that made both of them grin. Zuri wrote sound words along the map's edge with berry ink. Oak: many palms. Fern: cool brush. Moss: kind hush. Map: tiny twigs. Cap: bright tick. The fox watched each word appear as if the letters were fireflies landing in a row.

Curiosity tugged them a little farther, but never out of sight of the library door. At the rope footbridge Zuri held the cone near the ropes and heard a low hum when the breeze passed, a note that belonged to the bridge and not the trees. "You sing," she told the ropes, delighted. She did not lean over or rush. She stood on the first planks with the fox beside her and listened until the hum faded. On the map she added bridge: slow hum. The discovery felt big even though their feet had moved only a short way. Questions, Zuri thought, were paths that stayed friendly if you kept the way home in your eye.

Back among the ferns she found a sound she had almost missed. The fox's tail, brushing a leaf, made a velvet sweep that the cone caught beautifully. Zuri laughed. "You are part of the wood's music too." She wrote fox tail: velvet sweep, then showed the fox the words. The fox looked proud and a little shy, the way someone looks when a friend notices a good thing they did not know was noticeable. Zuri let the fox hold the cone in its paws. It aimed the wide end at her sleeve. She swished the cloth. They both heard a small rainless rustle. "Sleeve: tiny weather," Zuri said, and added it, because a listening list could be playful and still be true.

Inside the library again, she tried the cone on the leaf-pages themselves. Turning one page made a sigh, thinner than ferns, drier than moss. "Page: paper sigh," she whispered, so she would not drown the very sound she wanted. The fox sighed on purpose, and they got the giggles, which the cone turned into a bright tumble of joy. Zuri had to set the cone down until she could be quiet again. Curiosity did not mean serious faces all the time. It meant caring enough to notice, and laughing when a notice was funny. She smoothed the map so the new words would dry without smearing into one another.

She left the listening cone on the library table with a leaf note that said please borrow and return, so other visitors could meet the oak voice and the bridge hum. The fox placed an acorn cap beside the note as a paperweight. Zuri copied the sound words onto a spare scrap for her mushroom cottage, because she wanted to remember them while she drank evening tea. On the walk home she did not need the cone to keep listening. Her ears stayed awake. The picnic clearing offered a wide soft nothing, a pause between songs. She stood in it and felt rich.

Near her door the ferns brushed their cool hello, and she greeted them out loud so they would know she had learned their name in sound. The day had been a string of small discoveries, none of them heavy, all of them close to paths she already loved. Zuri set her scrap map on the table and read the list once more, tapping each word. She could hardly wait for tomorrow's question, whatever it might be, as long as she could meet it with open ears and a way back to the hollow tree.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the scrap of sound words, making the berry ink look warm as the noises themselves. Zuri held an empty hand up to her ear, pretending for a moment that the cone was still there, and heard only her cottage's gentle quiet. Somewhere the real cone waited for the next curious visitor. She smiled into her tea. Curiosity, she thought, was a bark tube, a list of honest sounds, and the joy of finding that even a rope and a fox tail had voices worth learning by heart.
