A paper leaf stuck out of a picture book on the town library cart, green on one side and penciled on the other. Mei slid the book free. The cover showed a beetle with legs like tiny twigs, and the pages smelled like sunshine that had sat on paper. On the leaf, someone had drawn a small map of the library, with arrows and little squares for shelves. A question mark sat beside a jar shape. Mei's eyebrows rose. Curiosity tickled her palms. She did not yank the leaf free in a rush. She carried the book to the reading rug and sat so the map could breathe.

Omar was already on the rug with a stack of animal books as high as his knees. He scooted to make room. The rug fuzz warmed Mei's legs. "What did you find?" he asked. Mei turned the leaf so he could see. "A map that starts here," she said, tapping a square labeled Quiet Books. "It points to the low shelf, then the window shelf, then a jar. I want to know who asked a question with a drawing." Omar's eyes shone. "We can follow it like a trail of crumbs, only the crumbs are books, so we must not lose them." Mei grinned. "And we put each book back if it is not the next clue. Curious can still be careful."

They began at the quiet shelf. The arrow on the map matched a row of blue spines. Between two books Mei found a second paper leaf, this one smelling faintly of crayons. It said, "Count the spots, not to win, just to notice." The picture on that page was a ladybug. Mei and Omar counted seven spots and whispered the number so the rug would stay peaceful. A third clue tucked nearby showed a seed with a crack. The map's next arrow aimed toward the sunny window, where the science jars sometimes visited on library days.

The window shelf held nature books and one real jar brought from school, beans pressing pale roots against glass. Light made a bright stripe on the table. Mei checked the map. The jar shape had the same question mark. "Why do the roots bend?" she read from a tiny note taped to the saucer, written in kid letters she knew from the kindness mailbox. Omar leaned close, and his breath fogged the glass for a second. They watched. The roots curved toward the wet towel, and the tiny stem tips leaned toward the window. "They are answering the water and the light," Mei said. "The question mark was a real question."

They opened the picture book from the cart to a page that showed a plant bending over many days, drawn like a flip story. Mei moved her finger slowly so the plant seemed to nod. "Curiosity is following until the question gets a shape," she said. Omar added, "And then asking one more, if your mouth is still curious." His new question was simple. "Do all the jars lean if we turn them?" They did not spin anything wildly. They turned the visiting jar a quarter turn, with two hands, and left a scrap note that said, "Please look tomorrow. We turned this one gently." The glass was cool. The bean did not complain, because beans do not, and also because they were gentle.

Near the last shelf the map ended at a basket of blank paper leaves and colored pencils. A card in the basket, still in kid writing, said, "Make a question for the next reader. Keep it kind and possible." Mei understood. This was a library trail made by friends, not a puzzle meant to stump anyone. She chose a fresh leaf. It crackled. She drew the lost-and-found box at school and wrote, "What small thing can wait safely until its person returns?" Omar drew shakers and wrote, "Which sound feels like rain on a soft roof?" They tucked their leaves into two picture books that needed travelers, one about buttons, one about weather that stayed friendly.

Mei slid the beetle book back to its gap. The shelf looked tidy, which made her chest feel tidy too. Omar patted the jar note so the tape lay flat. They washed pencil dust from their fingers at the library sink. The water was cool, and the soap smelled like green leaves. On the rug they tried Omar's question out loud, shaking nothing, only whispering "shhh shhh" like a roof in a light sprinkle. It made them both smile. Curiosity had given them a path, and the path had given them new questions that other children could enjoy.

"I like that the map did not shout," Mei said. "It waited in a book." Omar nodded. "I like that we can add to it without taking the wondering away." They left the basket where the next hands would see it, pencils sharpened, leaves stacked. Outside the window the town trees held still, and inside, the pages seemed ready to be opened.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the jar and the paper leaves. Mei watched the question mark on the saucer shine, small as a seed. She and Omar sat a moment longer, listening to the library's quiet, already curious about what someone might answer tomorrow.
