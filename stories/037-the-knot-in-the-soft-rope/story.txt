Bodhi found the rope already arguing with itself. It was the soft white one used to tie the little wooden boat when the family came ashore for lunch, and someone, perhaps the wind, perhaps a hurried coil, had turned it into a dense knot the size of a bun. He sat on the smooth shingle and poked at it. The knot grew tighter, which felt rude. "I am trying to help you," he told it. The boat waited nearby, patient, its fender breathing against the post whenever a small wave visited. Gulls picked along the waterline and did not scold him.

Auntie came with the picnic tin. Grandpa came with the folded tide chart. They both saw Bodhi's eyebrows, which had become a knot of their own. "May we see?" Auntie asked. Bodhi held up the bun of rope. "I made it worse by being in a hurry. It still has to be a rope before we tie up for sandwiches, or the loose end will wander into the oars." He had a wish to be tidy while the cove stayed calm. Grandpa set the chart on a dry rock. "Then this is a three-person knot," he said. "Not because you failed. Because knots like this are little puzzles with more than one hand."

They knelt in a triangle. Bodhi found a loop that might be an ear of the knot. Auntie found another. Grandpa hunted for the loose end and discovered it tucked under Bodhi's loop, shy as a crab in shade. "If we all pull, it will lock," Grandpa said. "If we each hold our part open and I feed the end, it may remember it was once a line." Bodhi liked the plan. It was teamwork with a drawing he could feel. He held his loop open with two fingers, gentle, no yanking. The rope was warm from the sun and smelled faintly of salt and cotton. Auntie did the same with her side. "I have a window," she said. "Keep it a window."

The first try failed because Bodhi's fingers slipped and the window closed. He laughed instead of groaning. "The window shut. Reopening." They reset. A gull hopped close, inspected the bun, and hopped away again, granting them privacy. Grandpa fed the end through a gap that appeared only when both windows stayed honest. "Smaller," he murmured. "Now a little slack, Bodhi, not a collapse." Bodhi gave a thumb's width. The knot loosened with a sound like a sigh in a pillow. "It likes us," Bodhi said, delighted. "It likes that we are not fighting it," Auntie answered. The end slipped free. The bun became a coil that could be shaken into sense.

They did not leap up and celebrate into a new tangle. They straightened the rope along the shingle, Bodhi at the bow end, Auntie at the middle, Grandpa walking the last length to the post. Kinks popped out one by one. Bodhi learned to turn the rope in the direction it wanted, which was another way of listening. Then they tied the boat with a simple clear knot Bodhi could also undo, leaving a neat coil where no oar could marry it by mistake. The fender settled. The boat looked comfortable. "Sandwiches are now officially allowed," Grandpa said, and the cove seemed to agree by sparkling.

While they ate, Bodhi asked why three people had been wiser than one strong pull. Auntie drew in the air. "I could see my window and not yours. You could feel your loop and not the end. Grandpa had the end and not enough hands to hold the rest. The knot needed our different views at the same time." Bodhi chewed bread and cheese and nodded. Teamwork was not three copies of the same effort. It was a triangle. He decided to remember that the next time a puzzle showed up in string, in a story, or in a plan for the tide chart's little sums.

After lunch they practiced one easy coil each so the rope would stay friendly tomorrow. Bodhi's coil was slightly oval. He redid it without being asked, slower, and it became round. Grandpa did not grab it away to fix it for him. Auntie held the end so it would not spring. The second coil sat like a cake. Bodhi felt a clean pride that had room for the other two people inside it. Across the rocks a starfish rested in a pool, all its arms doing different quiet jobs for the same animal. He pointed. "Even that is a team," he said. They did not touch it. They let the comparison be enough.

On the path home Bodhi carried the rope over his shoulder, lighter now that it was only itself. He told the story of the shut window and the reopened window, and Auntie added the sigh the knot had made, and Grandpa added the moment the end appeared like a shy guest. The story needed all three tellers. That seemed like proof. Below them the boat remained neatly tied, having a rest of its own. Bodhi patted the coil. "If you tangle again, bring friends," he told it. The dune grass brushed his ankles, and the afternoon smelled like bread and salt and a problem solved without a single cross voice.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the round coil of rope Bodhi set in its basket by the door. The fibers caught the light and looked almost gold. He could still feel the loop between his fingers, the careful thumb's width of slack, the way the knot had given up once it was understood from three sides. Auntie poured water. Grandpa hung the tide chart. Bodhi washed pebble dust from his palms and felt wonderfully unsnarled himself. Teamwork had turned a bun of trouble into a line that could hold a boat and a story.
