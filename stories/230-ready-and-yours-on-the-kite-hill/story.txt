Jovan stood on the grassy hill with the kite spool held in both hands. Kesia stood a few steps away with the red kite lifted, waiting for a sign from the bush. The sky was clear, and the breeze was respectful, arriving in small polite puffs. "This kite needs a team," Jovan said. "One of us watches the string, and one of us offers the cloth to the air." Kesia nodded. "And we can trade, so the jobs stay fair." Above them, a single cloud drifted like a slow boat that was in no hurry.

Their first try tangled because they both gave directions at once. "Run," said Jovan, just as Kesia said, "Wait." The kite dipped, and the string made a loop near the grass. Nobody was cross. Jovan reeled the loop in and took a breath. "That was two captains," he said. "A team needs one voice at a time." Kesia agreed. They made a plan with simple words. The person holding the kite would say when the leaves moved. The person with the spool would answer, "Ready," and let out string. Only then would anyone take a step.

The bush shivered. Kesia called, "Leaves." Jovan answered, "Ready," and loosened a short length. Kesia raised the kite and angled it the way they had practiced on another day. The breeze slid underneath. The cloth rose to the height of the bush and stayed, thinking. Jovan felt the friendly pull. "Your eyes did the timing," he said. "My hands did the feeding." Kesia grinned. "The breeze did the lifting. We just shared the chores." The kite wagged its tail as if it liked the arrangement.

They traded jobs before anyone grew tired of one part. Kesia took the spool. Jovan held the kite. The trade took patience, because the string had to stay loose and the cloth had to stay clean of the grass. They talked through each motion. "I have the spool." "I have the kite." "Leaves are talking." "Ready." The second rise was smoother. Jovan laughed when the kite passed his nose on the way up. "Teamwork is a recipe," he said. "Leave out a step, and the dough does not climb."

A warmer puff bowed the kite sideways. Kesia kept the spool steady instead of yanking. Jovan, now watching from the side, gave only one note. "Let it find the middle." The kite found the middle. Jovan felt proud that he had not grabbed the string away. Helping was sometimes keeping your hands still and your words short. Kesia felt proud that she had trusted the note. "We are getting good at borrowing each other's calm," she said. The string hummed its tiny song. The hill smelled like warm grass and string.

They tried a longer flight. Jovan walked the spool down the path a little while Kesia stayed as the lookout for tangles. Then they switched without letting the kite fall. The handoff was the best part. Kesia placed the spool in Jovan's hands, and he did not pull until she said, "Yours." That one word kept the kite peaceful. Below them the harbor flashed. The respectful breeze never became a shove. It seemed to enjoy their manners. Jovan said, "The sky likes a polite team." Kesia said, "So do I."

When they landed the kite, they landed it together. Jovan walked his hands along the string. Kesia caught the cloth before it could kiss the ground too hard. They checked the tail, the knots, and each other's grins. Nothing was broken. Something was better. They had a way to fly that did not depend on one person being the hero. Jovan wound the string. Kesia folded the kite. "Next time, you can start with the spool," she said. "Next time, you can call the leaves," he answered. The plan for later felt like part of today's success.

They sat a minute and watched the bush go still. Teamwork, they decided, was not only for kites. It could be for carrying a basket, building a chime, or reading a hard page. The hill had been their practice room, and the breeze had been a kind teacher that never raised its voice. They started down the path with the kite between them, each holding a side, so even the walk home shared the weight of their happy work.

They practiced the handoff on the ground once more, with no kite in the air, just so their hands would remember. "I have the spool." "I have the cloth." "Yours." The words became a rhythm as easy as a song. Jovan said the rhythm could keep them kind even on a day when the breeze was shy and the kite only rose to their knees. Kesia said a low flight would still count if the team stayed fair. They shook on it, a small handshake that matched the string's earlier tug. The hill seemed to approve, wide and green and in no hurry to send them away.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as Jovan and Kesia set the spool on the sill where they both could see it. The string was neat. The evening sky was mild. They remembered the word ready, and the word yours, and how those small words had lifted a kite higher than hurrying ever could.
