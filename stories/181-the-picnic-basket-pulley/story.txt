Basil stood on the low wooden platform under the fig tree and sniffed the warm afternoon. The leaves made a soft hush. Below him, on a checked picnic blanket, Cleo held a round basket that smelled like tomato sandwiches, cucumber, and orange peels. A bee hummed over the clover and drifted away. The platform was only a few wide steps high, with a sturdy rail Papa had sanded smooth, but the basket was broad. The sandwiches would squash if anyone tried to climb one-handed, and the juice bottle already clinked when Cleo shifted her grip.

"I can carry it," Cleo called, "but the bread will slide." Basil rested his chin on his folded arms. The rail felt warm. "What if the basket comes up by itself?" he said. "Not by magic. By a pulley. There is a smooth wooden ring in the shed, and we have a soft rope." Cleo grinned and set the basket in the shade so the cheese would stay cool. "Then we both get to invent and eat," she said. They could already imagine crumbs on their knees and the sweet pop of orange peel.

They walked to the shed, where the air smelled like cedar shavings and dry soil. Nana looked up from a coil of garden twine, her glasses a little dusty. "A pulley for a picnic?" she asked. "That sounds clever and careful. I will tie the rope so it cannot slip, and I will keep my hands near it the first time." Basil and Cleo agreed at once. Inventing was their job. Knots that had to hold a basket were Nana's job. The shed door clicked behind them, and a stripe of sunlight lay across the floor like a path.

They chose a fat cotton rope that felt fuzzy in their palms. Nana looped it through the wooden ring and fixed the ring to the beam. Basil and Cleo taped a cardboard cradle under the basket handle so the bottom would not tip. The tape made a sticky whisper. Cleo drew a tiny sandwich on the cardboard. "This side smiles at the sky," Basil said, tapping the drawing. They pressed every edge until it felt firm. The basket now wore a little cardboard jacket, pale brown and proud.

"Ready," Basil called. He and Nana pulled hand over hand. The basket rose, slow as a yawn, then tilted. A napkin fluttered onto the clover like a small white bird. The juice bottle chimed against the basket wall. "Stop, please," Cleo said, calm and clear. Nana held the rope steady. Nothing spilled. The sandwiches stayed wrapped in their clean cloth. The basket hung with one corner lower, like a sleepy boat waiting for a kinder tide. Cleo put one hand on the cardboard so it would not swing. The cotton rope was warm where their palms had rubbed it, and a clover blossom stuck to her shoe.

Basil climbed down the wide steps, touching the rail as he went. He studied the single loop. "The handle is pulling from only one place," he said. "We need two strings, like two friendly hands." Nana nodded. "Good noticing. Fingers away from the knot until I say it is safe." She added a second loop while the children held the basket level. The new rope smelled faintly of soap. They slid a flat piece of cardboard under the bottle so it would not roll. Cleo checked the drawing. The sandwich still smiled at the sky.

They tried again. "Up, up, easy," Cleo said, almost singing. The rope hummed softly over the wooden ring, and the basket rose straight. Basil guided it onto the platform. The sandwiches sat plump and square. He poured cool water, and a lemon slice floated like a tiny boat. The first sip tasted bright. From up there the yard looked like a green map, with the blanket as a bright island. They ate while a fig leaf tickled Basil's ear and a neighbor's wind chime made three notes.

"The pulley did the heavy part," Cleo said, "but we did the thinking, and we stopped when it tilted." "And Nana did the knots," Basil said. He passed her the orange. She split it and gave him a half without measuring, juice shining on their fingers. They sent the empty napkin down as a test, and it rode as neatly as a little flag. Crumbs dotted the platform. The rope waited below, ready for the basket when the picnic was done, and the clover smelled green and sunny.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when they came inside at dusk, their fingers smelling of orange and tape. Nana set the wooden ring on its hook, and the kitchen felt as steady as the basket had felt on its second ride. Basil washed the cups until they squeaked. In the lantern light the cups shone like small moons. Cleo whispered, "Teamwork made it light." She smoothed the cardboard sandwich drawing so they could use the cradle again. Outside, the fig leaves still hushed, and the rope creaked once, a tiny good-night over the dark grass.
