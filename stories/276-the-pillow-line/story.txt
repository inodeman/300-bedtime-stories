The shared bedroom had one window, one stripe of morning light, and two cousins who both loved that stripe. Omar set his pillow on the left. Tomas set his pillow on the left too, which was the same left, and the pillows collided like polite boats in a narrow harbor. They looked at each other. Nobody yanked. Still, the feeling rose, a cousin of disappointment. “I was hoping for the sun on my blanket,” Omar said. “I feel tight about it.” Tomas nodded. “I feel the same tight. If we both win the left, nobody sleeps.”

They sat on the rug with the pillows in their laps, which made them look like mushrooms. The tightness needed a plan. Omar remembered the mango rules from another visit, the way turns had made one bowl feel fair. “What if the bed has a sharing map?” he said. “A pillow line down the middle. Left side tonight for me. Right side is still good. Tomorrow we trade, and you get the stripe.” Tomas traced the middle with his finger. “And in the morning we open the curtain together, so the stripe is a show we both watch, even if it lands on one blanket.” Omar’s tight feeling sat up straighter, interested. “That is sharing, not losing,” he said.

They built the line with two extra pillows, end to end, a soft border. It was not a wall to keep kindness out. It was a reminder. Omar placed his book on his side. Tomas placed his socks, rolled into a nest, on his side. They checked the line from the door. It looked like a path and a joke. Tomas chuckled. “If I roll over the line in my sleep, you may gently roll me back, like a log, not like a grump.” Omar agreed. “Same for me. Grumps are not invited.” Naming grumps made them less likely. The room felt playful again.

Night came softly. The lantern was not yet the evening one in the kitchen. In the bedroom, a small lamp glowed. They took turns with the sink, then returned and did not rush to claim. Omar lay on the left and felt the cool of the window nearby. He also felt a pinch of guilt, because Tomas was on the right and the stripe was only a promise for morning. He whispered, “Do you feel left out of the window?” Tomas whispered back, “A little. And I feel glad we have a trade. Glad is bigger than a little.” Omar let those words sit on the pillow line between them like a bridge. Sharing included hearing the little feelings, not only the plan.

Morning arrived with a pale gold stripe, true to its habit. It fell across Omar’s blanket and made a warm road. He did not hug the road secretly. He sat up and said, “Curtain team?” Tomas scrambled over, careful of the pillow line, and they pulled the curtain wider together. The stripe grew and visited both sides for a moment before settling. They watched dust sparkle. Tomas said, “Tomorrow this road is mine, and I will still call you for the opening.” Omar said, “Deal.” The tight feeling from yesterday was a wrinkled sock now, easy to smooth. They made their beds on their own sides, then helped with the middle pillows so the line would be ready for the next night.

During the day the bedroom was a place to rest books, not a place to worry. When they came back after lunch, Omar found he had left a marble on Tomas’s side by mistake. He moved it back and told Tomas, because secret fixing felt less honest than a sentence. “I crossed the line with a marble,” he said. “I put it home.” Tomas grinned. “A marble is a tiny traveler. Thank you for the return ticket.” They added a new rule to a card on the dresser. We tell, we trade, we open the curtain together. The card was sharing written down so sleepy heads could remember.

By the second night, the trade felt easy. Tomas took the left. Omar took the right and discovered the right had a better view of the poster of leaves they had drawn. He had not noticed when he was busy wanting the other side. “The right side was a surprise gift,” he said. Tomas, already proud of the stripe and not stingy with it, answered, “Then both sides are good. We just needed turns to find out.” They bumped fists over the pillow line, gentle, a goodnight that did not knock anything over. The room held them both without choosing a favorite. Omar whispered one more rule, almost asleep. “If the stripe is extra wide, we call it a shared road and both put a hand in the light.” Tomas whispered, “Yes. A road, not a prize.” The tight feeling had no job left. It curled up and made room for rest.

The lantern by the window glowed softly along the pillow line and the gold memory of the morning stripe. Omar and Tomas watched from their pillows, each on the side that was his for this night. Sharing had given the window to both of them, one sunrise at a time, and the bedroom felt fair, friendly, and wide enough.
