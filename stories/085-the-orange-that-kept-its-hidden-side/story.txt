Petra carried an orange to the table and set it beside the window lamp. The real moon, outside above the balcony, was not a full circle tonight. It looked as if someone had taken a neat bite and left the rest shining. Petra's eyebrows drew together in a thinking way, not a worried way. "Where did the other part go?" she asked. Crickets ticked in the garden. Her bedtime slippers whispered on the floor. A quilt lay ready on the chair, and hot milk cooled in a blue cup.

Papa dried his hands and sat down. "The moon did not lose a piece," he said. "It is still round. We only see the part where the sunlight is landing. I can show you with this orange, if you are curious." Petra loved that word. Curious felt like a pocket full of questions that were allowed to come out. She nodded so fast her hair bounced. "I want to know, and I want to look, and then I want to know again."

Papa held the orange. The lamp was the sun for their little lesson. He turned the orange slowly. When the lamp lit all of the face Petra could see, he said, "Full." When only a side gleamed, he said, "Half." When just a curved edge shone, he said, "Crescent, like a smile." The rest of the orange was still there, tucked in softer light. Petra touched the dim side. It was not gone. It was only quieter. "The moon is doing the same thing, very far away," Papa said. "Nothing is broken."

They stepped onto the balcony with the cardboard telescope. The tube was snug in Petra's hands. She found the bright curve and held the tube still. "It matches the orange smile," she said. Papa pointed to a faint wash of stars. "That wide spoon shape is the Big Dipper. The stars stay in their places while the moon's light changes night by night. You can check again tomorrow and the smile may be a little wider." Petra liked a question that came with a tomorrow.

Back inside she opened a plain notebook. She drew four circles. In the first she shaded almost nothing. In the second she shaded a smile. In the third she shaded half. In the fourth she shaded the whole round face and wrote "full" in small letters. She did not worry about perfect circles. The orange sat on the page as a model, round and smelling sharp and sweet. She stuck one glow-in-the-dark sticker in the corner of the page so her moon notes could glimmer at bedtime like a tiny reminder to keep asking.

Petra still had a question. "If the orange is the moon, what are the paper stars on our window?" Papa laughed kindly. "Those are our own making. Real stars are far-off suns, shining on their own. Our paper ones are for joy, and they do not have to match the sky to be worth hanging." Petra taped a new paper star above her drawing, a little one, and decided it could be the orange's friend. Curiosity, she thought, did not squash wonder. It gave wonder more rooms to walk through.

She sipped the hot milk and listened. A cricket near the balcony pot sang one long phrase and then a short one. Petra drew a tiny cricket beside the crescent, not because crickets change the moon, but because the night held many true things at once. She looked through the tube one more time from the doorway, safe and warm, quilt over her shoulders. The moon's bright edge was steady. The hidden part was only hidden, like a secret that sunlight would tell later.

Before bed she put the orange in a bowl so someone could eat it in the morning, and she left the notebook open under the window lamp. Questions did not need to be finished to be good. They needed to be cared for. Petra lined up her slippers and touched the cardboard tube as if thanking it for the clear look. The garden smelled green. The sky looked honest.

Petra took the orange and the notebook onto the balcony for one more check, quilt around her shoulders like a cape that could think. She held the fruit beside the sky, not to touch the moon, but to let her eyes compare. "Smile to smile," she said. Papa pointed out that the moon's bright part faced the same way as the orange's bright part when he turned it just so. Petra drew a tiny lamp in the margin so she would remember which side was the sun in their game. A cricket sang from the mint, and she added a note that said the garden was awake too. Curiosity had room for fruit, stars, and small singers. She did not need every answer before bed. She needed one true look and a page that could wait with her until morning.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the four drawn moons and the real curve outside. Paper stars kept their cheerful watch on the glass. Petra slid under the quilt with her question tucked beside her like a smooth orange, round and whole even when only part of it showed. Tomorrow she would look again, and that was a happy plan.
