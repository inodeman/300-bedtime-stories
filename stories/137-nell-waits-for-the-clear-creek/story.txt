The creek after rain was the color of tea with too much milk. It still moved, but it hid its stones. Nell stood on the bank in boots and looked for the three rocks she had painted yesterday with dots of white, blue, and yellow, a tiny code that meant this bend was a friend. The paint was the kind that stayed wet-safe once dry. She had planned to visit them like neighbors. Today the neighbors were under a curtain. "The creek is thinking," Auntie said. "Mud from the upper path is passing through. If we wait, it lets go."

Nell wanted to wade in and feel for the dots with her toes. Auntie shook her head in a way that was information, not a scolding. "Feet stir the curtain thicker. Eyes can wait on the bank." Nell sat on the flat rock they always used, the one that stayed dry even when the creek was proud. Her boots dangled. The water sounded huskier than usual, a broom sweeping pebbles. She sighed once, then decided the sigh had done its job. "I will wait," she said. "Tell me if you see a dot before I do." Auntie sat beside her and opened a tin of apple pieces that smelled like the inside of a good day.

Waiting became a string of small notices. A leaf rode the milky water and did not snag. A beetle crossed Nell's boot and continued its errand. The smell of wet bark rose and softened. Nell made a chain of clover while her eyes rested and returned. Each time she looked, the creek was a shade more itself. "It is like a picture drying," she said. Auntie agreed. "Rushing a drying picture smears it. So does rushing a creek." Nell ate an apple piece. It cracked. The sound was brighter than the water. She did not count the minutes. She counted clover loops instead, and when she reached twelve she started a second chain for Auntie.

A clear patch appeared near the far edge, small as a window. Nell pointed and did not stand. Inside the window a plain stone showed, then slid back into milk as a swirl passed. "Almost," she whispered. Almost was not a reason to splash. She put her hands under her knees to keep them honest. The broom-sound of the creek grew lighter, more like spoons again. A dragonfly tested the air and chose a reed. Nell told it, "We are waiting for dots." The dragonfly did not answer, which she allowed. Patience could include company that did not speak.

Then the middle of the bend cleared, as if someone had lifted a veil by the corner. Nell saw blue first, three dots in a triangle. Then yellow, a little sun. Then white, a tiny path of specks. "There," she said, and her voice was a grin. She did not jump in. She looked. The paint had held. The stones had stayed put while the mud did its travel. Auntie clapped softly so the bank would not shake. "Your code kept its promise, and so did the waiting." Nell felt the clover chains in her lap, cool and green, a record of the time she had not wasted.

They stayed until a second veil of silt passed and cleared again, quicker this time. Nell learned the rhythm: cloudy, window, clear, a breath, clear. She told Auntie she would remember the rhythm on days when a wish felt late. Auntie said creeks are good teachers because they do not hurry for an audience. Nell rinsed her fingers in a cup of drinking water, not in the bend, and dried them on a cloth. The painted stones glowed under a hand's depth of clean water. She lifted a hand hello, and the dots did not need to wave back.

Before they left she stacked the clover chains on the flat rock as a temporary crown for the bank. The next rain would reuse them, and that was fair. Her boots had stayed dry. Her plan had changed from touching to seeing, and seeing had been the richer visit. The problem had been a milky curtain and a pair of impatient toes. The toes had stayed on the rock. The curtain had moved on. Nell walked up the path looking back only once, to be sure the blue triangle was still a triangle.

At home she drew the three codes on paper and wrote the word wait beside them in letters she liked. She told Mama about the beetle's errand and the spoon-sound returning. Mama hung the drawing where the morning light would find it. Nell's hands smelled like clover and apple. She felt full of a slow kind of success. The creek, she knew, was still practicing clearness without her watching, which meant the waiting had been a visit, not a job the water needed her to finish.

Nell lined her boots up with the door and brushed a clover petal from her sleeve onto the step, a green goodbye to the bank. She told the painted dots, in her mind, that she would visit when the water was clear or cloudy, as long as she could be patient either way. Auntie hung the damp cloth to dry and said the creek would keep their seats warm in its own cool fashion. Nell liked a place that saved seats without ropes. The second clover chain was still on the rock, she hoped, feeding the soil a little sweetness.

She poured a last cup of drinking water and tasted the tin rim, cool and plain. The taste belonged to waiting days. Nell decided she would bring the white paint next time only if a dot had faded, not because she was restless. Restless paint could cover a code that was still fine. Auntie agreed that looking comes before fixing. They left the window cracked so the room would smell a little like wet leaves, a polite visitor from the bend.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the drawing of blue, yellow, and white dots. Nell looked at the light and pictured the bend, spoons clicking, stones keeping their code. She was glad she had not stirred the curtain. Patience had lifted it for her. She closed her eyes and saw a small window open in milky water, and then the whole bend bright, like a story that had decided to be readable.
