Mateo sat on a stump in the picnic clearing and lifted a leaf-roll whistle to his lips. He had made it that morning from a sturdy oak leaf wound into a loose tube and tied with a thread of grass. When he blew gently, it hummed a note as round as a pebble dropped in a cup. He played three notes, low, middle, and a little hop upward, then listened to the oaks rustle back. For several warm days a young fox had listened from the ferns, ears showing, body hidden, never quite coming to the stump. Mateo did not call it out. He left space in the music, the way a path leaves room for another pair of feet.

Today he had brought a second leaf-roll whistle, the same size, tied with a paler grass thread so they would not get mixed up. He set it on a flat rock halfway between the stump and the ferns. "This one is yours if you want it," he said toward the green shade. "If you do not, it can stay a leaf, and that is fine too." He played the three notes again and then put his whistle in his lap. The quiet was part of the invitation. A firefly rested on the rock beside the spare whistle, then drifted off.

The ferns shivered. The young fox stepped out, cautious and curious, and stopped a body-length from the rock. Mateo smiled without showing too many teeth, because he had learned that soft faces feel safer. "Hello," he said. "I am Mateo. I like making sounds that do not startle the moss." The fox looked at him, then at the whistle. It sniffed the grass tie, sneezed at a speck of dust, and sat down. Mateo played the low note and waited. The fox's ears swiveled. He played the middle note. The fox leaned forward. He played the hop. The fox's mouth opened, and a small breathy huff came out, almost the same hop, almost a laugh.

"You have a note of your own," Mateo said, delighted. He nudged the second whistle with a fingertip, closer to the fox but not into its paws. "You can hold this, or you can answer with your voice. Friends do not have to match exactly." The fox touched the leaf tube with its nose, then, after a long thinking pause, picked it up very gently and set it down again, as if to say the gift was accepted even if fox lips were not made for grass ties. Mateo nodded as if that were perfect, because it was. He played the low note. The fox answered with a soft huff. Middle note. A warmer huff. Hop. A brighter one.

They played the pattern twice, then Mateo changed it, hop first, to see if the fox would follow. The fox did, after one miss that made them both pause and try again without any fuss. Missing a note felt ordinary and easy to fix. Mateo told a tiny story between songs about the rope footbridge and the hollow tree library. The fox listened with its chin high. When Mateo grew thirsty, he poured berry water into two acorn caps. The fox drank from one. Sharing a pause felt like part of the music too.

Later they walked a short loop so the whistles could rest. Mateo showed his paper map and pointed to the clearing. "I will come back tomorrow, here, if you would like another song," he said. The fox tapped the map with a claw, right on the clearing circle, a yes Mateo felt in his ribs. They did not need a long promise. A tomorrow in the same spot was a strong friendship seed. On the way past the mushroom cottages, Mateo kept the spare whistle in an open palm so the fox could see it was still the fox's. At the fern edge the fox took it again, carried it three proud steps, and placed it on a clean leaf to keep for the night.

Mateo did not take it back. Giving a second whistle meant he had enough song to share, and it meant the fox could practice huffs beside a shape that matched his own. He played one last low note from the path, not a command, just a goodnight color in the air. The fox answered from the ferns, a little braver than at the start of the afternoon. Mateo felt the sound land in the center of his chest like a warm pebble. He had solved the lonely feeling not by grabbing, but by making room and a second tube of leaf.

He walked home slowly, humming the three notes so he would not forget the fox's timing. The oaks laid dappled light on his sleeves. He waved at the rope footbridge just because joy likes to spill. At his door he looked at his own whistle and smiled at the paler thread he had almost used for himself. Saving the pale one for a friend had been the right small choice. The clearing would be there tomorrow, and so, he believed, would the ears in the ferns.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Mateo set his leaf-roll whistle on the sill like a tiny instrument at rest. He left a space beside it, empty on purpose, the way the rock had held a gift. Somewhere under the ferns a young fox was probably sniffing a grass tie and remembering a hop of sound. Mateo drank cool water and let the quiet of his cottage feel friendly instead of hollow. Friendship, he thought, was a second whistle, a pause long enough for someone shy to step out, and a song that sounded better when two very different voices shared it.
