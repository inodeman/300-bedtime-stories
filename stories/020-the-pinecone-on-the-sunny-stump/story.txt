The pinecone was shut tight, a wooden flower that had not heard it was time. Pip held it in both hands at the edge of the clearing. The scales overlapped like shingles, brown and a little sticky with a drop of resin that smelled sharp and clean. Inside, Nana Brook had said, were seeds with papery wings. Pip wanted those seeds for a pot on the step, a small planting, a hope with a rim. The cone did not rattle. Nothing fell out when Pip turned it gently. "If I pull the scales," Pip said, "I might meet the seeds sooner." Nana Brook sat on the sunny stump and patted the place beside her. "Sooner can bend a closed thing. Warmth knows the password."

Pip pictured prying with a careful fingernail and did not like the picture. The scales were not a lid with a knob. They were the cone's own coat. "Then we give it sun?" Pip asked. "We give it sun and a roof of air, and we do other jobs while it thinks," Nana said. They set the cone on the stump, not in a pocket, where darkness and fuss would keep it shut. The stump was warm from the morning. A line of ants detoured, busy with their own errand, and did not bother the cone. Pip washed resin from a thumb with a dab of soap from a tin and a sip of water from the bottle. The thumb smelled like a forest closet, then like lemon.

Waiting needed work, so Pip filled the planting pot with soil from the sack they had carried, crumbly and dark. A worm was not in it. The soil smelled like rain that had been stored. Pip set the pot near the stump, ready, not demanding. "The bed is made," Pip told the cone. "You can arrive when you are dressed for it." Nana smiled. "That is a courteous invitation." They walked a short loop to the creek and back, not to forget the cone, but to let the sun have a private minute with it. Water talked over stones. Pip's shoulders dropped. On the return, the cone looked the same. Pip's hope did not shrink. It learned a longer shape.

They ate apple slices on a cloth. Juice made their fingers tacky in a sweeter way than resin. Pip counted scales to pass the time, lost count, and laughed. "There are enough," Nana said. "Enough is a fine total." A jay shouted once and received no reply, then flew as if that had been the plan. Pip checked the cone again. One scale near the bottom had lifted, a small awning. A pale tip showed beneath. Pip's hands jumped, then landed in Pip's lap. "I will not pull," Pip said. "I will witness." The word felt grand and gentle. Witnessing was a job. It meant staying with the opening without becoming the opener.

Another scale lifted while they watched, then another, as if the cone had heard a music they could not quite hear. A seed slipped out, wing like a scrap of tissue, seed like a grain of warm sand. It did not fall far. Pip cupped it without pinching the wing. "Hello, traveler," Pip whispered. They laid it in a shallow dish Nana had brought for this exact happiness. More seeds followed, not all at once, a few, a rest, a few. The cone was becoming a flower of its own kind, geometric and brown. Pip felt the wait inside their body turn into a smile that did not need to be loud. The pot of soil waited too, a dark mouth ready to say yes.

They planted three seeds, no more, because a pot is not a forest. Pip pressed each one in just under the surface, the way Nana showed, and misted them with water from the bottle until the soil shone. The other seeds they would scatter at the clearing's edge, where a tree might like to try. "Not every seed owes us a sprout," Nana said. "We owe them a fair place." Pip nodded. The cone, opened, stayed on the stump for any last seeds that wanted more sun. Taking it home shut in a bag would have been the old hurry. Leaving it was the lesson, still warm in Pip's hands even after they let go.

They rinsed the dish in the creek with a quick dip and a wipe on the cloth. Pip's fingernails were clean moons again. On the walk toward the cottage the pot rode in a little crate, steady, not jostled. Pip told the three seeds a very short story about a stump and a password made of warmth. Nana added the ending: "And a child who did not pry." Birds crossed the bright gap above the path. The resin smell faded to a memory on the air. Pip's hurry had faded with it. What remained was a pot that clicked softly against its crate, full of a future that would keep its own time.

At the step they set the pot where morning sun would find it and midday shade would rest it. Pip wrote nothing on a stick yet. A label could come when a green thread appeared. Until then, the soil itself was the sign that someone had been patient. Nana brought out two cups of water, plain and cold. They drank. The clearing they had left would hold an open cone for a while, a small sculpture of waiting done well. Pip liked thinking of it there, not lonely, just finished with one job and ready for weather.

Pip turned the pot a quarter turn so every side would know the clearing's light. A winged seed they had not planted spun from the stump on a thread of breeze and landed in the grass, choosing for itself. "That one has its own plan," Pip said, pleased not to chase it. Nana Brook lifted her cup of water. "Plans that belong to seeds are allowed to be mysterious." They listened to the grass tick against the stump. The open cone smelled sweeter now, less like a shut box and more like weather that had finished a good job.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as the day leaned, and Pip looked from that glow to the quiet pot. Patience had been a sunny stump, a lap that kept still, and scales that opened without a single pull. The seeds were tucked in, the cone was free, and the cottage step felt like a good place to practice the long, kind art of letting things unfold.
