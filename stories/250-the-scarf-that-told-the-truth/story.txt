Sana looped red yarn around a wooden needle and felt how the strand was soft, a little fuzzy, and warm already from the basket. Timo sat beside her at the low table, his own needles clicked once and then went quiet because he was watching her first row. Grandma had started the scarf that morning, a neat edge of stitches, and then handed the work to Sana so she could learn the middle. "It is for Grandpa's chair," Sana said. "A wonky scarf can still be a warm one, if I am honest about the wonky." Timo nodded. "Honest is a good stitch. It holds."

The room smelled like wool and the cinnamon toast they had eaten, sweet and toasty in the air. Sana knit across, needle under, yarn around, stitch slipped off, the way Grandma's hands had danced it. Her own hands moved more like careful turtles. The first row was even. The second row gained an extra loop that she did not mean to make. The edge began to wave, a little ruffle where a ruffle had not been invited. Sana saw it. Her cheeks went hot. For a moment she thought she might fold that edge under and say, "It was always like that." The untrue sentence felt scratchy in her mouth, worse than wool.

Timo tilted his head. "The edge is waving at me," he said, not unkind, just noticing. "Did the yarn decide to dance?" Sana looked at the red ripple. She could still hide it. The chair would not tell. But Timo's question sat in the air like a clear window, and she did not want to fog it. "I made an extra loop," she said. "I was going to pretend it was Grandma's pattern. It is not. It is my mistake." Her shoulders dropped after the words. The room felt bigger. Timo smiled. "Thank you for telling me the real story. Now we can fix it or keep it on purpose. Both are honest. Hiding was the only wrong tool."

They chose a small repair. Grandma looked over, listened, and showed Sana how to ease the extra loop back off the needle without yanking the whole row apart. The yarn resisted, then slipped, a soft whisper. Sana undid three stitches herself, slowly, and knit them again. The edge calmed. It was not ruler-straight. It still leaned, a gentle wave, but it was a wave she had told the truth about. "I will leave a little wonky," she said. "If I chase perfect, I might never finish, and Grandpa's knees get cool in the evening." Timo laughed. "A true wonky is friendlier than a fake straight."

They took turns so Timo could learn too. His row was looser, the stitches fat and cozy. Sana's next row was tighter. The scarf began to stripe itself in texture, even though the color stayed red. "It looks like a path through tall grass," Timo said. "Some steps big, some small." The needles clicked, a tiny wooden music. Yarn fuzz stuck to Sana's sweater. She did not mind. When a stitch fell and ran down two rows, she felt the old hot wish to cover it with her hand. Instead she held the work up. "Dropped one," she said clearly. "Help me pick it up?" Together they laddered it back, hook by hook, until the red rung was home.

The scarf grew longer than a forearm, then longer than a lap. They stopped at a length that could wrap a chair arm twice, because that was the job, not a prize for the longest scarf. Sana bound off the way Grandma showed, tugging the tail through the last loop. The ending knot was firm. She sewed no fancy letters on it. She did not need a sign that shouted. Timo rolled the scarf and then unrolled it again so they could see the whole road of stitches. The place of the extra loop was a slight bend. The picked-up stitch was a little tighter. Both places were visible. Both were fine.

They laid the scarf over Grandpa's chair while he was still in the garden. The red looked cheerful against the brown cushion. Sana smoothed it and said, out loud, to the empty room and to Timo, "This is wonky in two places because I am learning. It is warm all over because we finished." Saying it felt like setting down a heavy basket. Grandpa came in, dirt on his cuffs and a smile already starting. He touched the scarf. "It hugs," he said. Sana answered, "It waves a little. I wanted you to know." Grandpa sat, pulled the red wool over his knees, and sighed a happy sigh. "Then it is honest wool. That is the warmest kind."

Sana did not spin in a circle or declare herself a master. She wound the leftover yarn into a tidy ball and put the needles in their case. Timo flicked a bit of fuzz from her sleeve. They shared the last corner of cinnamon toast, the sugar gritty and good. The chair held Grandpa. The scarf held the chair's chill away. Truth had made the making lighter, and the wave in the edge looked, in the end, like a smile the wool had decided to keep.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the red wool and made every fuzzy fiber shine. Sana and Timo watched Grandpa's hand rest on the honest bend in the stitches. The scarf was finished, wonky and warm, and no one had needed to pretend otherwise. Pride was a quiet heat in the wool, enough to share, with nothing to prove.
