Jonah liked the red pencil more than he liked most tools. It lived in a groove on the cottage table, sharp as a berry seed, ready for the tide chart. Every bright morning the lighthouse keeper read the water, and Jonah made a small neat mark so the little wooden boats would know when the cove would be deep and kind. The paper smelled like salt and graphite. A gull sometimes tapped the sill, as if delivering a reminder, then flew off without scolding anyone. Jonah pulled the pencil close. "I will make the line as straight as the horizon," he said.

He pressed. The chart needed a firm dot beside today's gentle rise. Jonah wanted the dot to be perfect, so he leaned his whole hand into it. The pencil's wood gave a tiny chirp. Then it snapped, clean and shocking, into a long piece and a short piece. Red paint flecked the chart like a sprinkle of confetti nobody had planned. Jonah froze. The keeper was outside, rinsing a mug at the outdoor tap. The cottage was quiet enough that the snap seemed loud. Jonah's ears went hot. A perfect morning had cracked in his fingers.

His eyes went to the shell dish where smooth white shells waited like tiny bowls. The broken pieces would fit underneath. He could set a different pencil in the groove and say nothing. The chart's dot was almost done. Maybe no one would notice the red dust. Jonah even lifted the shell dish. It was cool against his palm. Then he pictured the keeper reaching for the red pencil at dusk and finding an empty groove, the worry walking across his face, the boats depending on clear notes. A hidden break would still be a break. It would just be a lonelier one.

"I broke it," Jonah said, before his courage could sit back down. His voice came out smaller than the bell on the path, but it reached the doorway. The keeper stepped in, mug dripping, eyebrows lifted in a question that was not angry yet. Jonah held out both pieces on his open hand. "I pressed too hard because I wanted the dot to be the best dot. I almost put the pieces under the shells. I did not. The red dust on the chart is from the pencil, not from a crayon storm." He breathed. The truth felt bumpy and much better than the story he had nearly built.

The keeper dried his hands and looked at the pencil, then at Jonah, then at the chart. "Thank you for the straight road," he said. "A hidden break would have sent me hunting through drawers while the tide did not wait." He was not pleased about the snap. Jonah could see that. He was pleased about the telling. They sat together. With a small knife kept only for pencil work, the keeper showed Jonah how to shape the longer piece into a shorter pencil that could still write. The shavings curled like pale ribbons. "Tools can have second chapters," the keeper said. "If we are honest about the first."

Jonah swept the shavings into a cup. He erased the wild red flecks carefully so the tide line stayed readable. Then he made the dot again, lighter this time, letting the pencil do the work without a wrestle. The mark was round and clear. "Does it still help the boats?" he asked. "It does," said the keeper. "Helpful does not have to be heavy." They added a tiny note in the margin, not about blame, just about the time of the rise. Jonah's shoulders dropped from around his ears. Outside, the cove glittered, and a wooden boat rocked as if it approved of clear information.

They walked the note down to the dock so Uncle, who was coiling a loose line, could hear the morning's numbers. Jonah told the pencil story himself instead of letting the keeper tell it. Uncle whistled, soft and kind. "I once hid a cracked mug behind the flour tin," he said. "Spent a week drinking from a jar and feeling fuzzy. Truth would have saved me six odd breakfasts." Jonah laughed, surprised. The dock smelled like rope and warm boards. A starfish rested in the pool beside the ladder, untouched by their talk, a calm orange star minding its own wet business. Honesty, Jonah thought, was a kind of cleanliness.

Back at the table, the short red pencil lay in the groove, different and still itself. Jonah sharpened a spare gray pencil too, so tomorrow would have a backup. He did not hide the gray one. He set it in plain sight. "If I break something again, I will say it while the sound is still in the room," he told the keeper. "That is a good tide to catch," the keeper answered. They drank cool water. The chart looked orderly. The shell dish held only shells. Jonah touched the red pencil once, grateful it had a job again, grateful he had not asked it to disappear.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the mended morning and the honest dot. Jonah stood with the keeper and looked out at the boats, each one using the day a little more wisely because the chart was true. The short pencil threw a small shadow, and Jonah did not mind that the shadow was different from yesterday's. He had chosen the bumpy sentence, and the cottage felt wide again. When he left, he carried no secret in his pocket. He carried the light feeling of a mistake that had been told, mended, and set gently back to work.
