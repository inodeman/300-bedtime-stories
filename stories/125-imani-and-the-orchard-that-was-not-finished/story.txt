The orchard at dusk smelled like warm apples and cut grass. Baskets sat in a row, each one full enough to make a person proud. Imani wiped juice from her wrist and looked down the rows. The easy apples were gone. The ladders were folded. Even the wheelbarrow had been rolled to the shed, its wooden squeak already a memory. "Is the orchard finished?" she asked. Grandpa shook his head, though the baskets said otherwise. "Finished picking is not the same as finished looking," he said. Imani was not sure. Her sketchbook page was mostly blank, with one lopsided apple and a lot of white.

She had wanted to draw the whole day, but her hand had been busy carrying fruit. Now the light was sliding sideways and the trees looked like dark lace. A moth with dusty wings rested on a basket handle, breathing in a way she could barely see. Imani leaned close. The wings had tiny windows in the pattern, pale dots like seeds. "Oh," she said, and the moth did not mind her voice. She sat on the low wall and opened the sketchbook again. This time she drew the dots before she drew the apple. The page stopped being empty. It started being a place.

Grandpa walked slowly between the trees, touching a branch here and there as if saying good night. The orchard cat, whose name was Pebble, trotted after a beetle and then forgot the beetle when a leaf moved more interestingly. Imani followed a row she had already walked. Close to the ground, on a bent branch, three small apples still hung, rosy on one cheek and green on the other. They had been hidden by a curtain of leaves. She did not pick them yet. She watched a bead of dew gather and let go, landing on her shoe with a tap she felt more than heard.

"There is still fruit," she called. Grandpa came and nodded. "Low fruit is for low pickers and for people who are willing to bend." Imani bent. The apple came off with a soft twist and a stem that smelled sharp and green. She held it to her ear as a joke and then to her nose for real. It smelled like the cold middle of a cloudy day. She set it in a little cloth bag, not in the proud baskets. This one was for noticing. Pebble sniffed the bag, approved, and sat on Imani's foot like a warm stone.

The grass began to hold small lights. Not big, not loud, just a few patient blinks near the clover. Imani went still so her eyes could learn the pattern. One light, then another, then a pause long enough to wonder if she had imagined them. "Grandpa," she whispered, "the grass is thinking." He chuckled. "Fireflies. They come when the picking noise goes home." Imani did not chase them. She let them write dotted lines in the air. Her pencil tried to follow and made a trail of tiny circles. The blank feeling she had carried was gone. The orchard was busy in a quieter alphabet.

They walked to the oldest tree, the one with bark like cracked maps. Imani pressed her palm to it and felt the day's warmth still stored there. A cricket tested one note. Far off, a porch bell rang for someone else's supper, a round sound that rolled between the rows and faded. Imani added the bell to her page as a small circle with lines, a picture code only she needed to understand. "I thought we had taken everything," she said. Grandpa handed her a sprig of apple leaf. "We took the part for pies. The orchard kept the part for eyes."

On the path home the cloth bag bumped her knee, a gentle reminder. Pebble escorted them to the gate and then sat, which was his way of saying the rows were his evening job. Imani looked back. The baskets were still full, but they were no longer the whole story. Dew, moth windows, low fruit, firefly pauses, and a warm map of bark had been waiting under the work. Her sketchbook rubbed the side of her hand. She could hardly wait to show the dotted wings, though she also liked having seen them first in the real air.

In the kitchen she washed the little apple and set it on a blue plate. She told Grandpa which circle meant the bell and which dots meant wings. He listened as if the code were a fine recipe. Imani's feet were dusty and her hair smelled like leaves. The problem had been a finished feeling, a page too white, a worry that wonder had been picked and carried away in baskets. Looking again had solved it without taking anything the trees were not ready to give.

Imani crouched by a low stump and found a ring of tiny mushrooms, each cap no bigger than a button. She did not pick them. She counted eight and then lost count when a ninth hid under a leaf, which made her laugh instead of frown. Grandpa said hidden things are still part of the orchard. Imani drew a button-cap in the corner of her page and left the real ones in their ring. Pebble sniffed once and chose a warmer stone. The stump smelled like rain that had happened yesterday and stayed in the wood.

On the gatepost a scrap of red cloth fluttered, a marker Grandpa used so pickers could find the path at dusk. Imani touched it lightly and felt the day's heat still caught in the threads. She understood the orchard kept small signals for people who looked twice. A bee lumbered past, heavy and harmless, heading for a late blossom she had missed entirely. She added a tiny wing-dash to her sketch. Wonder, she thought, was a basket you carried inside, and it did not get too full to close.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the blue plate and the open sketchbook. Imani watched the light sit on the rosy cheek of the low apple. She imagined the orchard keeping its secrets kindly, moth and firefly and cricket still at their quiet work. Her pencil rolled and stopped. She touched the page once, the way Grandpa touched branches, and let the day be unfinished in the best way. Sleep came like a slow row between trees, full of small lights.
