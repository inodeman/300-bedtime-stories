Esme lined three small printing blocks on a tray while the kitchen table wore an old sheet to catch drips. One block was a potato half that Papa had shaped into a leaf that morning, so the shaping stayed in his hands. Another was a scrap of foam glued to wood, with a star pressed into it by a blunt pencil. The third was a cork that made a plain dot. Fabio leaned in until his nose almost touched the green paint. "It smells like the art shelf," he said. "A little sharp, a little like rain on a tin watering can." Esme nodded. "I want to know what happens when the block meets the paper."

She rolled green paint over the leaf block with a small roller. The paint made a sticky whisper. When she pressed the block onto cream paper and lifted it, a leaf appeared, bright and a bit ridged, with tiny holes where the paint had not quite reached. "It looks like a real leaf that took a breath," Fabio said. He tried the star. His press was harder. The star came out fat and a little blurry. "Too much hug," Esme said gently. "Try a medium hug." Fabio laughed and tried again. The second star had clean points. They both bent close to see the tiny bubbles drying in the green.

Then Fabio stamped a letter block Papa had made, a simple E for Esme. He stared. "The E is facing the wrong way," he said. "Did I break the alphabet?" Esme did not know. She picked up the block and looked at the raised E. It looked right on the block. On the paper it looked backward. Curiosity tickled her like a feather. "Let us do it slowly and watch." She painted the block again. Before she pressed, she held it above the paper. "The feet of the E point this way on the block." She pressed, lifted, and the feet pointed the other way. Fabio's eyes widened. "The paper is a mirror."

They tested the idea. Fabio held the block in front of a spoon and saw the curve twist the shape. Esme pressed the leaf, then looked at the block again. The leaf's stem was on the left on the wood and on the right on the paper. "So if I want the stem to point toward the star, I have to aim the block the other way first," she said. They tried. It worked. A leaf leaned toward a star as if the star were the sun. The cork dots became berries along a vine they invented as they went. Each print had a soft thud and a little shine that dulled as it dried.

A smudge happened when Esme's sleeve touched a wet leaf. The green went gray at the edge. She felt a pinch of disappointment. "I could throw this sheet away." Fabio shook his head. "Or we could ask the smudge to become a cloud." He dabbed the cork nearby, not covering the mistake so much as keeping it company. The smudge turned into weather above the vine. Esme smiled. "You looked at it until it became something." They printed a second sheet more carefully, sleeves rolled, fingers at the edges. The roller clicked when it reached the end of the tray.

They hung the papers on a string with wooden clips. The air from the window moved the sheets so the leaves seemed to flutter without being real leaves. Paint dotted Esme's thumb and Fabio's knuckle. They did not mind. At the sink the green swirled pale and then gone, leaving their skin smelling like soap. "I still do not know everything about mirrors," Fabio said. "I know one true thing," Esme answered. "A stamp shows its other face. Next time I will check before I press, because checking is part of the making." They ate apple slices while they waited, the fruit crisp and sweet, juice on their clean fingers.

When the prints were dry, they chose the sheet with the cloud-smudge for the kitchen wall and the neater sheet for a present for Papa, who had cut the leaf. Esme wrote, in pencil on the back, a small thank-you. She did not write that they were famous printers. She wrote, "We learned the picture flips." Fabio added, "Medium hugs work best." They taped the thank-you sheet to the pantry door, low enough to see. The leaf, the star, and the dots sat together like a tiny garden that would never need watering.

Esme touched the dry paper. It felt smooth where the sheet was bare and a little stiff where the paint sat. "I want to try a moon next," she said. "We can ask Papa to help shape it, and we can figure out which way it should face." Fabio tapped the cork. "And dots can be stars that did not grow big yet." Their curiosity had not run out. It had simply found a place to rest until the next color, maybe blue, maybe the brown of twigs.

The lantern by the window glowed softly under the drying line, and the printed leaves looked deeper green in that warm light. Esme and Fabio stood quietly, pleased without puffing up. They had asked a question with their hands, and the paper had answered. The blocks rested in a row, paint cleaned away, ready for another curious morning.
