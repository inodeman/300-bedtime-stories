The honey jar on the bakery counter was full of gold that would not move. Yara tipped it over the cinnamon rolls. A heavy lump slid to the mouth of the jar and stopped, as if it had decided to stay home. Sable tried a gentle shake. The lump wobbled and stayed. Lewis held a warm roll in a napkin, ready and patient, while the bread ovens ticked behind them and the Saturday market murmured beyond the door.

"I could scoop it with a strong spoon," Yara said. "If I dig, I might get a chunk."

Sable peered into the glass. "A chunk would sit on the roll like a stone. Honey wants to be a ribbon."

Lewis set the roll down so his hands were free. "At the soup pots we warm things that are shy," he said. "Maybe honey is shy, not stubborn."

They did not bang the jar. They did not dig. Yara filled a bowl with warm water from the kettle that was only warm enough for hands, not hot enough to startle. She set the honey jar in the bowl so the gold stayed above the waterline. The glass fogged a little. A sweet smell grew stronger, like flowers stepping into the room.

"Now we wait," Sable said. "Waiting is the cheerful step that does not look like a step."

Yara watched the lump. It still looked sleepy. Her fingers drummed, and then she folded them. Lewis counted the clinks from the market stalls, one through fifteen. Between counts they talked about orange peels, how the curls on the plate were drying at their own speed too. Nothing in the bakery was truly stuck forever. Some things just liked a softer door.

When the water had hugged the jar for a while, Sable lifted it with a cloth and tipped it again. A thick shine moved, slower than milk and faster than the lump had been. It paused. They waited one more quiet minute. Yara hummed so she would not rush. The hum matched the oven's low note.

The next tip sent a ribbon of honey in a shining line. It curled onto a spoon like a golden thread. Lewis laughed. "It learned to pour."

"We learned to let it," Yara said.

They carried the jar to the shared table, where rolls were stacked in a generous pile and a basket of extra bread sat underneath for anyone who came later. Picnic cloths were folded at the end. Orange peels waited in a cup. They did not drown the rolls. Yara poured a ribbon on one, Sable on the next, Lewis on the next, and then they set the jar in the middle so others could pour their own. The honey kept its new good manners as long as the jar stayed a little warm near the window light.

Yara tasted a bite. The honey sank into the cinnamon crumb and made it taste like a slow afternoon. Sable added a tiny peel on top. The orange woke the sweetness up. Lewis took his time with his piece, letting the ribbon stretch and break on its own.

"I almost dug a hole in the jar," Yara said. "I would have made a mess and a grumpy lump."

Lewis nodded. "Patience turned the lump into a line. Sharing turned the line into everybody's bite."

They wrote a note on a recipe card. If the honey will not pour, give it a warm bath and a little song. Do not dig. Do not bang. When it ribbons, pass the jar. They propped the card against the bowl so the next baker would see the trick before the frustration.

The soup pots sighed under their lids, full and unbothered. The ovens rested. Outside, a stall keeper laughed at something kind. Inside, three friends wiped honey from the table in easy circles. The jar was still more than half full. Plenty had never been the problem. The problem had been speed, and speed had gone home.

Yara capped the jar loosely so it could breathe and close later without sticking. Sable rinsed the bowl. Lewis stacked the napkins. They left two ribboned rolls on a plate, picture-pretty, for the next people through the door.

The ribbon kept its shape even when Lewis drew a little spiral on a roll, a golden snail that made Yara snort a laugh. Sable said the snail could be their mark for patient honey. They made three spirals and left the rest of the rolls plain, because not every bite needed a drawing to be delicious. The jar, still cozy from its bath, poured without lumps. Warm water in the bowl had cooled to a pleasant temperature, and the glass no longer fogged. Yara dried the bottom so the table would not wear a ring. She liked the tidy ending. It matched the tidy ribbon. Outside, a stall bell chimed, and nobody felt pulled to run. The rolls would wait. The friends would wait with them until the last spiral had been admired and then eaten, slowly, with orange peel bright on top.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the warm honey jar. Yara, Sable, and Lewis watched one last ribbon catch the light and fold back into the gold. The rolls waited, cinnamon-sweet and ready, and the morning felt as smooth as honey that had been given time.
