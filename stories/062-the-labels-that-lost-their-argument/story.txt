The Saturday market woke up in clinks and cinnamon. Amira walked between the stalls with a recipe card tucked in her pocket and a picnic basket on her arm. At the honey stall, jars stood in two neat rows. One row wore labels the color of amber. The other wore labels the color of orange peels. Lewis was lining the jars so their glass caught the sun. Otto was tying a little string around a stack of blank cards in case a neighbor wanted to write a note.

Amira leaned close. The amber jars smelled deep and floral, like a warm meadow. The orange-labeled jars smelled sharp and sunny, like peel just twisted over a bowl. She frowned in a thoughtful way, not a cross way. "Lewis," she said, "my nose and the labels are having an argument."

Lewis blinked. "An argument?"

"This jar says orange peel syrup," Amira said, tapping the bright label. "But it smells like honey from the bakery shelf. And this one says honey, yet it smells like the peel the bakery zests for glaze." She did not taste from the selling jars. Otto had set out two tiny sample spoons on a saucer, with plenty left in every jar behind them. Amira waited her turn, then tried a dot of each. The tastes agreed with her nose.

Otto's eyebrows rose. "We labeled them before the sun was up," he said. "The light was gray. I may have crossed the cards."

For a moment Amira looked at the brighter orange labels. They were prettier, and a line of neighbors was already drifting toward the stall with baskets. It would be easy to say nothing and let the pretty cards stay. Her cheeks felt warm. She put her hand on the recipe card in her pocket, the one that said honey in one step and orange peel in another. Those steps only worked if the jars told the truth.

"I think we should fix them," she said. "If someone wants honey on bread and gets syrup, the taste will be a surprise they did not choose. Surprises are nicer when they are honest."

Lewis nodded at once. "You are right. I was hurrying. Hurry mixed the words."

They did not peel the labels off in a rush. Otto brought a bowl of warm water and a soft cloth, the same gentle trick the bakery used when a honey lid felt stubborn. They loosened one label at a time. The wet paper came away in slow curls. Amira read each jar with her nose, then with a clean sample spoon, and only then did she choose the new card. Honey jars got honey words. Orange peel syrup got orange words. Lewis wrote in clear letters. Otto blotted each card until it was dry enough to stick.

A neighbor paused with an empty spot in a picnic basket. "Is the honey ready?" she asked.

"Almost," Amira said. "We are making the labels true. It will take a few patient minutes. There is plenty, and it will taste like itself."

They kept a small plate of plain bakery bread at the side of the stall so nobody had to wonder while they waited. The bread was soft. The cinnamon from the bakery next door drifted over in little waves. When the last jar wore the right label, Lewis held two spoons up to the light.

"Honey for the golden card," he said. "Orange for the bright card. Thank you for telling us, Amira."

Amira felt the warm feeling in her cheeks change into a glad one. "I almost liked the pretty mix-up," she admitted. "But the truth tastes better. It matches the recipe."

They carried a honey jar and a syrup jar to the shared table beside the bakery window. Otto sliced bread into generous pieces. Nobody scraped the bottom of anything. The pots of soup inside still sent up a friendly steam, and the bread ovens glowed behind the glass. Amira spread honey on one half of a slice and a thin shine of orange syrup on the other half. She took a bite of honey first. It was round and floral. Then the orange side sparkled on her tongue.

"Same bread, two true flavors," Otto said. "That is a good market morning."

Lewis laughed. "Next time I will label jars after the sun is up, and I will smell before I stick."

Amira wrote a tiny note on her recipe card so she would remember. Smell, taste, then name. She set the card where the others could see it. The stall looked calmer with honest jars, like a song sung in the right key. People chose what they wanted, and every choice was a real one.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the shared plate of bread. Amira, Lewis, and Otto sat with sticky spoons and true labels drying in a row. Honey gleamed in one jar. Orange syrup gleamed in the other. The market moved on around them, full of baskets and cinnamon air, and the small mistake had become a clear, sweet thing they could share without confusion.
