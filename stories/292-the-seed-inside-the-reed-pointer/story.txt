Jaleel sat on a tea-colored cushion on the book barge while the reader held a hollow reed pointer, pale gold and light, the kind that slides under words so children can follow. The story was about a hill orchard and a clay saucer that caught rain for a young tree. The reed should have glided. Instead it caught on every line and made a tiny scratch sound. "It feels heavy at the tip," the reader said. "It never used to." The page waited. The children waited. Jaleel leaned closer, curious, not grabbing.

"May I hold it?" he asked. The reader passed it over. The reed was warm from a hand, and yes, one end felt fuller, as if it had eaten a crumb. Jaleel turned it toward the window. Light went in and did not come out the far side. "Something is inside," he said. "A seed, I think. I can see a dark oval." He did not poke with a sharp thing. Sharp things and paper were not friends, and neither were sharp things and eyes. "How can we invite it out?" he asked.

The reader brought a thin paintbrush with soft bristles, used only for dusting pages. "Try a gentle nudge from the wide end toward the tip," the reader said. Jaleel held the reed over a small dish, not over the book. He brushed. The seed did not move. He turned the reed and listened, the way someone listens to a shell, and heard a dry tick when he tipped it. "It is loose if I tip slowly," he said. He tipped. A sunflower seed, striped and whole, slid into the dish with a sound like a tiny heel tap. The children made a quiet oh, the pleased kind.

Jaleel lifted the reed again. Light now traveled through. He passed it back. The reader set the pointer under the first line, and it glided, no scratch, no snag. "The sentence can walk," the reader said. Jaleel grinned. "The seed was a passenger who missed the stop." They read the orchard page properly, each child watching the reed so no one got lost. Jaleel felt the good itch of curiosity settled into an answer. The odd weight had been a question. He had asked it instead of forcing the page.

"What should we do with the seed?" he asked, because leaving it in the dish felt unfinished. The reader had a small cup of soil on the windowsill, meant for herbs. "We can plant it and see if the barge can grow a sunflower, with water and patience," the reader said. Jaleel pressed the seed in with one finger, not deep, and misted the soil. "If it sprouts, it will be a story that started as a problem," he said. The reader nodded. "Many good things start when someone wonders why a tool feels wrong."

They finished the reading. The reed pointer lay in its groove, light again, ready for the next page. Jaleel looked around the barge with new attention. A bell rope, a fish-shaped clip, a cushion thread: each might have a small secret, not scary, just unnoticed. He did not go hunting for trouble. He only promised himself to ask when something felt heavier than it should. Curiosity, he thought, was a polite knock.

The river tapped the hull. A slow ferry passed, and a red scarf showed at its bell, bright and clear. Jaleel waved. He told the nearest friend, "If a pointer scratches, look inside before you blame the page." The friend nodded as if storing the sentence in a pocket. The planted seed sat in its cup, a brown comma in dark soil, asking for days, not minutes. Jaleel could wait for that part. Finding it had been the day's work.

He helped stack the cushions without burying the dish or the cup. The book stayed open one extra minute so everyone could see the last line clearly, the reed resting like a thin golden underline. Jaleel touched the rim of the seed cup. "Grow if you like," he said. "You do not have to. But you do not have to live in a pointer either." The reader smiled and wrote the date on a scrap so they would remember when the seed had been found.

Jaleel held the hollow reed up to the window one last time and watched the light go all the way through, bright as a thought that has room. "It is a pointer again, not a pocket," he said. A younger listener asked to see the dish where the seed had landed, and Jaleel showed the empty dish and then the cup of soil. "We did not throw the seed away," he said. "We gave it a better job." The listener nodded, curious in the same quiet way, and promised to tell the reader if another tool felt heavier than it looked.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the cup of soil, warming the place where the seed now had room. Jaleel watched the hollow reed, clear at last, in its groove. Curiosity about a heavy tip had freed the story for every listener. He stepped onto the landing with his grown-up, already wondering, kindly, what else a small sound might explain.
