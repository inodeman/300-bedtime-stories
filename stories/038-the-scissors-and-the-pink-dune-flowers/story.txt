Amina climbed the dune with the round-tipped scissors ticking in her pocket. They were small, safe, and very ready. Nana had said the cottage table looked plain, and Amina had seen the answer at once, a cloud of pink flowers nodding above the grass, each blossom no bigger than a fingernail. She could already imagine a jar of them by the bread. The friendly lighthouse shone off to the side. Below, little wooden boats rested, and a picnic blanket made a square of color on the sand. The dune itself smelled like warm green stems and salt. "Just a few," Amina promised the hillside. "The prettiest few."

She knelt. Up close the flowers were even better, pink with a pale heart, stems slightly fuzzy. She opened the scissors. They made a clean eager sound. Then a bee arrived, gold and serious, and slid into a blossom as if it were a tiny room with work inside. Another bee followed, and a small butterfly the color of sand when sand is almost white. Amina held still. The scissors stayed open and suddenly looked too loud. The bee's legs were dusty with yellow. It was not visiting for decoration. It was grocery shopping for its whole busy day. Amina's cutting picture wobbled.

"If I take the prettiest, I take the pantry," she said softly. Nana was down on the blanket, not hearing, which meant Amina could decide without a performance. She thought of the table, plain wood, perfectly fine with pears on it. She thought of the dune tomorrow if every walker took a few. A few plus a few plus a few could become none. The butterfly opened its wings and showed her the flower again, like a teacher who did not need words. Amina closed the scissors. The click sounded like a choice. "You can keep your rooms," she told the bees. "I will borrow the look another way."

She sat all the way down, took the pencil and folded paper she used for tide notes, and began to draw. The first flower was too fat. The second had a stem like a stick bug. The third caught the pale heart. Amina smiled. Drawing was slower than cutting, and the slowness let her see a curl in a petal she would have snipped past. A bee walked across the page's shadow and off into real pollen. She did not wave it away. She waited, then drew the blossom it had preferred, a slightly darker pink. Gulls turned in the high air, helpful weather vanes, not interested in flowers, and their cries were thin and kind.

Nana came up the path with two cups of water and paused when she saw the closed scissors. "No bouquet?" she asked, not disappointed, only curious. Amina showed the page. "The bees got there first, which means they needed it first. I am making a paper bouquet that does not wilt and does not rob anybody." Nana sat, careful of the stems. "That is a good reading of the dune." They drank. The water tasted like the metal cup and a little like the sky. Amina added a butterfly that was mostly two triangles and a brave dot. "Nature is not a shop," she said, testing the sentence. It fit. "It is a place we get to visit."

She drew six flowers, one for each person who might sit at the table that week, and then stopped so the page would not become a crowd. Stopping was part of respecting a living hillside. She noticed a low plant that was not in bloom and left it alone too. Not everything had to become hers by being interesting. On the way down she kept the scissors latched and the paper flat between her palms. A gull on the path walked ahead, then lifted away in a bright flap, free of any plan to be useful or ornamental. Amina liked it for being exactly a gull.

At the blanket she propped the drawing against the basket. From a step back it really did look like a small bunch, pink hearts repeating. Uncle, arriving with dry towels, leaned in. "I can smell them," he joked, and Amina laughed. "You can if you go up the dune and sniff the real ones." They did, later, noses only, hands behind backs, the way one visits a tide pool without scooping the starfish. The flowers smelled faintly sweet and green. A bee objected to nobody. It simply continued. Amina felt the urge to pick fade into a nicer feeling, a feeling like being trusted by a place.

Back at the cottage she set the paper bouquet in a clip on the wall where the plain table could see it. The scissors went into their tin, still shiny, saved for string and paper and not for the pantry of the bees. Amina told Nana she might draw the pools next, including a starfish that would stay wet and wild. "Good," Nana said. "Then our home can remember the shore without emptying it." Amina washed pink pollen off her wrist that had brushed a stem by accident. Even that little dust felt like something to rinse back toward the world, not to keep.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beneath the paper flowers, warming their pencil pinks into something almost alive. Amina stood with Nana and looked from the drawing to the dark shape of the dune outside. The real blossoms were still out there, feeding whoever needed them, nodding at a sea they did not have to leave. She touched the closed scissors once and felt no regret. Nature had shared its color in the way it preferred, by staying put. Amina ate bread at the plain wooden table and found it beautiful enough, with a paper bouquet watching, gentle and full, from the glow.
