Chiara arrived at Deepak’s kitchen with a paper of apple slices and a rhythm in her hands. At her home, the family clapped a pattern while the soup stirred. Clap, clap, rest, clap. Deepak’s family had a different hello, a wooden spoon tap on a pot that sounded like rain deciding to be polite. They stood by the table and demonstrated at the same time. The clap and the tap collided. Both children stopped. Chiara felt shy, a new-friend kind of shy. Deepak felt the same. “I wanted you to like my kitchen’s sound,” he said. “I feel wobbly about it.”

Chiara set the apples down so her hands could be honest. “I feel wobbly too. I do not want my clap to erase your tap. And I do not want to hide my clap, because it is how my people say the soup is loved.” Deepak nodded hard. Friendship, he thought, might be big enough for two rhythms. “Teach me slow,” he said. “Then I will teach you slow. Then we can see if they can sit in the same song without bumping.” Chiara’s shy feeling loosened. A plan was a friendly chair. She smiled. “Slow is my favorite speed for new things.”

She clapped the pattern once, with space between the sounds. Deepak watched her hands the way he watched basil leaves, carefully. He tried. His rest was too short. The pattern tripped. He did not frown at himself for long. “Again?” he asked. Chiara said, “Again,” with no sigh in it. On the third try his rest landed. They clapped together, soft enough for a kitchen that also held a sleeping cat on a chair, a cat who opened one eye and approved. Chiara felt the warm click of being understood. Deepak felt proud and also ready to share his own sound, not instead, but beside.

He handed her a wooden spoon and a small pot. “Rain tap,” he said. “Two tiny taps and a quiet.” Tap tap, quiet. Chiara’s first try was a bit loud. The cat’s ear twitched. She whispered, “Sorry, song,” and tried again from the wrist, not the elbow. The sound became a sprinkle. Deepak joined her. Their spoons did not crash. They sounded like two gutters after a kind cloud. Chiara laughed. “Your kitchen talks in weather,” she said. “Yours talks in steady claps,” Deepak answered. “Like a calm walk home.” They both looked pleased with the descriptions. Friendship was growing a language.

Now came the mixing. They drew a map on paper. A hand shape meant clap. A spoon shape meant tap. They built a line that went clap, clap, rest, tap tap, quiet. It was longer than either home song and somehow familiar to both. They practiced until the map was in their bodies. When Chiara forgot a tap, Deepak pointed at the spoon drawing without taking her spoon. When Deepak rushed a rest, Chiara hummed the space. Nobody called anyone wrong-home. The new song was a third home, made of both. Deepak felt a big feeling and named it. “I feel friendly,” he said. “Like my kitchen grew a window toward yours.” Chiara said, “I feel the same, and a little brave.”

They played the new pattern for Grandma Elena, who was slicing bread and did not mind being an audience. She clapped on the clap parts and nodded on the taps, a perfect guest. “That is a song from two kitchens,” she said. “I would like it on Sunday.” Chiara and Deepak bowed, small bows, spoons held like flowers. They taught Grandma Elena the quiet, because quiets are easy to skip and important to keep. She learned it. The cat stayed asleep, which they decided was a fine review. Apples were shared, one slice at a time, in time with a slow clap, which made the snack part of the friendship too.

Before Chiara went home, they copied the map twice. One copy stayed in Deepak’s cupboard. One copy went in Chiara’s pocket, a little creased and very wanted. “If I play this at my stove, you play it at yours on Sunday,” Chiara said. “We will not hear each other, and we will still be in the same song.” Deepak touched the paper as if it were a handshake. “Friends can match from two towns, or from two ends of a table,” he said. They tried the pattern one last time by the door. It fit. The wobbly feeling had become a rhythm they could carry. They clapped the goodbye at the door, clap, clap, rest, and Deepak answered with two finger taps on the frame, tap tap, quiet. It was their song in miniature, small enough for a hallway, big enough to mean come back. Chiara whispered, “Friends,” and Deepak whispered, “Same song.” The cat, still a judge of gentleness, did not even open an eye.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the two spoons they had set side by side to dry. Chiara and Deepak watched the light, then looked at each other and nodded the start of their song without making a sound. Friendship had made a third music, gentle and shared, and the kitchen seemed to glow in time with it.
