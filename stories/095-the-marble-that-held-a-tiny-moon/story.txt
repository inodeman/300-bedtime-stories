Bibi held a clear glass marble up to the window lamp, and a tiny lamp appeared inside it, curved and bright. She turned the marble toward the balcony door. A tiny moon appeared too, small as a seed of light. "There is a room in this glass," she said. Her voice was full of wonder, not worry. Curiosity tickled her like a feather. Bedtime slippers warmed her feet. Hot milk sat in a cup she had not sipped yet because looking was too interesting. Crickets sang in the garden. The quilt waited, patient as a folded cloud.

Mama sat beside her. "Glass can bend light," Mama said. "It gathers a picture and tucks it into a round place. A raindrop can do a cousin of that trick. Nothing is trapped. The moon is still in the sky." Bibi checked. The real moon was still above the rail, whole and calm. The marble moon was only an echo. She liked echoes. She rolled the marble gently from palm to palm and watched the tiny moon travel with it. "If I look closer, will I find more?" she asked. Mama nodded. "Closer is a good question's favorite road."

They tried a drop of water on a square of waxed paper. Bibi leaned until her nose almost touched it. Inside the drop she saw a bright point, the lamp made tiny. She did not poke the drop and lose it. She watched it shiver when Mama's gentle breath moved past. Then it grew still again. "The little picture comes back," Bibi said. "Looking did not break it." She drew the marble, the drop, and the moon in a notebook, three circles in a row, each with a spark.

Next they used the cardboard telescope, which was a different kind of looking. The tube did not make the moon tiny. It framed it. Bibi compared the two tools. "The marble keeps a souvenir. The tube points at the real thing." Mama said both were ways of paying attention. They found the Big Dipper and Bibi tried, just for fun, to catch one star in the marble. The marble held only a general twinkle, and she decided that was honest. Not every tool does every job. Curiosity included finding the edges of a trick.

Bibi pressed a glow-in-the-dark sticker onto a paper circle and set the marble on it like a pearl on a cushion. When they dimmed the lamp a little, the sticker glowed and the marble held a green seed. She clapped softly. "A new echo." She did not keep the discovery a secret. She called Mama over to see. Sharing a question's answer made the answer bigger. They hung a paper star in the window and used the marble to hold that star too. Paper, glass, and sky stacked up in one small ball, and Bibi laughed at the crowded wonder.

She finally sipped the hot milk, and even the milk gave her a question. Tiny bubbles sat on the surface like far-off stars. "Are they constellations?" she asked. Mama said they were warm air saying hello, not stars, and that it was clever to notice. Bibi drew three bubbles in the notebook and labeled them milk stars so she would remember the joke and the truth. The crickets seemed louder now that she was listening with her curious ears. She opened the balcony door and tried the marble on a mint leaf's shine. A little green light rolled inside the glass.

Before bed she made a looking kit: marble in a tin, notebook, and the cardboard tube side by side. She did not need to solve the whole sky. She needed to keep a path for questions. Mama wrote on the tin, in simple letters, Look kindly. Bibi traced the letters with her finger. Kind looking meant not snatching bugs, not tearing leaves, not claiming the moon. It meant letting pictures come and thanking them. She put her slippers under the bed and the tin on the shelf where morning Bibi would find it.

The room felt larger than it had before the marble. Ordinary things had opened small doors. Bibi touched the quilt, the cup, the paper star, and the cool glass once each, a checklist of wonder. Outside, the garden rested in silver. She yawned a curious yawn, if such a thing exists, and decided it did.

Bibi took the marble onto the balcony and held it near a mint leaf dotted with water. Each tiny drop held a spark, and the marble held the leaf, and the leaf held the night's cool smell. She whispered, "Questions can stack." Mama warned her kindly not to shake the pot and startle the cricket, so Bibi stayed still and let the singer finish a phrase. Then she drew a quick map in her notebook: door, pot, moon, marble. The map was messy and dear. Curiosity had given her a way to walk through the evening twice, once with her eyes and once with her pencil. She closed the tin softly, already wondering what the marble would keep of tomorrow's breakfast light.

The lantern by the window glowed softly through the round marble, making one more tiny lantern inside the glass. Bibi slid under the quilt with her questions tucked in like extra pillows. The crickets carried new puzzles across the night, and she was glad the world repaid a careful look.
