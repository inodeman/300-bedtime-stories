Rohan held one end of the moon-patterned tape while the cardboard tube tried to roll off the table. "It will not stay," he said, laughing. Auntie caught the tube with her palm. "Then it is a two-person telescope," she said. "Some round things need a friend before they can be useful." The window lamp threw gold onto their hands. Hot milk waited in a pan. Crickets sang beyond the balcony door. A quilt was piled on the chair like a cloud that had come indoors to help.

They were making the paper ring that would keep the tube's mouth from going flat. Rohan had cut the strip, a little wiggly. Auntie had drawn tiny moons along it earlier, so the tape and the ring would match. Now the strip wanted to spring open. Rohan pressed one end to the cardboard. Auntie turned the tube. Rohan pressed the next finger-width. If either of them let go too soon, the ring popped loose and they had to giggle and begin again. Teamwork, Rohan decided, sounded like tape making a soft little seal.

"Your turn to say when it is smooth," Auntie said. Rohan ran his finger along the seam. One bump remained. "Not yet. Can you hold here while I press?" Auntie held. Rohan pressed. The bump gave up. "Now," he said. They both waited three breaths before letting go, because they had learned that patience belonged inside teamwork too. The ring stayed. Rohan cheered in a whisper so the night would stay gentle. Auntie tied off the tape with a neat tab.

Next they chose a viewing spot. The balcony was safe and a bit cool, so Rohan carried the slippers that Auntie had left by the door, and Auntie carried the tube. Neither job was the whole job. On the boards they took turns being the holder and the looker. Rohan rested the tube on the rail. Auntie aimed it toward the moon, then stepped aside so Rohan could see the bright circle. "Your eyes get the first real look," she said. "You did the wiggly cutting." Rohan saw the moon's soft gray marks and grinned into the cardboard smell.

They wanted a constellation too. Auntie knew the Big Dipper. Rohan knew how to keep the tube from wobbling. She named the stars. He tracked them, moving only a little when she said, "Handle, now bowl." Alone, the tube would have drifted. Together, the spoon of stars sat still inside the round view. Rohan felt the good click of a plan that needed both people. "We make a better telescope than either of our hands," he said. Auntie answered, "That is the nicest review a tube could hope for."

Inside, they poured hot milk, Rohan tipping the pan while Auntie steadied the cups. A drop ran toward the table and Rohan caught it with a cloth before it traveled. "Good catch," Auntie said. "That was teamwork with a drip." They sat under the quilt and stuck one glow-in-the-dark sticker inside the tube, but only after both agreed on the spot. Rohan wanted the left. Auntie liked the right. They chose the top, a third place that belonged to the idea rather than the argument. The sticker would drink lamplight and greet the next looker.

Rohan hung a paper star from the tube's string handle so their tool would look like part of the window's decorations. Auntie handed him the pin. He handed her the star. The exchange was so easy they both noticed it and smiled. Outside, the crickets seemed to chirp in pairs. Rohan listened and thought the garden understood teams. He wiped the table, and Auntie folded the tape roll closed so it would not stick to the quilt in the night.

When the cups were rinsed, Rohan set the finished telescope in a basket by the balcony door, handle up, ready for a shared look tomorrow. He did not hide it in his room. A thing made by two people should stay where both could find it. His slippers waited, warm from the rug. He felt proud in a wide way, proud of Auntie's fingers and his own, proud of the bump that had given up when they pressed together.

They carried the night picnic of milk and quiet out only as far as the balcony chairs, close to the door. Rohan spread a corner of the quilt over both their knees because the boards were cool. "I will hold the tube if a breeze comes," he said. A small breeze did come, smelling like wet mint, and the tube stayed steady in his lap while Auntie sipped. Then they traded. The trade took no speech, only a look and two hands ready. Rohan noticed that teamwork could be almost silent when people had practiced the noisy part first. He stuck a paper star to the chair back so they would remember this was their building place. When a cricket started up, they both said "There" at the same time and broke into soft laughter that fogged the night air for a second and then was gone.

The lantern by the window glowed softly along the moon-patterned tape and the paper ring. The cardboard tube rested in its basket like a quiet teammate. Rohan slid under the quilt, already wondering which star they would visit next, and the crickets kept time for a song that needed more than one voice.
