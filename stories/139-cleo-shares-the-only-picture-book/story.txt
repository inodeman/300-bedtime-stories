The treehouse nook held one picture book on a shelf between a pinecone and a smooth jar of pencils. Cleo had climbed up early with Uncle's help and claimed the plum cushion fairly, by arriving first and saving space rather than by blocking the door. The book was about a baker who made bread in the shapes of animals. Cleo was on the page with a bread swan when two visitors appeared at the hatch, cousins from the next street, cheeks bright from the ladder. "We heard there is a book," said the taller one. "We hoped for the page with the bread fish," said the shorter one. There was only one book. There were three children.

Cleo felt the page under her thumb. She could keep reading silently and let them look at the pinecone. The nook was small, and silent reading was a good habit at home. Here the hope on their faces was a second kind of page. Uncle's voice drifted up from the grass. "Share the shade if the shade is big enough." The shade was big enough if Cleo made it so. She shifted on the plum cushion. "We can all see if I read out loud and hold the book open," she said. "Fish page and swan page both. We will go in order so nobody's animal gets skipped." The visitors climbed in and sat on the pear cushions, knees careful of the pencils.

Cleo turned back to the beginning, even though she had wanted the swan. Starting over was the sharing. She read in a clear voice about flour like snow and a baker who hummed. She turned the pages slowly and tilted the book so the pictures faced the room. The bread mouse made them smile. The bread turtle made the shorter cousin trace a shell in the air. When they reached the fish, Cleo paused so the wish could land. "Its scales are sesame," she said. The taller cousin sighed happily. "That is the one I hoped for. Thank you for not hiding it in your lap." Cleo felt the thank-you sit warmly beside her earlier wish to read alone.

They reached the swan, and Cleo let herself enjoy it too. The neck was a braid of dough. She described it for anyone sitting at a sharp angle. Uncle passed up a bottle of water in the string basket, and they each took a small sip without dripping on the pages. The treehouse smelled like wood and, now, like imagined bread. A jay called outside and was not invited in, which was best for books. Cleo's voice grew easier. Sharing the story did not shrink it. It made the animals bigger, because three people were seeing them and adding their own quiet thoughts.

When the baker in the book went to sleep beside a bread cat, Cleo closed the covers with both hands. "Again the fish?" the shorter cousin asked, not grabbing. Cleo nodded. "Once more, then the shelf gets the book back so the nook stays fair for tomorrow." They revisited the sesame scales and the swan neck. Then Cleo stood and slid the book home between pinecone and pencils. The taller cousin said, "You were the baker. You portioned the pages." Cleo laughed. "I was the reader. The book was the bakery." They tidied crumbs of bark from the cushions. Nobody left with a private page, and nobody left hungry for the story.

Uncle asked from below if the hatch was clear. They climbed down one at a time, Cleo last, so she could look once more at the shelf and feel glad the book was resting where the next visitor could find it. On the grass they told Uncle the sesame secret. He pretended great surprise. The problem had been one book and two extra hopes, plus Cleo's own hope to keep the swan private. Reading aloud had braided the hopes like dough. The ladder rungs were warm. The afternoon felt wide.

At home Cleo drew a bread fish with sesame dots and a bread swan with a braided neck, side by side, not one hidden behind the other. She told Mama about portioning pages. Mama said a voice can be a plate big enough for a whole nook. Cleo liked that picture. She set her drawing on the desk and did not fold it. Some shares should stay open. Her throat was a little tired in a satisfied way, the way feet are tired after a good walk. She drank water and tasted the memory of the treehouse sip.

She thought about tomorrow and whether she would want the book alone again. Maybe for a few pages, and then she would listen for the hatch. Wanting alone time was not unkind. Offering the story when friends arrived was the kindness she had chosen today. Both could live in the same week. The pinecone on the shelf, she imagined, was keeping the place between visits. Cleo yawned a baker's yawn, full of imaginary flour-snow and happy animals.

Cleo washed pencil dust from her fingers and watched it cloud the water, then clear. She thought of sesame seeds and how small things, lined up, become scales. The house clock tapped. She did not need another story tonight. One bakery of bread animals was a full meal for her imagination. She placed a pinecone from the yard, a cousin of the shelf pinecone, beside her drawing so the treehouse and the desk could match. Uncle would smile at that in the morning. For now the match was her secret treat, quiet as a closed book.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the drawing of the fish and the swan. Cleo watched the light pick out the sesame dots she had penciled. Somewhere the real book sat in the oak, closed and waiting, fair for anyone who would climb. She was glad she had tilted the pages outward. Sharing had made one story into a room. She let the treehouse hush come indoors, and she slept as lightly as flour on a table.
