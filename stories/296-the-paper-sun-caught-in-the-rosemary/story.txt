Nassim stood in the plaza with a small crowd of children and grown-ups, all looking up at the clock tower in a friendly way. At noon the keeper lowered a basket on a cotton string. Inside were folded paper suns, golden and light, one for each chime, so the children could lift a sun as they counted and nobody had to shout the numbers. Nassim liked the ritual. It made the hour feel like a shared handful of light. The basket came down. He counted the suns with his eyes. "There are only eleven," he said. "Noon needs twelve."

His grown-up looked too. "One is missing." Nassim stepped back to see the tower's window box, where rosemary grew in a green hush. A golden point was stuck among the needles, a paper sun caught by a sprig, fluttering but not falling. "It is there," he said. "It did not get left behind on purpose. The plant is holding it." He did not throw anything up. He did not call the tower unkind. He walked to the door and knocked the polite knock the keeper had taught the plaza.

The keeper opened, already smiling, then saw Nassim's serious face. "The basket is short one sun," Nassim said. "It is caught in the rosemary. I can see it from the tiles. I did not want the count to start without it, and I did not want to poke the plant." The keeper's smile changed into thanks. "That is a kind notice. Come to the lower room. I will free the sun from the inside window, gently, and you can fold a spare in case the first one is creased." Nassim liked having a job that was not watching only.

In the bright lower room the keeper reached the window box without any hurry and eased the paper sun off the sprig. A tiny tear showed on one ray, not a ruin, just a mark. "It can still be a sun," the keeper said, "and we will add a spare so the dozen is sure." Nassim took a square of golden paper. The keeper showed the folds: corner to corner, then a small fan, then a ray pinched with care. Nassim's first fan was bulky. "May I try again?" he asked. He unfolded, smoothed, and folded more slowly. The second sun sat flat and bright. "This one can be number twelve," he said.

They carried both suns to the plaza, the rescued one and the new one. The keeper held up a hand so the count had not started. "We have a full dozen," the keeper said. "Nassim saw the one the rosemary borrowed." The children made room in the basket. Nassim placed the new sun on top and the creased one beside it, so neither was hidden. When the chime began, kind and clear, each child lifted a sun in turn. Nassim lifted the spare on the twelfth note. The sound and the paper matched. Nobody was left holding an empty hand.

Afterward a smaller child asked to see the crease. Nassim showed it. "It still counts," he said. "And the rosemary did not mean to keep it. Plants catch what the breeze offers." He felt glad he had spoken. If he had stayed quiet, the count would have ended at eleven and felt like a missing chair at a table. Speaking up had made room for the last number. His grown-up squeezed his shoulder. "You noticed a small absence. That is a real kindness."

The keeper tucked the cotton string into a neat coil. "Tomorrow, look up before you look in the basket," the keeper said. "You have good eyes for strays." Nassim said he would. He also thought he would keep a spare square of gold paper in his pocket, not to show off, but to be ready if another sun needed a twin. Readiness was a kind of care. The rosemary moved in a mild breeze, empty of paper now, smelling sharp and clean.

Nassim remained until the suns were counted back into the basket, twelve again, none forgotten on a bench. He liked endings that matched beginnings. The plaza fountain glittered. People went toward lunch and the bridge and the barge. The hour felt whole. He folded his hands and then unfolded them, still remembering the paper's path: caught, freed, and joined by a spare he had made.

Nassim showed his grown-up the fold one more time on a plain scrap, not gold, so the real suns could stay neat. "Corner to corner, then a small fan," he said. His grown-up followed and made a pale practice sun. They did not add it to the basket. Practice was allowed to be practice. Nassim tucked the scrap into his pocket beside the thought of a spare gold square. Next noon, if the rosemary borrowed another ray, the plaza would still have twelve.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the tower's lower window, near the rosemary that had given up its paper sun. Nassim looked at that glow and at his folded spare, now back in the basket. Telling the keeper about the caught sun, and making another with patient folds, had let the whole plaza count to twelve. He walked on with his grown-up, the kind chime still round and complete in his ears.
