Bima stood under the lowest peach tree on the hill orchard, where the leaves made a green shade and the fruit smelled like sunshine kept in a jar. A tall basket waited on the grass. Its wide leather strap was the color of honey and soft at the edges from many careful shoulders. The orchard keeper, a grown-up with a hat full of air holes, smiled. "Today you may carry the near row, if you want to try," the keeper said. Bima wanted to. He also felt a small wobble in his knees that was not from the hill. "The strap looks wider than my chest," he said.

"It is wide so the weight can spread," said the keeper. "We will not fill it high. Courage here is not a big lift. It is a short walk with company." Bima slipped the strap over his shoulder. It rested like a friendly arm, but when he stood, the empty basket still seemed tall enough to tip. He imagined peaches rolling, bumping, making a mess he would have to explain. His mouth went dry. "I think it will slide," he said, honestly, not in a wail. The keeper nodded as if that sentence was useful. "Then we will walk to the first tree and stop. You can set it down whenever you like."

They picked only the peaches that came off with a gentle turn, ripe and rosy, warm from the day. Bima placed one in the basket, then another, nestled in a cloth so they would not knock. He counted under his breath. At four peaches he paused. The strap had not slid. The basket had not tipped. Still, the path down the hill looked longer than a story. "My feet want to hurry so it will be over," he said. "Hurrying is how fruit bumps," said the keeper. "Let us try again from here, slower than the ferry."

Bima took a breath that filled him to his ribs. He stepped. The strap held. He stepped again, heel then toe, the way the keeper showed, so the basket stayed quiet. A bee visited a blossom nearby and did not come to the basket. Birds talked in the upper leaves and stayed there. Bima's knees still knew the wobble, but his feet knew the path. "I am doing it," he said, surprised and pleased in the same small voice. "You are," said the keeper, walking on the river side so the hill side was Bima's alone to manage. "Four peaches is a real carry."

Halfway, the strap crept a thumb's width toward his neck. Bima stopped without being asked. "It moved," he said. "May we fix it before it becomes a problem?" The keeper helped him lift the strap back to the flat of his shoulder and tightened the buckle one polite notch. "That was brave too," the keeper said. "Stopping is part of trying." Bima felt the praise land softly, not like a prize he had to hold up, but like shade. He walked the rest of the row. The peaches stayed nested. His hands stayed free to balance, not to clutch.

At the orchard table they set the basket down together. The fruit glowed in the cloth. Bima rolled his shoulder and smiled with his whole face, then made the smile smaller so he would not startle the quiet trees. "I thought brave meant not feeling the wobble," he said. "Brave can mean walking with the wobble and a grown-up and a strap in the right place," said the keeper. They ate no peaches yet. Those were for the plaza basket later. Bima drank cool water and watched the hill shine.

He looked back at the lowest branch, lighter now by four fruits, and at the path that had seemed long. It was only a path. He touched the honey-colored strap and said, "Tomorrow I could try five, if you are still beside me." The keeper agreed. Somewhere below, the river showed between the trees like a silver thread, and the clock tower's kind chime arrived late and gentle, as if it had climbed the hill slowly too.

Bima helped fold the cloth over the peaches so the sun would not warm them too much. He noticed he was humming. The wobble in his knees had gone to sit somewhere else, not gone forever, but not in charge. Trying the short row had been enough courage for one afternoon. The tall basket no longer looked like a problem wearing leather. It looked like a tool that fit when he did not rush it.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the orchard's little shed, where the window faced the last of the daylight and the lamp was already awake. Bima set the strap straight on the basket and stood with his grown-up keeper in the warm smell of peaches. He had been afraid the fruit would bump, and he had tried anyway, slowly, one peach at a time. The path felt friendly under his shoes as they closed the shed door.
