Yusuf saw the bottle because the sun gave it away. It lay on the shell line, green glass gone soft and foggy from years of polite waves, a cork jammed in its mouth and a pale paper curled inside like a sleeping letter. He did not smash anything. He sat down beside it and brushed dry sand from the glass with his sleeve. The friendly lighthouse stood up the beach, and a gull inspected the bottle, decided it was not food, and walked on, helpful by not pecking the cork to bits. "Someone meant you to be opened by fingers," Yusuf told the glass. "I am fingers."

The cork would not agree. Sand had packed its edge into a gritty seal. Yusuf twisted gently. Nothing. He remembered Uncle saying that force was how glass became a problem. He tried a slower turn, then a wiggle, then he stopped to think. Curiosity, he decided, could be patient without giving up. He poured a capful of fresh drinking water from his bottle, not seawater, around the cork's rim to loosen the salt. While it soaked, he read the shore. Empty shells, a ribbon of weed, a tiny stick shaped like a key. Questions piled up in a friendly crowd. He let them. He did not have to answer all of them with the same bottle.

The cork turned with a grainy sigh and came free. It smelled like wet wood and old storms that had ended well. Yusuf tipped the paper into his hand and unrolled it before the breeze could join the project. The writing was the lighthouse keeper's, block letters, familiar as the chart on the cottage wall. It said, When the sea leaves, which pool keeps a starfish cool, the high puddle or the low one, and why? Yusuf's eyebrows rose. It was not a map to a treasure chest. It was better. It was a question with legs. "A riddle with wet shoes," he said, and laughed.

He put the note in his pocket, the cork back in the bottle so it would not be lost, and walked the rocks with his eyes open. High pools sat like cups on the upper stone, some of them thin as saucers. Lower pools were deeper, tucked in shade, still full because the last tide had poured them and the rock had held on. Yusuf knelt, careful of his knees and of the lives below. In a high saucer he saw only sand and a flicker of light. In a low pool, under a little overhang, a starfish rested, cool and unbothered, every arm at home.

"The low one," he whispered, "because it is deeper and shaded, so the leftover sea stays and stays cooler." He did not touch the starfish to test the idea. The water against his nearby fingertip was enough, cool as a cellar step, while the high saucer felt like a sunny plate. He checked a second pair of pools and saw the same pattern, a crab choosing the lower shade, the upper cup empty of animals. The reason held. Curiosity felt like a lantern lighting one more step, not like a hand grabbing. Yusuf rolled the note and added his answer in pencil underneath, small and neat, so the keeper could read the path of his thinking.

He brought the bottle back to the cottage rather than leaving a guess on the beach. The keeper was polishing a mug and looked up with a grin that already knew. "You found today's question," he said. "I found today's answer," Yusuf said, "and I checked it twice, and I left everybody in the pools." He read his sentence aloud. The keeper tapped the table, pleased. "Yes. The sea lends water, and the low rock is a better bowl. You did not let the riddle stay a riddle." Yusuf's chest felt sunny. He asked if he might write tomorrow's question. "Only if it can be solved by looking kindly," the keeper said. Yusuf already had one brewing.

They set the green bottle on the sill to dry, cork beside it. Yusuf washed salt from his hands and described the overhang, the cool fingertip, the saucer of warm light. He added a drawing of the starfish that was more blob than star, and they both liked it anyway. "Wrong guesses would have been all right too," the keeper said, "if you had gone to see. Curiosity is the going." Yusuf nodded. He thought of other corks in his life, stuck lids, tight knots, questions that felt jammed. A little water, a little time, a walk to look. That was a method. It did not require breaking.

Before lunch he walked back to the low pool once more, not to change his answer, just to say a quiet thank-you to a place that had taught him. The starfish had shifted one arm's width and no more. Yusuf kept his shadow from covering it for long. The gull passed overhead and did not dive. The shore went on with its bright work. Yusuf's note crackled in his pocket, a small paper sail. He felt richer than if the bottle had held a toy. He held a reason, and reasons could be used again.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the green bottle as Yusuf copied his answer into a little shore notebook. The glass took the light and turned it into a gentle green coin on the table. He set the cork loosely in the neck so the next question could breathe. Outside, the pools would change with every tide, and that meant new questions would never run out. Yusuf closed the notebook, satisfied and still wondering, which felt like the right pair of feelings. Curiosity had not needed a crash or a prize. It had needed a cork turned slowly and a starfish left in its cool low home.
