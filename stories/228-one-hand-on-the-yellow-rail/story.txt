A friendly fog rested on the harbor like a pale blanket that had not yet decided to fold. Haru stood at the start of the pier with both hands on the yellow rail. The boats were soft gray shapes, and the far buoy rang a slow, cozy note. Oleg waited beside Haru, calm and close. A harbor helper stood a few steps ahead, checking that the rail was clear. "The fog is only a cloud sitting low," the helper said. "This morning it is gentle. We can see the yellow rail, and we can hear the bell. That is enough to walk."

Haru looked at the hidden masts and felt unsure. Courage, today, did not feel like a leap. It felt like a question. "What if the pier looks longer in the fog?" Haru asked. Oleg answered in a steady voice. "Then we walk one board at a time. I will count with you. The helper will stay where we can hear footsteps." Haru nodded. The fog did not sting or rush. It cooled the air in a soft way. Still, Haru wanted a plan. Plans made brave feelings grow from something real.

They agreed on three comforts. They would keep one hand on the rail. They would stop whenever Haru asked. They would turn back if the yellow paint disappeared. The helper liked those rules and walked a little ahead, never so far that the footsteps faded. Haru took the first step. The board was solid. The second step was solid too. Oleg counted, "One, two," and then let Haru count, "Three, four." The buoy bell rang again, not as a warning, but as a friendly voice in the white air. Haru's shoulders dropped a little.

Halfway along, a boat's outline grew clearer, then softer, as if it were playing peek. Haru paused. "I need a stop." Oleg stopped at once, no sighing. They listened. Water tapped the pilings in small polite sounds. A gull called once and sounded nearby, not lost. Haru named what was still easy to see. The rail. Oleg's sleeve. The helper's hat. "I can do the next boards," Haru said. Saying it out loud made it true enough to try. Courage, Haru decided, could be a sentence you believed for ten steps, and then renewed.

The fog began to brighten from above. A pale gold seeped in, and the top of a mast drew itself out of the white. Haru smiled without letting go of the rail. "It is clearing from the top, like a lid lifting." Oleg laughed quietly. "You are watching like a sailor." They kept the same slow pace even though that small unsure feeling was thinning too. Haru did not need to pretend the start had been easy. The middle had been the brave part, and the middle was made of counting and a hand on yellow paint.

Near the bell post, the helper waited and pointed to the buoy, now a clear shape on the water. The fog pulled back in ribbons. The harbor returned piece by piece, ropes, reflections, a stack of crates, the far shore. Haru touched the post. "I walked the whole pier." The words felt warm. Oleg bumped a shoulder gently against Haru's. "You walked it in a way that kept everyone safe. That is the kind of brave I like." The helper nodded. "Fog can be a friend when we respect it and stay by the rail."

They stood and watched the last thin veil leave the water. The air smelled like clean rope and cool wood. Haru asked if they might walk back without holding the rail the whole time, now that the boards were easy to see. The helper said yes, but the rail would stay beside them like a choice. Haru tried a few free steps, then took the rail again just because it felt wise, not because the fog demanded it. Choosing care even after the worry faded seemed like a second kind of courage, a quieter one.

On the way back, Haru told the story of the walk in order. The rules. The stop. The counting. The mast appearing. Oleg listened as if it were a fine tale, because it was. The sky above the harbor was opening into a mild blue. Nothing had been frightening in a sharp way. It had been unknown, and then it had become known. Haru liked that change. The pier felt shorter in clear air, but the brave steps still counted at full size.

At the harbor gate they paused so Haru could look back on purpose. The pier was visible now, every board honest and plain. The bell sounded smaller in clear air, a friendly tap instead of a guide. Haru told the helper the three rules again, not because they were needed, but because repeating them made the courage feel earned and tidy. The helper said the rules were good on foggy mornings and on bright ones. Oleg wrote them with a finger in the cool air, one, two, three. Haru laughed and felt the walk settle into a story that would be easy to trust next time the pale blanket returned.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Haru looked out toward the harbor and saw the lights along the pier like patient stars. The fog was gone, and the evening water lay calm. Haru touched the windowsill and remembered the yellow rail. Courage had been a slow walk with a friend, a helper, and a bell that never asked anyone to hurry.
