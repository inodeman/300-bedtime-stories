The post office parcel table was wide and scarred in friendly ways, with a jar of pencils and a pair of scissors on a string so they could not wander. Samir stood there with Uncle and a box of drawings for his faraway aunt. The box smelled like pencil and a little like the cinnamon Uncle had tucked in a corner as a joke. Samir's ribbon was green, long, and wound around a card. "We will tie a bow the aunt can untie without a struggle," Uncle said. Samir liked the plan. He liked being the person who held the ribbon straight.

Beside them a child about his age held a parcel wrapped in brown paper. The child's string had frayed to a fuzzy sigh. One end dangled, too short to knot. The child tried once, then set the parcel down and looked at the jar as if a pencil could become string. It could not. "My string gave up on the walk," the child said, mostly to the table. "The parcel is a book for my granddad. He likes boats." Samir felt the green ribbon in his hands. There was more than a bow's worth. There was a whole card of it. His first wish was to keep every inch for the perfect bow. His second wish was kinder.

He looked at Uncle, who lifted a shoulder in a way that meant the choice was Samir's and the answer could be generous. Samir measured with his eyes. "We can share," he said. "I will cut enough for your book to travel, and I will still have a bow." The child's face opened like a window. "I am Nia," she said, then laughed. "Not the creek Nia. The parcel Nia. Thank you." Samir laughed too, because the town was full of names doing their jobs. He slid the scissors' string aside and cut a length, careful, the way Uncle had taught, blades away from fingers, eyes on the line.

They tied Nia's parcel together. Samir held a finger on the knot while she pulled gently. The green looked bright against the brown paper. "Granddad will think the book came with a garden," she said. Samir tied his own bow next, a little smaller than the one he had pictured, and still proud. The cinnamon box looked dressed for a journey. Uncle weighed both parcels on the scale, which clicked and settled. The clerk pressed stamps: a sailboat for the boat book, a pear for the drawings, because pears were what the sheet had left. Samir pressed the pear's corner. Nia pressed the sailboat's.

"You saved the trip," Nia said. "The book would have waited at home another day, and Granddad's birthday is not a good waiter." Samir shook his head. "The ribbon was longer than my box needed. Extra is for sharing." They slid the parcels through the slot one after another. The slot whispered twice. Samir imagined two roads leaving the town, one with boats and one with drawings, both wearing green. He kept the leftover ribbon wrapped on the card. It was shorter and still useful for a future parcel or a jar in the kitchen. Useful felt better than endless.

They washed stamp-damp fingers at the corner sink, where the soap smelled like lemons. Nia told him the boat book had a page about ferries. Samir told her the drawings were of their street in four kinds of weather. Uncle bought a single extra stamp for the kitchen jar, a tiny bird, so the leftover ribbon would have a friend. The fan turned. The cubbyholes watched like a quiet audience. Samir's problem had been a beautiful length of ribbon and a wish to spend it all on one bow. The wish had loosened. Two parcels were on their way.

On the step outside, Nia waved and went toward the bakery. Samir waved with the ribbon card still in his fist, then relaxed his hand so the card would not crease. "You measured with your eyes and with your kindness," Uncle said. Samir nodded. He could still see the sailboat stamp in his mind, blue and small. The post office door closed on a soft hinge. The street smelled like bread and warm dust. He swung Uncle's hand and practiced the story he would tell at supper, the frayed string, the green knot, the two whispers of the slot.

At home he put the leftover ribbon in the kitchen jar beside the bird stamp. He told Mama about parcel Nia and the granddad who liked boats. Mama tied a practice bow on a spoon and let Samir untie it, so he would know the aunt's hands would be all right. The bow came free in one smooth pull. Samir grinned. Sharing had not ruined the gift. It had made two gifts travel, and a spoon could still wear green for a minute of practice.

Samir watched the clerk slide their receipts into a little tray, each one a pale ticket to patience. He read his aunt's name on the label and pictured her kitchen table, clear except for the cinnamon box. Nia read her granddad's name and smiled at the sailboat stamp one last time through the paper gap. They did not trade parcels. They only traded a look that said both gifts were safe. The scissors on their string swung and settled. Samir liked a room where tools stayed attached to their jobs and ribbons could still be shared by hand. He folded his receipt into a small square and tucked it where the cinnamon smell could find it later.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the jar of ribbon and the little bird stamp. Samir watched the light sit on the glass. Somewhere a book of boats and a box of drawings were beginning their patient rides. He hoped both would be opened with the same gentle pull he had practiced. Sharing, he thought, was a ribbon long enough for two knots. He let the post office fan's memory turn slowly until his eyes closed.
