Callum knew the chorus and not the verses. The town's evening song was like a path he could join at the gate. After the festival supper, people lingered on the green with lanterns at their feet. Bowls were stacked. A lute had gone quiet. Mr. Ibarra, who watched over the music, lifted a hand and hummed the first line of the chorus so softly that the grass might have started it. Aunt Mae looked at Callum. Her eyebrows asked a question without pushing.

"I can do the part that repeats," Callum said. His voice came out smaller than the sentence in his head. He tried once more, just for her. "I will sing beside you, not in front." Aunt Mae nodded. "Beside is a brave place," she said. "The verses can belong to whoever knows them. The chorus belongs to anyone who wants the song to feel like a circle." Callum's shoulders dropped. A circle had room. He did not need to be a gate all by himself.

They stood near a lantern with a blue shade. The light made Aunt Mae's earrings look like tiny drops of water. Callum held a cup of warm barley tea in one hand and nothing in the other, so the free hand could rest on his chest and feel the song while it was still gathering. The tea smelled toasted. He took a sip, and the warmth went down like a steadying rail. Mr. Ibarra hummed the chorus again, a little clearer, still not loud.

Aunt Mae sang the words, barely above speech. Callum joined on the second phrase. His note was thin and true. He did not chase volume. He matched her. A woman across the circle smiled without turning it into a performance. The verse that followed was sung by Mr. Ibarra alone, a story about bakers closing their doors kindly and lanterns walking home. Callum listened hard so he would know when the gate returned. His free hand felt his breath go in and out. The chorus came back, and he was there.

"Again," he whispered, pleased. Aunt Mae's eyes shone. They sang the chorus three times in all, and each time Callum's note sat more comfortably in the chord of quiet voices. He heard a child-sized gap where his sound fit, though he did not think of himself as filling a hole. He thought of himself as laying a stitch. The green smelled like cut clover and lemon peel from the empty cups. Someone passed a basket of plain crackers. Callum took one after the chorus, not during, so crumbs would not interrupt a word.

Mr. Ibarra thanked the circle for keeping the song small enough to live in. He looked at Callum. "Your chorus was a railing," he said. "People can hold a railing." Callum did not know what to do with the praise, so he said, "Aunt Mae started." Mr. Ibarra nodded. "And you continued. That is how railings get long." Callum felt heat in his ears that was not trouble. It was the temperature of being seen kindly. He drank the rest of his tea. The last sip was cooler and still good.

They walked the edge of the green once, lanterns lifted, the song finished. Callum did not sing on the walk. He let the chorus rest in his pocket of memory. Aunt Mae hummed one line and stopped, as if checking that he still had it. He hummed the answering line. The pair of lines met and were enough. A baker's window showed empty trays, clean and pale. The evening felt rinsed and calm. Callum's feet were sure on the path.

Before the first chorus, Callum asked if he could hear the words one extra time. Mr. Ibarra spoke them like a recipe, slow and clear, with no tune at all. Callum repeated the last three under his breath. They tasted like ordinary speech, which helped. "Now the tune can wear them," Aunt Mae said. He nodded. A moth circled the blue shade and found another errand. Callum rolled his shoulders once, the way he had seen singers do, and felt his neck grow easy. The tea cup was warm against his palm. He was not late. The song had not started without the chance to prepare.

When the crackers came around again, Callum passed the basket before he took his second piece. The salt was bright. He brushed crumbs onto a napkin, not onto the lantern glass. Aunt Mae pointed out how the circle had swayed, barely, on the last chorus, bodies agreeing without a plan. "You were inside that sway," she said. Callum could still feel it, a small boat motion in his knees. He pressed his feet into the grass until the feeling became a memory he could keep. Courage, he decided, had not been loud. It had been a repeated door he was willing to open.

On the walk home he matched his steps to the rhythm of the chorus without making a sound. Four steps, a rest, four steps. Aunt Mae noticed and smiled sideways. "You are practicing in your shoes," she said. Callum laughed once, quietly. A window they passed let out the smell of soap and oranges. He breathed it in and let the breath be as long as a note. The long word felt shorter now that he had crossed it with company. He touched his chest where his hand had rested and found his heartbeat walking, not racing. The grass was cool under his shoes, and a single cricket kept time without showing off. He tasted a little salt from the cracker still hiding at the corner of his mouth and smiled. Lantern light rested on Aunt Mae's sleeve like a piece of warm paper.

At the gate he told Aunt Mae the exact word that had been hardest, a long one in the middle of the chorus, and how he had leaned on her note to cross it. She said leaning was allowed in music and in walking. He believed her. The blue lantern at the green was far behind them now, and their own windows waited. At home he rinsed his cup and let the chorus rest in the quiet room. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and Callum hummed one small line toward it, no louder than the house, glad his voice had joined the circle.
