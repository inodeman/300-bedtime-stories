The little wagon stopped at the front step with a soft thud. Inside it, oranges bumped together and sent up a smell like sunshine and peel. Jamila put her hands on her hips. Kostas put his hands on the wagon handle, which was warm from the walk. One low step stood between them and the path to their uncle's gate. Their uncle grew mint by the fence and always saved the last cookie. Today he needed oranges for a salad, and his knee liked fewer trips up steps.

"We could carry them in our shirts," Kostas said. An orange peeked out and nearly escaped. Jamila caught it. The peel was bumpy and cool. "Or we could build a ramp," she said. "A gentle one, so the wagon rolls and the fruit stays put." Kostas liked that better. Kindness, he thought, should not include chasing oranges down the walk. A sparrow hopped nearby, looked at the bright fruit, and decided seeds were more its size.

Papa heard the thud and came out with a pencil behind his ear. "Show me," he said. They showed him a short board from the scrap pile. He set it against the step. It looked steep, like a slide for brave crumbs. "If you try that, the wagon may run," Papa said. "We can use it only if we make the slope kinder and I stand at the side. No fingers near the wheels." Jamila and Kostas nodded. The rule felt as solid as the step.

They taped a wide piece of cardboard to the board so the wagon would have a smooth road. The tape smelled sharp for a moment, then faded. Kostas drew arrows that pointed up, big and friendly. Jamila folded a towel at the top so the board would not slip on the step. Papa pressed the towel with his shoe and said, "Good. I will still hold the side." They loaded the oranges again. One leaf from the bag stuck to Jamila's wrist. She blew it off, and it spun into the grass.

"Slow," Papa said. Kostas pulled. The wagon climbed, wheels ticking on the cardboard. Then the ramp's steep middle made the handle jerk, and two oranges hopped the side like round rabbits. They did not go far. They bumped the flowerpot and sat there, fragrant and fine, leaves pointing at the sky. "Stop," Jamila called, already calm. Papa held the wagon. Nobody was hurt. The arrows on the cardboard pointed up as if they were still hopeful. Kostas puffed his cheeks. "Too much hill," he said. "The oranges think it is a game." A petal stuck to one orange, pink and silly.

They looked at the scrap pile again. A second board was longer. Papa laid the two boards so the ramp started farther from the step and rose slowly, like a yawn instead of a jump. Jamila moved the towel to the new top. Kostas added a cardboard lip on each side, taped flat, so fruit could not wander. "May we test with one orange?" he asked. Papa nodded. The single orange rolled only a tiny bit and then stayed when Kostas kept the wagon steady. "The path is polite now," Jamila said, and she laughed at her own sentence.

They brought the rest up together. The wheels hummed a low note. The oranges crowded close but did not jump. At the top, the path to the gate was flat and warm. The little bell on their uncle's fence was not theirs to ring yet, so Jamila knocked on the gate with her knuckles, soft as a question. He opened it and the mint smell came out to meet them. "Oranges," he said, delighted. "And a ramp. You made my step into a hello." He let them each peel one. Juice misted the air, cool and bright on their wrists. The salad could wait one juicy minute while they told the story of the ramp.

They told him about the two that had tried to hop like rabbits. He chuckled and set those two on top, "the adventurers," with their leaves still attached. Kostas wound the wagon handle so the string would not drag. Jamila wiped her sticky fingers on a damp cloth Papa had brought, and the cloth smelled like clean cotton. The ramp waited behind them, longer than their first idea, gentle enough for kindness. A bee investigated a peel and changed its mind. The walk home felt easy, the wagon light, their pockets empty and their errand full. Even the step looked friendlier now that it had a gentle answer.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when Papa set it on the sill to guide them up their own step. They did not need a ramp there, only careful feet and the rail he had checked last spring. Jamila looked back at the gentle boards, pale in the evening, and Kostas said, "Tomorrow we can take the mint our uncle promised." The lantern made a warm pool of light on the floor, and the orange smell still lived on their hands like a small, kind sun.
