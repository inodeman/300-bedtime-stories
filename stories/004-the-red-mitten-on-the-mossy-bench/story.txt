Sam stood at the birch that marked the start of the woods path, Ada's red mitten folded in his pocket. It was soft wool, still warm from his hands, with a tiny blue stitch near the cuff that Ada's grandmother had sewn. Ada had left it on the school step, and Sam had promised to set it on the mossy bench before supper, because that was where Ada always paused on her way home. Papa rested a palm on the birch's white bark. "The path is the one you know," Papa said. "I will wait here and listen for the woodpecker. You walk to the bench, leave the mitten in the dry nook, and walk back. Steady feet. Bright eyes." Sam nodded. His first solo errand felt large and also simple, like a spoon that was bigger than it looked.

The path smelled of pine needles and damp bark. Sun came through the leaves in coins of light that slid over Sam's shoes when the breeze moved. A squirrel flowed up a trunk and sat with its tail like a question mark, not blocking the way. Sam touched the mitten through his pocket to be sure it stayed. He did not run. Running, Papa had said, was for open yards, not for roots. Sam stepped over a root that looked like a sleeping elbow. He said, "Excuse me," to a fern he had to brush, then smiled at himself for being polite to a plant. The politeness made his chest feel roomy. Birds stitched songs from branch to branch. None of the songs were warnings. They were just the woods being busy and green.

At the little bend by the hollow log, Sam usually liked to stop and count the shelf mushrooms, orange and neat as stacked plates. Today he counted only three, then remembered the mitten and the bench. "I can count the rest on the way back," he told the log. Saying the plan out loud made it sturdier. His voice sounded like his own voice, not too loud, not tiny. A yellow leaf turned in the air and landed on his sleeve. He let it ride for a few steps, then set it down so it could finish its fall. The path widened. He saw the pale stone that meant the bench was near. His shoulders, which had been up by his ears, settled into their ordinary place.

The mossy bench sat under a beech tree, green on top and smooth where people sat. Sam took the mitten out. It had fuzzed a little from the pocket. He smoothed it with his palm, thumb over the blue stitch so it would show. Under the bench was a dry nook between two stones, the place Ada had once called the mitten shelf, because she forgot things and then remembered where forgetting lived. Sam slid the red wool in, cuff out, so the color could be seen from the path. "There you are," he said. "Ada will be glad her hand has a home." He stood a moment and listened. Water moved somewhere, a thin creek, more like a whisper than a shout. The air felt cool and clean on his cheeks.

Going back was a new kind of courage, because the errand was done and the path still needed walking. Sam did not sit down and wait for Papa to come find him. He turned his feet toward the birch. He passed the hollow log and counted the rest of the shelf mushrooms: four, five, six. "Six," he said, pleased. A jay flashed blue and then was only leaves again. Sam's shoelace ticked against his ankle. He stopped, tied it with a double bow, and checked the other lace too. Small care, he thought, was part of a brave walk. Brave did not have to be noisy. Brave could be a bow that stayed tied and a promise that arrived where it belonged.

He saw Papa before Papa saw him, because Papa was watching a woodpecker climb in little hops. Sam did not call out at once. He finished the last steps on his own, all the way to the birch, and then he said, "It is on the bench nook. Cuff showing." Papa's smile folded the corners of his eyes. "That is a finished job." He offered his hand, and Sam took it, not because he needed pulling, but because holding hands was a good ending. They walked the lane toward home. Sam's pocket felt light. His legs felt useful. He told Papa about the six mushrooms and the fern he had excused himself to. Papa chuckled. "The woods like good manners. I am almost sure of it."

Later, from the garden gate, they saw Ada and her grandmother on the far side of the green, Ada waving a red mitten like a small flag of thanks. She did not need to cross all the way over. The wave was enough. Sam waved back with both hands, then remembered one hand was still in Papa's, and laughed. Ada cupped her hands around her mouth. "It was dry!" she called. "Thank you, Sam!" The words crossed the grass without getting lost. Sam felt them land warmly. He had not done a giant thing. He had done a true thing at the right time, and the truth of it was bright as wool.

Supper tasted like potatoes and dill. Sam ate and talked and then grew quiet in the comfortable way that follows a finished promise. He looked toward the trees, now a dark green wall with one pale birch still easy to find. He knew the path was there even when he could not see every root. That knowing felt like a lantern of its own, though the real one was indoors. He stacked his bowl in the sink and wiped the table sprinkle of salt into his palm, then into the bin, so the wood stayed smooth.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Sam set his shoes side by side, laces neat from the bow he had tied in the woods. Papa read the first page of a calm book and left the rest for tomorrow. Sam climbed into bed with the day still sweet on him: pine, moss, and the idea of a red cuff showing just enough. He had walked to the bench and back again. The mitten was where a cold hand could find it. Courage, he decided, could be as quiet as moss and still be strong enough to carry a promise all the way home.
