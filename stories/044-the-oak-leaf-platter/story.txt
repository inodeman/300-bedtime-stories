Hassan balanced a wide oak-leaf platter on both hands as he walked the mossy path to the picnic clearing. The platter was a big stiff leaf he had dried flat, and on it sat six berry scones, golden at the edges and purple where the berries had burst. They smelled so warm that he had to remind his fingers not to sneak a nibble. "These are for the picnic," he told himself. "I will taste one when the platter is safe on the stump." Sunlight speckled his sleeves. Ferns brushed his legs with cool tips. A paper map crackled in his pocket, though he knew this path under the oaks by heart.

The clearing opened like a green room with a floor of moss. Hassan set the platter down and arranged the scones in a circle, the prettiest one in the middle, the one with the most berries shining like small jewels. He had planned to eat that one himself. He poured water into acorn caps for tiny cups, then sat and admired the look of it all. A gentle fox stepped out from the oaks, then a smaller fox with bright curious eyes, and then a third who moved slowly, as if every patch of moss deserved a visit. They sat at a polite distance, noses lifting at the sweet smell.

"Hello," Hassan said. His voice came out shy and glad at the same time. "I brought scones. I think I brought enough if we each take a friendly piece and not a whole mountain." The foxes' tails drew soft questions on the moss. Hassan looked at the middle scone. It was the one he had saved in his mind all morning while the cottage oven did its warm work. He picked it up, broke it into four, and felt only a small pinch of wanting. Then the pinch loosened. "The middle is for sharing," he said. "The best bite should not stay a secret in my pocket."

They ate together in the dappled light. The scones were crumbly and sweet, with warm berries that stained the oak-leaf platter in cloudy purple shapes. Hassan laughed when the smallest fox got a crumb on its nose and crossed its eyes trying to see it. He passed an acorn cap of water, and the fox lapped without tipping the cap. "You are very good at picnics," Hassan said. The slow fox nudged a spare crumb toward the smallest, a share inside the share. Hassan noticed and felt his chest grow warm. Sharing, he saw, could hop from one friend to another like a smile.

He had two scones left, and his stomach still had a little room. He also had the walk home to think about. For a moment he pictured wrapping both for his own shelf. Then he remembered the hollow tree library, where leaves were stacked like quiet books and a reader might like a surprise on the broad root. "One for the path, and one for the library step," he decided aloud. He wrapped them in smaller leaves and tied them with grass. The picnic foxes watched without grabbing. "You already have your pieces," Hassan said kindly. "This one is traveling to someone else."

They lingered while the light shifted from bright to soft. Hassan unfolded his paper map and showed the rope footbridge and the mushroom cottages drawn in berry ink. The foxes sniffed the paper as if maps might smell like paths, and maybe they did, a dry smell mixed with berry stain from his fingers. He told them how he had stirred the scone dough in a wooden bowl and waited for the berries to cool. "Waiting was part of the cooking," he said. "Sharing is part of the eating." A firefly tested its glow under a fern, then dimmed, saving its brightness for the warm dusk still to come.

When it was time to go, Hassan rinsed the oak-leaf platter with a little water and shook it dry. The berry stains made a pattern he did not want to scrub away. "I could keep this on my wall," he said. "A platter that remembers a good circle." The slow fox walked with him as far as the library tree. Hassan set one wrapped scone on the root by the hollow door, where leaf-pages rested in shady piles. "For whoever is reading," he called, soft enough not to startle. A brush of a tail inside showed that someone had heard. Hassan smiled and did not stand there waiting to be thanked.

The other foxes had already slipped back among the oaks, but the path did not feel empty. Hassan ate his piece of the middle scone as he walked, and it tasted brighter than he expected, perhaps because it had been part of a circle and not a lonely bite. Crumbs dotted his palm. He let them fall gently onto the moss. Near his mushroom cottage the ferns stood tall, and his round door looked glad to see him. He paused to listen to oak leaves rubbing their palms together overhead, a dry friendly sound that followed him up the step.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as Hassan set the oak-leaf platter on the sill to finish drying. The light turned the berry stains the color of warm plums. He touched the middle of the platter, where the best scone had rested, and felt glad it had become four bites instead of one proud secret. Somewhere a fox was probably licking the last crumb from a whisker in the picnic clearing. Hassan drank a sip of cool water and listened to the quiet wood. Sharing, he thought, had not made his day smaller. It had made the clearing, the library step, and the walk home feel generously full.
