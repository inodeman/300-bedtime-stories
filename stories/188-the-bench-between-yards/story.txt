Paz found the tape roll in the grass, speckled with clover. Qadir found the ball of string under the bench that was not a bench yet, only a wish. The shady strip between their yards smelled like damp leaves and tomato vines. A plant stake leaned like it was tired, and the tomato plant's little stem needed a friend to stand beside it. "We keep losing the tools," Paz said. "Tape in the grass. String in the dirt. Clothespins in a cup in your kitchen, and a different cup in mine."

Qadir set down a sturdy crate Auntie had said they could use if it stayed on flat ground. "A shared bench," he said. "Low enough for us. Cups for sorting. And a rule so we do not both pull the tape at once." Paz liked the word shared. It felt like a wide chair. She ran her hand over the crate. The wood was rough in a friendly way, with a knot that looked like an eye. Above them, leaves clicked when the breeze came through, and the shade was cool on their necks.

Auntie looked over the fence with a basket of clean rags. "If you want the crate turned into a bench, I will sand the top edge so it does not snag sleeves," she said. "You may sort the cups. Scissors stay with me." They agreed. The sanding made a soft whisper and a pale dust that smelled like dry wood. Paz wiped the top with a damp rag until it felt smooth. Qadir set three cups in a row: one blue, one yellow, one white. The cups had once held rice. Now they would hold ideas with handles.

They began to sort. Clothespins clicked into the blue cup. String went into the yellow cup, but only after Qadir wound it into a ball again, because loose string is a nest for trouble. The tape was the problem. Paz reached for it at the same moment Qadir did. Their fingers met. The roll jumped, the end stuck to the crate, and a long shiny ribbon leapt out and wrapped the tomato stake, Paz's wrist, and one unlucky clover. "Oh," they said together. It was sticky, silly, and a little tight. "Auntie," Paz called, calm. "The tape hugged us."

Auntie peeled it back slowly so it would not pull skin. Nobody was hurt. The freed tape sat in a loop like a shiny snake that was not a snake, only tape. Qadir's ears were pink. "We need the rule before the tools," he said. Paz nodded. She cut nothing. She folded a card and drew two faces, one with a sun, one with a moon. "Sun turn, moon turn," she said. "The person whose turn it is holds the tape. The other holds the end. Then we swap." They tucked the card into the white cup, which would be the home for turns and labels.

They tried the new way. Qadir had the sun first. He held the roll. Paz held the end flat against a small sign. The sign said "string" and went in front of the yellow cup. Another said "pins." Another said "tape." The letters were big. The pictures were bigger: a curly line, a pin, a round roll. When the tape tried to race ahead, Qadir pressed his thumb on the roll the way Auntie had shown him, away from Paz's fingers. Sharing felt slower than grabbing. It also worked. The signs stayed put. The clover was free.

Then they helped the tomato plant. Auntie held the stake steady. Paz and Qadir took turns with the soft tie, not the tape, because stems like cloth better than stickiness. The stem stood taller, not squeezed, just accompanied. A green tomato, small as a marble, bumped Paz's knuckle. She grinned. "The bench shared its string," she said. Qadir put the ball back in the yellow cup without being asked. That was the part he was proudest of. The shade smelled like tomatoes and clean rag. Their hands were a little grassy, a little sticky, and done.

They made one more label, "back home," and pointed it at the cups, a reminder at the end of play. Paz set the sun and moon card where tomorrow could find it. Qadir rolled the leftover tape until the end lay flat, a tiny square, no wild ribbon. The crate bench waited between the yards like a quiet agreement. Sharing, they were learning, was not only passing a thing. It was leaving the thing ready for the next hand.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in Paz's kitchen, and Qadir could see it from his steps like a small shared star. He lifted a hand. She lifted one back. On the bench, the cups sat in a neat row, blue, yellow, and white, each tool in its home, labels facing the path so morning would understand them. The tomato stake stood straight in the dim garden, and the tape, for once, stayed exactly where it belonged, the loose end pressed flat as a promise.
