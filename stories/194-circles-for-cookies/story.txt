Cookies cooled on the rack, smelling like butter and brown sugar, their tops cracked in friendly lines. Qadir's fingers hovered. Luna held a stack of small plates, one for each porch on their side of the lane. Nana had said they could share the batch if the counting was fair and if no one touched a cookie until the rack was cool enough for her to test. She had tested. The cookies were warm, not hot. The kitchen window was fogged at the corner from the baking, and the air felt like a hug you could eat.

"Four plates," Luna said. "Four cookies each. And if there are extras, we decide together." Qadir nodded. He also knew which plate would go to his friend on the corner, the one who laughed at his drawings. His hand, almost by itself, placed two extra cookies on that plate. They sat there looking perfectly happy and perfectly uneven. Luna was counting the rack and did not see. Qadir's stomach felt the way a wobbly stool feels. The extras were a kind wish and a crooked truth at the same time.

He could have slid them back before anyone noticed. He almost did. Then he thought of the jars in Luna's kitchen, the ones she had told the truth about, a story she had shared on the steps. Honesty had looked like a relief on her face. Qadir set his palms on the table. "Luna," he said. "I put two extra on the corner plate. I wanted my friend to have more. I should have said it first." The words were shaky and then solid. Luna looked at the plate, then at him. She did not grab the cookies back in a huff. She said, "Thank you for telling me. Now the cookies can be fair for real."

Nana, who had heard from the sink, dried her hands. "That was a straight sentence," she said. "How will you make the sharing match the sentence?" Qadir pulled a piece of cardboard from the craft box. "Circles," he said. "One circle, one cookie. If a plate has an empty circle, it is still hungry. If a cookie has no circle, it is a wanderer." Luna smiled and fetched a cup to trace. The cup left round pencil moons on the cardboard. They made four rows of four. The pencil smelled like school. Sugar crystals glittered on the table like sand.

They moved every cookie onto a circle, including the two wanderers. Qadir did the moving while Luna watched the count, then they swapped so both pairs of eyes did the job. Sixteen cookies. Sixteen circles. No one hiding at the edge. The corner plate would still be delicious. It would not be secretly taller. Qadir felt the wobble in his stomach settle into a flat, good feeling. He told Luna, "I can give my friend a drawing as the extra, instead of someone else's cookie." "That extra is yours to give," she said. "It does not nibble the fairness."

There were two cookies left on the rack that had not fit the first plan. Nana had set them aside as the baker's thank-you, and Qadir had almost forgotten. They drew two more circles to the side and labeled them with a small heart that meant thank you, not more for a favorite. Nana's plate got those, and she pretended to be very formal, which made them laugh. Then they covered each neighbor plate with a clean napkin. The napkins puffed up like little tents. Warm cookie smell leaked out anyway, advertising the errand to anyone with a nose.

They walked the lane together. At each porch they counted again, not because they did not trust themselves, but because honesty likes company. Four. Four. Four. At the corner, Qadir gave the plate and the drawing of a bird with sugar dots on its wings. His friend hugged the plate with careful arms. Nobody compared piles because the piles matched. On the way home Luna said, "The truth made the circles easy." Qadir said, "The circles made the truth easy to see." Both sentences belonged on the cardboard, so when they got back he wrote them along the edge in small neat letters.

The cardboard stayed on the table until the crumbs were wiped and the pencil moons were smudged with a little butter. Qadir did not mind. A tool could look used. What mattered was that he had not let a quiet extra stay hidden. Luna rinsed the cup they had traced. The kitchen cooled. The window cleared. Somewhere down the lane, neighbors were tasting the same sweet number.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the empty circles, which now looked like a map of a fair afternoon. Qadir set his bird drawing's spare corner under the lantern so the lines showed, sugar dots and all. Luna leaned on the doorframe, full and glad, the smell of brown sugar still in the room. Honesty had not made the cookies smaller. It had made the sharing something they could both hold up to the light.
