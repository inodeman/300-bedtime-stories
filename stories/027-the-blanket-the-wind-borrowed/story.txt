Kira snapped the striped picnic blanket open, and the dune wind accepted the invitation at once. The cloth rose like a cheerful sail, blue and cream and melon-pink, then folded over her head with a soft whump. She laughed into the cotton. It smelled like last week's sun and a hint of lemon soap. "I meant to spread you, not wear you," she told the blanket. When she wrestled a corner down, another corner lifted. A gull hovered, not to tease, but as if offering to be a fourth helper, then settled on a fence post to supervise properly. The friendly lighthouse watched from the point, white and calm above the wooden boats.

Nana arrived with a basket that thumped against her knee. Uncle came behind her with a jug of water beaded in cold drops. They took in the sight of Kira hopping on a living rug. "The wind has joined the picnic," Nana said. "It did not bring a napkin." Kira blew hair from her eyes. "I can do it. I almost had three corners." Even as she spoke, the three corners became two, then one, then a lively heap. Sand peppered her ankles. She felt a small prickle of wanting to win alone. The blanket did not care about winning. It cared about air.

"May we come aboard?" Uncle asked. Kira nodded, a little relieved and a little stubborn at the same time. "Yes. Please. It is bigger than my arms." They made a plan without speeches. Kira would take the seaward edge. Nana would take the lighthouse edge. Uncle would hold the middle so the cloth could not belly up and slap the jug. On the count of three they lifted together, not high, only enough to let the sand slide off. The blanket cracked once, a bright sound, then lay down like an animal deciding to rest. "Now," Nana said. "Weights, before it remembers it can fly."

Kira pinned her corner with a smooth gray stone she had chosen on the way up. Nana used her sandals, set neatly side by side. Uncle parked the basket on the third corner and the water jug on the fourth. The wind tugged, found itself outvoted, and went off to comb the marram grass instead. Kira sat in the middle and felt the cloth stay. "It worked because we picked jobs," she said. "If we had all grabbed the same corner, the other three would have gone to sea." Uncle grinned. "That is teamwork. Different hands, same blanket."

They unpacked bread, cucumber, and a little pot of soft cheese. The boats below made their wooden music. Kira started to pour water and noticed the seaward stone inching as a stronger puff arrived. "Incoming," she called, not frightened, just clear. Nana put her hand on that corner while Kira found a heavier shell-cluster stone. Uncle shifted the basket a thumb toward the sneaking edge. Nobody scolded the wind or each other. They moved like people who trusted the plan. The gull clapped its wings once, a small applause, and stayed wild on the post.

Kira had thought teamwork might feel like giving her picnic away. It felt instead like the picnic growing a stronger floor. She told Nana about the moment she had wanted to say she could do it alone. Nana passed her a cucumber slice, cool and green. "Wanting to be able is a fine spark," Nana said. "Asking when the spark is not enough is the warm fire." Kira crunched and thought. She liked sparks. She also liked sitting on a blanket that did not suddenly become a hat. Both things could live in the same afternoon.

After lunch they shook the crumbs toward the open sand, away from the gull's post so the bird could choose, not be crowded. Then they folded the blanket from three sides, Kira matching her corners to Nana's, Uncle keeping the fold from dragging. The stripes lined up almost perfectly. "Almost is handsome," Uncle said. Kira smoothed one last ridge. She looked at the cove, the tide pools winking, a far starfish like a dropped blossom nobody would pick. The dune was still breezy. Their hair still misbehaved. The difference was that the misbehavior no longer ruined the resting spot.

They carried the fold down the path, Kira in front so she could see the ruts, Nana and Uncle sharing the weight behind. When the basket strap slipped, Kira paused without being asked, and Uncle fixed it. The pause was tiny. The kindness inside it was the same pattern as the blanket. Kira smiled into the wind. "Next time I will set the stones before I boast," she said. "And I will still call you if the wind brings a friend." Nana's laugh rolled ahead of them toward the cottage, bright as the stripes now tucked safe under Kira's chin.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the folded blanket as Kira set it on the wooden chest. The stripes made a quiet rainbow in the low light. She could still feel the moment the three of them had lifted together and the cloth had agreed to be a blanket again. Outside, the dune wind kept practicing its games, and that was fine. It had the whole shore. Inside, Kira washed cucumber scent from her fingers and felt pleasantly useful. Teamwork had not made her smaller. It had given her a picnic that stayed, and people glad to hold the other corners.
