Lila knelt in the garden row with both hands resting on her knees. The soil smelled cool and sweet, like rain that had decided to stay. Yesterday she had pressed tiny radish seeds into the dark earth, each one no bigger than a bread crumb, and patted the row smooth with her palm. A wooden marker said RADISH in her careful letters. A robin hopped along the fence and tipped its head. Lila brushed a speck of soil from the marker. She had looked at breakfast. She had looked after her apple. Now the late morning sun warmed the back of her neck, and the ground still looked like plain ground. "Are you growing yet?" she whispered, so she would not bother anything small that might be trying.

Lila's hands wanted a job. The small trowel lay beside the watering can, its metal warm from the sun. If she scraped the soil aside, she might see a white seed and know it was still there. Grandpa had said the seeds needed a dark, quiet room under the ground, the way bread dough needs a covered bowl. Even so, the waiting felt long. A bee hummed over the lavender and did not rush. The pea vines climbed their strings one curl at a time. Lila set her fingers on the trowel, then lifted them off. "Not yet," she told herself. "A cover stays on while something is working." She folded her hands again and let the garden buzz on without her digging.

She filled the watering can at the rain barrel. The water was cool and smelled faintly of wet wood and leaves. She tilted the sprinkler cap, the round piece with many little holes, and let the water fall like a soft shower, not a rush. Mud sparkled in tiny mirrors. A worm slipped deeper, safe and unbothered, and Lila was glad she had left the soil alone. "There," she said. "A drink, and then I leave you to it." She set the can down and wiped her damp palms on her apron. The row looked the same, only darker, like a path after a gentle rain. Somewhere a gate latch clicked, and the robin flew to a higher rail to watch the drops sink in.

At lunch the kitchen smelled of tomato soup and buttered toast. Lila swung her feet under the chair. "Grandpa," she said, "the radishes are still hiding." Grandpa nodded, a shine of butter on his thumb. "They are not hiding. They are busy where we cannot see." He tapped the table twice, slow taps, the way a clock might if it were kind. "Roots first. Then a green hello." Lila tore her toast into small squares and ate them one by one, practicing the feeling of not rushing. Steam from the soup curled past her nose, tomato-sweet and warm. Outside, the robin sang a short bright line and stopped, as if the song also needed a rest between its notes.

After the dishes, Lila carried a picture book to the garden step but did not open it at once. She watched the row. A ladybug crossed a warm stone. The shadow of the bean pole moved the width of her finger. Nothing green popped up. Her patience felt like a full cup she had to hold level so it would not spill. She spoke to the soil in a friendly voice. "I can wait for your green. I will not pull the cover off." The breeze lifted the hair at her forehead and set it down again. The book stayed closed on her lap, its cover the color of new leaves, while the garden made its small working sounds: drip, leaf, bee, and her own steady breath.

The next morning a mist sat low over the lettuce. Lila walked out in her socks on the sun-warm stones, then remembered and went back for her boots. She crouched so her eyes were near the row. At first she saw only dark crumbs and one pale pebble. Then, near the middle, a bent green loop pushed up, thin as a thread, wearing a cap of soil like a hat. Lila's mouth opened. She did not touch it. "Hello," she whispered. "You did it in your own time." Another tip showed farther down, and another, like tiny umbrellas deciding to open. The robin looked too, bright-eyed, and did not peck a single leaf.

Lila started toward the kitchen door at a run, then slowed, because good news could still be carried carefully. "Grandpa, three green hellos!" Grandpa came with his mug and his soft shoes and stood beside her without counting too fast. "Look at that first one," he said. "It is sturdy because it was not tugged." Lila smiled until her cheeks felt round. She watered again, only a light pass, and the drops sat on the new leaves like glass beads. The soil smell rose, rich and clean. She wiped the wooden marker where water had spotted it, so the word RADISH still showed clearly in the sun.

All that week Lila visited the row each morning and once before supper. She lifted only the weeds Grandpa pointed out, the easy ones that did not belong, and she left every radish baby in its place. When a leaf looked a little dusty, she did not scrub it. Plants, Grandpa said, liked air and light and a drink, not fussing. On Friday the row was a dotted line of green. Lila sat on the step with a strawberry from the bowl, sweet and warm, and ate it slowly while the radishes did their quiet work. The robin found a different fence post and sang a longer song, as if the garden had earned an extra note.

That evening Lila washed her hands at the kitchen sink. Dirt spiraled away, and her fingers still smelled a little like rain and leaves. The lantern by the window glowed softly, laying a round of gold on the sill where she would set her thoughts until morning. Grandpa put two cups on the table, one of milk and one of tea. "You gave them time," he said. Lila nodded, sleepy and pleased. Outside, the garden rested under a pale sky, roots and new green all tucked in. She touched the glass once, gently, and carried her patience indoors like a small warm stone in her pocket.
