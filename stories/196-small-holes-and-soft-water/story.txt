Soren shook a bright drop from his fingertip and watched it fall back into the tray. The seedlings were small, each with two leaves like tiny spoons, standing in soil that had gone pale at the top. Tova sniffed. The step smelled like wet stone and a little like peppery leaves. Their neighbor had left a note that morning, tucked under the tray: "At the library until afternoon. If the soil is dry, please give them a soft drink. Thank you." The note had a drawing of a smile. The children had come with a clean bottle and a question.

"A cup might be too heavy for these stems," Tova said. She touched the soil with one finger. It felt dusty and light, not like a sponge. Soren nodded. "Mama said she can poke a few holes in the lid if we hold the bottle still and keep our hands on the bottom." The bottle was clear and empty, a little scratched from washing, and it made a hollow tock when he tapped it. Bees were busy in the clover and did not need the step. The seedlings did.

Mama came with a small tool of her own and a towel. "Lid on the rail, hands away from the point," she said. They set the bottle down and stepped their fingers back. She made holes, not many, and handed it over closed tight. "Fill it at the outside tap," she said. "Tip it slowly. If it rushes, we change it. Plants like rain, not a waterfall." The tap water was cool and smelled like pipes and then like nothing, which is how clean water smells. Soren filled the bottle halfway so it would not be a heavy surprise.

They tipped it over the first pot. Water rushed out in eager streams, dug a little hole in the soil, and splashed a leaf until the leaf bent. "Oh," Tova said, lifting the bottle upright at once. The seedling stood back up, shiny and a bit startled, but not harmed. Soil speckled the step. Soren's cheeks warmed. "Too many mouths," he said. "The bottle is shouting." Mama nodded. "Then we teach it a whisper. Tape can close a hole without fuss. I will tear the pieces. You may press them on the lid, away from the open ones."

They taped until only two holes remained, small and side by side. Tova tested over the bare step first. Water came out in a soft patter, darkening a circle and then stopping when she turned the bottle up. The sound was like a tiny rain they could hold. Soren grinned. "That is a garden voice." They went back to the pots. Each seedling got a slow count of three, soil turning from pale to deep brown, leaves receiving only a mist at the edges. The peppery smell grew stronger, as if the plants were saying thank you in scent.

They noticed things while they worked, because nature is a conversation if you leave room. One stem had a new leaf folded like a green letter. One pot held a crumb of bark that they lifted out so the sprout would have space. A worm was not in the tray, and they did not go digging for one. The sun warmed their necks. The library note fluttered under a stone they had placed so the breeze would not make it a kite. Soren wiped a muddy fingerprint on the towel and laughed when it looked like a small map.

When every pot was evenly dark, they stopped, even though the bottle still held water. More would have been less kind. Tova wrote on the back of the note, "Soft drink given. Soil was dry. Two holes were enough." Soren drew the bottle with two dots. They set the note where their neighbor would see it first. The seedlings glittered and then dried at the tips, standing a little prouder. The step smelled alive. Mama poured the leftover water on the clover, which was happy to take what the seedlings did not need.

They waited on their own porch until they saw their neighbor turn the corner with a library book under her arm, the cover bright in the sun. Her face opened when she saw the dark soil. "You listened to the leaves," she said. Soren and Tova felt as green and glad as the tray. They did not need a long thank-you. The standing seedlings were the sentence. The bottle, lid dotted with tape, waited to be a gentle rain another day. A warm leaf-smell followed them to their own steps.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as the seedlings outside settled into the evening, each leaf holding a last bead of light. Soren and Tova looked at the note they had written and at their taped lid, a tool made kinder on the second try. The garden smelled cool and peppery through the open window, and the tray was a row of small dark moons. Nature had taken a soft drink, and the children had learned how to offer it without rushing a single stem.
