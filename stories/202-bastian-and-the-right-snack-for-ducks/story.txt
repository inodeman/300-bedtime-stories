Bastian stood in the kitchen with a paper bag of old bread. The crusts were dry at the edges. Some slices were still fine. He could hear the ducks at the pond, soft and busy, and he thought a big crumb picnic would make him a hero. He tucked the bag under his arm and headed for the door.

The grown-up looked up from the sink. "Is that bread for the ducks?" the grown-up asked, kindly and not cross. Bastian nodded. "They like snacks. I see people do it in stories." The grown-up dried both hands and knelt so their eyes were level. "Ducks do like a treat," the grown-up said, "but bread is not a great food for them. It fills their bellies without giving them what they need. Oats and peas are a better pond snack. And we will not dump this bread, because food still has a job."

Bastian felt his plan tilt, like a boat that finds a better current. He set the bag on the table. Together they sorted. Slices that were still good went onto a board for toast. The grown-up warmed them until they smelled nutty, and Bastian spread a thin shine of butter for the household plate. Dry ends that no one would enjoy were torn into small bits for the compost crock. "The garden will use them," the grown-up said. "Nothing is thrown aside as if it does not matter."

Bastian washed his hands. He scooped a little rolled oats into a tin and added a handful of green peas from the cool bowl. The tin was light. He liked that. On the way to the pond he practiced his new sentence. "Bread is for people and for the compost. Oats and peas are for ducks." A rabbit watched from the hedge and did not follow. Button the dog stayed on the porch, chin on paws, because this errand was a quiet one.

At the pond the ducks were three: one pale, one speckled, one with a neat dark bill. They dabbed and tipped and talked in small wet sounds. Bastian did not throw food at them. He did not crowd the bank. He crouched on the path and scattered oats and peas in the shallow edge where they already liked to look for snacks. "Here is a better picnic," he said. The pale duck noticed first. She glided over without hurry. The others came because they chose to. Peas floated like tiny green boats. Oats drifted and sank slowly. The ducks ate in their own time.

Bastian watched their bills work, neat and busy. He felt proud in a new way. The pride was not about being the child with the biggest bag. It was about matching the gift to the friend. A dragonfly rested on a reed and left again. Across the water the heron stood on one leg, patient as a posted letter, not part of their snack at all. Bastian did not try to call the heron closer. He let the pond stay a pond.

When the tin was empty he showed the ducks his open hands so they would know the treat was finished. They swam back to their circles. No one quacked in a worried way. Bastian rinsed the tin at the outdoor tap and tipped the water onto the herb bed. Inside, the toast was gone and the compost crock wore its lid. The old bread had not been wasted. It had been given two honest jobs.

On the walk back, Bastian met Ioanna near the pump. She had a crust in her hand from her own toast and looked toward the water as if a habit were calling her. Bastian held up his empty tin. "I learned a pond rule," he said. "Bread is not their best food. People can eat the good slices. Dry bits can help the soil. Ducks get oats and peas." Ioanna listened with her crust paused in the air. She nodded and took a bite herself. "Then this crust stays with me," she said. They rinsed their fingers and let the spare splash water the mint.

Bastian checked the compost crock so the lesson would feel finished in his hands, not only in his head. The dry bread sat among leaves and clean eggshells, ready to become garden food later. Nothing had been dropped in the lane as if it were worthless. He patted the lid shut. At the window he watched the pale duck draw a silver line across the pond, full and unhurried. Nature felt less like a place for leftovers and more like a neighbor with clear, kind preferences.

Bastian wrote the pond rule on a card and propped it by the bread box so the next helpful person would see it before the bag. Good slices for toast. Dry ends for compost. Oats and peas for ducks. He read it twice, slowly, until the words felt like a path he could walk without guessing. Outside, the pond kept its soft talk, and he did not need to add a single crumb to feel he had been a good guest.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Bastian told the story of the peas to anyone who would listen. He drew three ducks on a scrap of paper and wrote the words oats and peas underneath in careful letters. Tomorrow, if he visited the pond, he would bring the same right snack and the same calm feet. The meadow could trust him with its ordinary, important rules.
