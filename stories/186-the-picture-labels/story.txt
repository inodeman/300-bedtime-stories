The kitchen shelf smelled like cinnamon, vinegar, and the clean cloth Luna had used for dusting. Jars stood in a row, glass shoulders touching, each full of something red or gold or green. Malik climbed onto a sturdy stool that did not wobble and reached for the apple butter. His bread waited on a plate, soft and pale. "This one," he said, patting a jar with a gold lid. Luna's stomach made a small dip. Yesterday, while she dusted, she had lifted every jar and put them back by color, because the colors looked pretty. She had not kept their places.

"Wait," she said. The word came out thinner than she wanted. Malik paused, fingers on the lid. Afternoon light made a bright bar across the counter. A drip from the faucet tapped the sink, patient as a clock. Luna could have stayed quiet. The jars all looked proud and innocent. But Malik's bread smelled like toast, and a surprise of spicy pickle would not feel like a joke if he did not expect it. Honesty tugged her sleeve. She stepped closer until her socks met the sunny patch on the floor.

"I moved them," she said. "All of them. I wanted the reds together and the golds together. I do not remember which one is apple butter." Her ears felt hot. Malik set the jar down carefully. The glass made a tiny polite clink. He was quiet for a breath, then he said, "Thank you for telling me before the bite." The thank-you surprised Luna more than a frown would have. She let out a long breath that stirred a crumb on the plate. The truth had not broken the afternoon. It had saved the bread.

Mama came in, drying a spoon. "I heard a brave sentence," she said. "We can sort this together. No one tastes from a big spoon, and I will open the lids so the edges do not surprise your fingers." They nodded. Mama opened the gold-lid jar. A sharp, pickly smell rose, bright as a trumpet. Malik leaned in and laughed. "That is not apple butter. That is the crunchy kind." Luna's shoulders dropped. One mystery was already speaking. Mama dabbed a rice-grain of it on a spoon for each child. It tingled, tasty and bold. They drank cool water and smiled.

The next jar smelled like cooked apples and a little cinnamon. "Hello," Malik said to it, as if it were a neighbor. They found pear slices, mild and pale, and a green herb jelly that tasted like the garden after rain. Mama kept the lids loose only while they looked, then closed them until they clicked. Luna took blank labels from the drawer. They were plain paper, a little fuzzy at the edges. "Words and pictures," she said. "If I forget letters, the picture still tells the truth." Malik drew an apple with a smile. Luna drew a pickle with tiny spark lines. They did not draw spark lines on the apple. That would have been another mix-up.

They taped the labels on with Mama watching the glass. The tape lay flat and clear. Luna wrote "apple" and "pickle" in slow letters under the pictures. Malik added "pear" and a small leaf for the herb jelly. Then Luna put the jars back, not by pretty color, but by the labels, with space between them so nobody had to guess. The shelf looked less like a rainbow and more like a map. She liked it better. Maps were for finding things. Rainbows could stay in the sky.

"I moved them because I thought pretty was the same as right," Luna said. She wanted the whole truth, not only the first part. Malik spread apple butter on his toast. It shone softly. He cut the toast in half with a table knife Mama handed him, handle first, and gave Luna the other half. "Pretty is nice," he said. "Right is nicer when we are hungry." The cinnamon smell finally matched the jar. Crumbs fell onto the plate like tiny confetti for an honest day.

They made one extra label that said "ask Mama" and kept it in the drawer for any jar that did not have a picture yet. Luna stuck a small drawing of an open mouth beside it, a reminder to wonder out loud. Malik licked a cinnamon crumb from his thumb and laughed when it stuck. The kitchen felt orderly and kind, the way a true sentence feels when you finally say it aloud and someone passes you half of the toast.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the newly labeled shelf. Each picture faced the room, apple, pickle, pear, and leaf, telling the truth even when the children were in another room. Luna touched the pickle label and smiled at the spark lines she had drawn so carefully. Malik turned the stool back under the counter until its legs sat square. Outside, evening settled in a blue hush, and inside, the jars clicked faintly as the house cooled, a quiet chorus of things put right. The toast plate, empty now, still smelled like cinnamon.
