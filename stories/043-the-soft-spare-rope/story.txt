Sylvie stood at the near end of the rope footbridge with a coil of soft spare rope over her shoulder. The bridge was the same friendly bridge she crossed on bright days, with oak planks spaced so fox paws and small boots could find them. This morning one side rope sagged in a loose loop, not broken, just tired, and it swayed when the warm breeze pushed it. Sylvie's stomach made a small flutter. She put her hand on the coil. "I can make a steady guide," she said. "I will go slowly, and I will keep my eyes on the planks I know."

A gentle fox waited on the moss, watching the sagging loop with a calm face. Sylvie knelt and showed the spare rope. "It is soft, so it will not scratch paws," she said. "If we add it beside the loose one, the bridge will feel sure again." The fox touched the rope with its nose and stepped onto the first plank to show it was willing. Sylvie stood, breathed in the green smell of ferns, and stepped after it. The bridge hummed a little under her boots, a sound she knew, like a big quiet string singing under the oaks.

Halfway across, the breeze lifted her hair and tugged the sagging loop. Sylvie stopped and held the rail that was still firm. Her heart tapped faster, but her feet stayed where she put them. "I am all right," she told the fox. "I am only noticing the wind." The fox sat on a plank and waited, which helped more than any hurry could. Sylvie uncoiled part of the spare rope and passed one end around the sturdy middle post. Her fingers remembered the knot she had practiced at her mushroom cottage table, over and over, until the loops felt like a song.

She tied a round, tidy knot and tested it with a gentle tug. The soft rope lay along the tired one like a friend walking beside a friend. She tied the far end near the next post, humming so her breath stayed even. The fox shifted to give her room and kept its tail clear of the knot. "Thank you for not rushing me," Sylvie said. Below the bridge, ferns held a pale firefly that was resting through the day, a tiny lamp not needed yet. Seeing it made Sylvie smile. The wood was ordinary and wonderful at the same time, and her hands were doing a real job.

When the new guide rope was firm enough to hold, Sylvie tried it. She walked back to the middle and wrapped her palm around the soft rope. It did not wobble like the tired loop. It felt warm from the sun and a little fuzzy against her skin. "This is better," she said. Her flutter had become a steady feeling, the kind that shows up when a task is true and your fingers are already solving it. She looked along the planks and felt proud in a quiet way. Pride, she thought, could sit beside care without pushing anyone else off the bridge.

The fox crossed to the far side, then back again, trying the guide with a light paw. It chuffed, a happy sound in its throat. Sylvie laughed. "You are the inspector of knots." She checked every tie again, counting them out loud so she would not skip one. She left the old rope in place because it was not ruined. It only needed company. Fixing did not mean throwing a thing away. It meant adding care where care had grown thin. She coiled what was left of the spare rope and hung it on the end post for the next walker who might want an extra handhold.

They rested after in the picnic clearing. Sylvie shared acorn biscuits and cool berry water from a small oak-bark cup. Her shoulders felt loose now, as if the knots had untied something inside her too. "I thought courage would feel like a shout," she told the fox. "It felt more like tying a knot while my heart tapped its own little drum." The fox leaned against her boot for a moment, a warm weight. Around them the oaks made a high green roof. A paper map stuck from Sylvie's pocket, and she smoothed it flat and drew a tiny extra line beside the bridge, a mark that meant the guide rope was ready.

On the way home they crossed once more, slower than usual, enjoying the sure feel under their palms and paws. Sylvie greeted the ferns and the moss on the posts as if they were neighbors. She did not look down with worry. She looked down with interest at the dotted light and the green fans. The fox's paws made soft thumps on the planks, a rhythm she matched with her steps. When they reached her mushroom cottage, Sylvie hung the rest of the coil by the door where she could see it and remember what steady hands could do on a breezy morning.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Sylvie washed the bark cup and set it upside down to dry. She could still feel the shape of the knot in her fingers, a little ridge of memory that made her smile. The gentle fox curled on the step for a short visit, nose under its tail, content to be near the round door. Sylvie spoke through the open window. "Thank you for walking with me." The fox's ear twitched, which she took as a kind you are welcome. Courage, she decided, was a spare rope carried before it was needed, and a calm step taken even when a breeze tried to hurry her heart.
