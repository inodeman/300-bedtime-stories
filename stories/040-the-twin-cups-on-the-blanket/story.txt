Lotte spread the picnic blanket where the dune dipped like a kind lap. From there the friendly lighthouse was a white post in the afternoon, and the little wooden boats rested in the cove. She set out two tin cups, twins, sky-blue outside and silvery inside. One for her. One for her friend from the blue cottage, who came on bright days for a sip of something warm. Lotte polished the rims with her sleeve.

The second cup wobbled. Lotte pressed the rim. It rocked, tick-tick, because of a dent in the bottom she had not noticed on the path. Perhaps the cup had met a stone in the basket. It would not sit flat, which meant it might spill on the blanket and on the ritual. Lotte felt a small sting of dismay. She could hide the dented cup and use a chipped mug from home, but the twins were the whole point. They matched. They said you belong here before any words did. She turned the cup, hoping the dent would become harmless. Tick-tick. "I see it too," Lotte said. "No pretending."

Her friend arrived with wind-tossed hair and a smile that was already a hello. "You saved the corner with the view," her friend said, dropping onto the blanket. "I saved the cup with the problem," Lotte answered, and showed the wobble instead of hiding it behind the flask. They put their heads together, two investigators of tin. "We could pound it," her friend offered, then frowned. "Pounding might make a new dent. What if we give it a shoe?" Lotte opened the napkin basket. A folded square of cloth, thick enough, went under the shy side of the cup. The tick stopped. The surface stayed level. "A shoe," Lotte said. "You are brilliant in ordinary ways."

They poured barley tea from the flask. Steam made a small weather over the blanket. Lotte waited, because the clink was a door they opened together, not a sound she grabbed first. Her friend lifted the other cup. "To the cove," they said, and metal met metal with a bright kind ping. A drop jumped and landed on Lotte's wrist, warm and funny. They laughed. The dent had not ruined the ritual. It had added a story the ritual could carry. Friendship, Lotte thought, was not the same as everything being undented. It was bringing the dent into the circle and finding a napkin shoe together.

They traded the first stories. Lotte's was about a starfish she had watched from the rock, orange and calm, not touched. Her friend's was about a gull that had led her away from stepping on a soft nest of weed where tiny crabs were hiding, simply by standing in the better path and refusing to move until she understood. "Helpful," Lotte said. "Not bossy. There is a difference." They sipped. The tea tasted nutty and sweet. Below, the tide chart nailed to the fence fluttered once and settled. They did not need to go anywhere. The point of the cups was staying.

A breeze tried to steal the napkin shoe. Lotte caught it. Her friend put a smooth shell on the cloth's corner, empty and clean, a paperweight from the shore. "Now the shoe has a buckle," her friend said. They clinked a second time, smaller, a private echo. Lotte felt the sting of dismay finish leaving. She had wanted a perfect pair of cups. She had a pair of friends instead, girl and cup and girl and cup, all slightly real. She told her friend the truth about almost hiding the dent. "I am glad you showed me," her friend said. "I do not need you to be a shop window. I need you to be on this blanket."

They shared a bun, picking out the raisins and giving each other the best middle pieces without keeping score. A gull received a pea-sized crumb on a stone and flew off still itself. Lotte's shoulders loosened. She asked about next week and heard that next week was wanted. Wanted was a warm word. They planned nothing grander than the same hollow, the same toast, perhaps a drawing of the boats if the light was good. Grand plans were not required. A ritual worked because it was small enough to keep. The dented cup sat steady on its shoe, shining like the undented one if you did not demand perfection from every angle.

When the tea was gone they tapped the cups upside down over the sand, away from the blanket, and listened to the last drop fall. Lotte folded the napkin shoe and tucked it inside the dented cup so they would not forget the fix. Her friend carried the flask. Lotte carried both cups, a soft clatter against her chest. Halfway down the path they paused to watch a wooden boat turn toward home. "We did our toast," her friend said. "We did," Lotte said. The words were small and full. She felt lucky in a way that did not need to be loud. Someone would come because she had saved a corner. She would go because a corner was saved for her.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the twin cups Lotte set side by side on the sill, one of them wearing its folded napkin like a quiet secret. She looked at the pair and then out toward the blue cottage, where another window held another light. Friendship did not mind a dent. It minded whether you saved the place, told the truth, and clinked anyway. Lotte touched the rims so they met one more time, gentle, no spill, no audience. "To the cove," she whispered, and the lantern seemed to answer with a steady glow, keeping both cups in the same warm circle until the next bright day.
