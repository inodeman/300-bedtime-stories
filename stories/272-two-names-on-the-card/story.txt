The card for Grandma Soraya was almost done, and it was beautiful. Tomas had drawn the balcony pots. Cousin Joy had drawn the flowers twining between them, each petal careful and different. They had worked at the kitchen table while soup warmed, trading pencils without grabbing. Now Tomas held the card and imagined Grandma Soraya’s face. He wanted, suddenly and sharply, to hear her say that he had made the whole thing. The wanting surprised him. It felt pinchy, like shoes a size too small.

Joy was washing honey off her fingers at the sink. She hummed. She did not see Tomas’s hand hover over the bottom of the card, where both names were supposed to go. He wrote Tomas in his best letters. He stopped before Joy. The pinchy feeling had a name if he was brave enough to use it. It was dishonest, or at least almost dishonest. He had not lied out loud yet. He was standing on the edge of a lie, and the edge felt wobbly. His stomach knew before his mouth did.

He looked at Joy’s flowers. They were the part he hoped Grandma would notice first. Without them, his pots were just round pots. With them, the card was a garden. Tomas put the pencil down. “Joy,” he said. His voice came out scratchy. “I started to write only my name. I wanted the praise to be all mine. That is not true, and it does not feel good. Your flowers are half the garden. Will you come write your name with me?” Joy turned, hands dripping, eyes wide. Then she smiled in a way that was surprised and kind. “Thank you for telling me before the card dried,” she said.

They stood shoulder to shoulder. Tomas’s name stayed, because his pots were real and his work was real. Joy wrote Joy beside it, the same size, not smaller. She added a tiny leaf between the names so they looked like neighbors. Tomas felt the pinchy shoes come off. What arrived was relief, and also a cleaner kind of pride. “I feel honest now,” he said. “I felt selfish for a minute. Selfish can knock, but it does not get to sign the card alone.” Joy bumped his elbow. “I felt a flash of worry when I saw only your name. Your sentence fixed the worry. That was a good sentence.”

They did not hide the almost-mistake. When Grandma Soraya came to the table, Tomas told her the truth in simple words. “I wanted all the credit. I told Joy. We both made this.” Grandma Soraya looked at the card for a long time. She traced a pot with her eyes and a flower with her smile. “Then I get to thank two artists,” she said. “A garden is better with both. And I am proud of the truth as well as the drawing.” She hugged them one at a time, equal hugs, which made Tomas’s chest feel roomy. Joy’s cheeks shone. The soup smell wrapped the moment up like a napkin.

After bowls and bread, the cousins made a second tiny card, this one for themselves. It said, Both names, always, when both hands worked. They taped it inside the pencil cup so the next project would see it. Tomas read it twice. He wanted the reminder, because the pinchy feeling might visit again when something turned out pretty. Joy said, “If it visits me, you can ask me who else held a pencil.” They made a plan that sounded like friendship and honesty holding hands. Neither of them called it a lecture. It was just how they wanted their table to be.

Grandma Soraya set the garden card on the shelf where the lantern would find it in the evening. Tomas and Joy washed brushes and watched green water swirl and fade. Tomas said, “The flowers were the hardest part, and they were yours.” Joy said, “The pots were steady, and they were yours.” They did not trade those sentences to be fancy. They traded them because true words tasted better than a lonely name. The kitchen felt clear, as if a window had been opened inside their chests. Later, while the soup pot soaked, Tomas practiced the honest sentence one more time, not because he was in trouble, but because he wanted it ready. “I wanted all the credit, and that was not true.” Joy listened as if it were a useful song. She practiced her own sentence. “I felt worried, and I am glad you told me.” They said the sentences to the wooden spoons, which made them laugh, and then they said them to each other again, which made them steady. Grandma Soraya, passing with clean bowls, added a sentence of her own. “I am glad this kitchen tells the truth while the soup is still warm.” The pinchy feeling had no shoes left to wear.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across both names on the card. Tomas and Joy watched the light touch the leaf between them. Honesty had kept the garden whole, and the glow made the shelf look like a place where praise was wide enough for everyone who had helped.
