Flora heard the meadow before she saw its smallest workers. A warm hum rose from the clover, steady as a song someone hummed while sweeping. She loved questions the way some children love pebbles, and she already had three. What were the bees carrying? Why did they choose purple flowers more than white? Could she see a stripe if she stood very still? She picked up her pencil and a scrap of stiff paper and started toward the hum.

The grown-up walked with her, which was the rule for bee visits. "We can be curious from the path," the grown-up said. "The path is our place. The clover is their busy kitchen. We do not touch bees, we do not wave at their flowers, and we do not look for a hive. Watching is a full adventure." Flora nodded so hard her pencil tapped her chin. She wanted to know, and she also wanted to be the kind of guest the meadow would invite again.

They stopped where the grass was short and the clover began several long steps away. Flora could see gold-brown bodies moving from bloom to bloom. She did not creep closer when a bee lifted and turned. She bent her knees a little so her eyes were kinder to the scene, and she held her hands behind her back. The hum was clearer now, not angry, just occupied. It sounded like many tiny plans happening at once.

"Stripes," Flora whispered. She drew a small oval, then bands of dark and light. She drew a flower as a cup. She watched one bee land, almost disappear into the petals, and come out dusty at the legs. "Those dusty spots are how they carry pollen," the grown-up said. "Pollen is flower dust that helps new flowers later. The bees are not making a mess. They are doing their work." Flora added two little baskets to her drawing and labeled them with the word pollen in letters she could read herself.

She counted visits the way she counted stairs. One flower, two flowers, three. The same bee did not stay for a chat. It had a route. Another bee chose a different cup. Flora's paper filled with arrows. Her curiosity, which sometimes felt like a pull on her sleeves, settled into looking. A question answered by eyes did not need fingers. When a bee drifted nearer to the path, Flora stayed quiet and let it pass like a boat that had its own river.

She wondered aloud about the white flowers that stood a little apart. "Maybe they smell different," she said. "Maybe they already had visitors. Maybe I do not know yet." The grown-up smiled. "That last answer is a real answer. Curious people are allowed to leave a space where an answer will grow." Flora wrote I do not know yet under the white bloom in her picture. She liked the honesty of the line.

Before they left, Flora wanted one stem for the kitchen jar. Her hand even rose. Then she looked at how many bees still needed the patch. She lowered her hand. "The bouquet can be the drawing," she said. "The flowers can stay on duty." They stepped back along the path. Rabbits kept to the hedge. The heron, far at the pond, did not belong to this lesson, and Flora did not mix her wonders into a pile. One wonder at a time was plenty.

At the porch she shaded the stripes more carefully and told Bramble the goat, from a respectful distance, that hats were Alba's story and pollen was hers. Bramble chewed and seemed to agree that friends could have different interests. Flora set the paper under a smooth stone so the breeze would not steal it.

On the porch Flora told Orhan what she had learned without making the bees sound like a riddle he had to solve up close. She showed the arrows, the pollen baskets, and the line that said she did not know yet. Orhan tied his own notebook shut and stayed on the step with her, which was another kind of distance done right. "Looking is a real visit," he said. Flora added a tiny path to the bottom of the page so the picture would remember where people belong. The grown-up brought water, and they drank it slowly while the hum went on without them, steady and safe and far.

Flora rinsed her pencil jar and left the drawing unpinned so she could add one more note in the morning if a new question arrived. She already knew she would not add a closer view. The path had given her stripes, pollen, and a honest empty space, which was a full page for one day. When a bee-like hum seemed to drift near the herbs, she only listened from her chair. The sound moved on. Her curiosity waved, in a manner of speaking, and stayed seated.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the finished drawing. Flora traced the arrows with her eyes and listened. She could not hear the hum anymore, and that was fine. The bees were still working, far enough away to be safe and close enough to be remembered. Curiosity, she decided, is a way of saying I care what you are doing, and I will not interrupt it.
