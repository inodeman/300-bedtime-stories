Queisha lifted a handful of dry onion skins from the bowl on the counter and let them rustle back down. They were thin as moth wings, copper and gold and pale brown, and they smelled sweet and sharp, like soup just beginning. Rowan held a square of white cotton, soft from many washings, and rubbed it against his cheek. "Grandpa says the skins remember the garden," Rowan said. "Even after the onion has gone into dinner, the shirts of the onion can still share their color." Queisha smiled. "Then today the cloth can wear a sunset that grew in the dirt."

Grandpa filled a pot with water and the skins and set it on the stove. The children did not touch the hot handle. Their job was the cool part and the watching part. They stood on a sturdy stool at a safe side of the counter while the water slowly turned the color of tea. Steam rose in a soft ribbon and fogged the window a little. The kitchen smelled like onion and warm cotton, a cozy smell, not a harsh one. "Listen," Queisha said. The pot made a small blurp, gentle as a frog in a far pond. Rowan grinned. "The color is talking."

When Grandpa said the dye had had a long, quiet steep, he lifted the pot off the heat and set it aside to cool. Waiting was hard. Queisha wanted to dunk the cloth at once. She held it in her lap instead and traced the hem. "The garden did not rush the onion," she said. "We can be like the garden." They went to the window and looked at the real garden, where green tops had once stood in a row. A bee nosed a leftover blossom. Soil in a pot smelled damp and dark when Rowan cracked the window. Nature felt close, even in a kitchen, even in a handful of skins that most people would have tossed aside.

After the liquid was cool enough for Grandpa to call it friendly, he poured it through a sieve into a big bowl. The children took turns stirring with a wooden spoon, slow circles, while the cloth went in and sank like a little cloud. Color crept into the white. At first it was only a blush. Then it became a warm gold, deeper in the folds. Queisha's fingers, dipped at the edge with permission, came up tea-colored. "I am wearing the onion too," she laughed. Rowan pressed the cloth gently under, then let it float. "No wringing yet," Grandpa said. "Let it drink."

They lifted the cloth together when Grandpa nodded, holding the corners so it would not drag on the floor. Cool dye dotted their wrists. They rinsed the cloth in a basin of clear water until the water ran pale, and still the cotton stayed gold, as if it had decided. The wet cloth was heavier and smelled clean and faintly of onion soup. They carried it to the line on the porch. Pins clicked. The square hung and dripped onto the step, each drip a tiny gold coin that vanished. Wind moved it. Light moved through it. For a moment the porch seemed to wear a piece of afternoon.

A corner had stayed paler, where the cloth had been folded tight. Rowan noticed. "Should we hide that side?" Queisha shook her head. "We can give it another dip now that we know." They asked Grandpa, and the cool pot still had color enough. They soaked just the pale corner, then rinsed again. The repair was small and kind. When the cloth dried, the corner matched well enough, with a soft ring that looked like a moon behind cloud. "That ring is the truth of the fold," Rowan said. "I like a cloth that tells what happened."

They spread the dry square on the table. It was golden, light, and a little wrinkled in a way that invited a hand to smooth it. Queisha folded it into a napkin for the bread basket. The bread, set on top, looked like it sat on a field at sundown. Rowan sniffed. "Almost no onion smell now. Just cloth and a sweet hint of the skins." They did not announce that they had invented color. They thanked the onions, which felt silly and also right, and they thanked Grandpa for the stove part. Queisha said, "The garden gave the gold. We gave the stirring and the patience."

Outside, the onion tops that remained swayed. Inside, the skins in the sieve looked pale, their color generously given away. Queisha saved a few dry skins in a jar, not to hoard, but to remember the rustle. Rowan placed the jar by the window where the skins could glow even without a pot. Nature had come indoors as a color you could fold. Their hands were clean again, only a faint tea stain in one nail, a tiny souvenir.

The lantern by the window glowed softly through the golden cloth when they held a corner up, and the light turned even warmer, like honey in a spoon. Queisha and Rowan stood quiet, proud in a small way, watching garden color rest in cotton. The napkin was finished. It would catch crumbs. It would remember onions and sunlight and a pot that had talked in blurps.
