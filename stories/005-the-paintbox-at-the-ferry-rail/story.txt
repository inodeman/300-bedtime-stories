Pim sat on an upturned crate at the harbor rail with a tin paintbox open on her knees. The cakes of color looked like candy that was not for eating: sea blue, leaf green, a shy gray, a dot of gold. Uncle Sol coiled a rope nearby. It smelled of salt and sun-warm hemp. The little ferry, the Merry Minnow, rested against the dock with its white side ticking gently in the swell. "Paint the water," Uncle Sol said. "It is the biggest thing here, and it never sits still long enough to be bossy." Pim dipped her brush in a jar of clear water. The first stroke on the paper was a flat blue stripe. She frowned at it in a thoughtful way, not a cross one. The real water was not only blue.

She looked again. Near the pilings the harbor was green, like bottle glass held up to leaves. Farther out it turned silver where the sun laid a path. Under the ferry's shadow it was a deep calm gray. A scrap of orange peel floated by, and around it the water wrinkled into little moving lines. Pim's stripe suddenly seemed too sure of itself. "I think I painted a blanket," she said. "The water is more like a song." Captain Idris, checking a clipboard on the deck, heard her and leaned on the rail. His cap was faded to the color of oatmeal. "Songs have more than one note," he said. "So do harbors. Take your time. The Minnow does not sail for a while."

Pim rinsed her brush until the jar clouded, then poured that water over the side, watching it disappear into the bigger water without a fuss. She mixed green with a breath of gold. She laid a new wash beside the blue, letting the paper stay wet so the colors could meet and make a third color she had not planned. That meeting looked like the edge of the shadow under the boat. She smiled. A gull stood on a post and tucked one foot up, as if saving it for later. Pim did not paint the gull yet. She wanted the water to be true first. Her sleeve smelled like the dock, a clean wet smell, and a drop of green landed on her thumb like a tiny leaf.

Uncle Sol pointed with his chin, never letting go of the rope. "See the stripe out there, where the current from the river meets the tide? It is browner. River water carries hills in it, ground fine as flour." Pim leaned, careful of the crate. Yes. A soft brown ribbon moved through the green, slow as a cat. She added a thin line, then softened it with a clean wet brush so it would not look stuck on. "The hills are visiting the sea," she said. Captain Idris nodded. "And the sea is polite enough to make room." The ferry's bell gave one low practice tone, round and gentle, not a hurry. Someone on the shore laughed. Pim's paper began to look like a place you could step into, if paper could hold a shoe.

A cloud crossed the sun. All the colors cooled at once, as if someone had laid a clean handkerchief over the harbor. Pim stopped her hand. She watched. The silver path faded. The green grew quieter. Then the cloud passed, and the silver came back, brighter than before. "It changed while I was holding the brush," she said, full of wonder. "I do not have to make it stay one way." She painted a second small picture in the corner of the page, a cooler harbor, and left the first one sunny. Two truths, side by side. Uncle Sol peered over. "That is honest painting. Most folks pick one minute and pretend it is the whole day."

When the passengers began to gather, Pim closed the water jar so no one would tip it. She showed the page to a small child holding a wooden horse. "The water wears different coats," Pim said. The child whispered, "I like the brown ribbon." Pim tore a blank strip from the edge of her paper, a piece she did not need, and gave the child a gold dot of paint on it to carry, dry enough not to smear. "For your horse to remember the sun path," she said. The child beamed. Captain Idris touched his cap. "Art and ferries both carry people," he said. "Yours carries eyes." Pim felt tall on her crate, though she had not grown an inch.

She stayed to watch the Merry Minnow ease away, ropes coiled, engine a soft purr. The wake spread in a fan of lace. Colors braided and then smoothed. Pim did not try to paint the whole leaving. She added three curved lines and stopped while the picture still had room to breathe. Uncle Sol hung the last fender, the rubber bumper, against the dock. "You looked long enough," he said. "That is a skill. The harbor will be here tomorrow, wearing yet another coat." Pim packed the tin. The cakes of paint glistened, a little used, which made them friendlier. Her paper dried in the air, edges lifting, then settling.

They walked up the plank street toward home as the shops lit their ordinary lamps. Pim held the painting flat between two bits of cardboard Uncle Sol had found in the chart room. She could still smell the wet paint, green and a little like rain. At the corner she looked back. The empty berth rocked with light. It was hard to say which color was the true one, and she liked that hardness. It was not a problem anymore. It was an invitation to look again.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when they reached the cottage above the chandlery. Pim set the painting on a chair to finish drying, sunny harbor beside cloudy harbor, brown ribbon and all. She washed the brush until the water ran clear, a small kindness to tomorrow's colors. Wonder, she thought, was not a spark that ended. It was a harbor that kept changing its coat, and a person who stayed at the rail long enough to see it. She dried her hands, drank a cup of warm milk, and let the day rock her gently, the way a good boat rocks when it is safely tied.
