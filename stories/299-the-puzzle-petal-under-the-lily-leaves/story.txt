Ruya knelt at the plaza fountain, where the water was shallow and the tiles showed like pale moons. A wooden lily puzzle floated in a loose ring: five smooth petals that could drift and then be gathered into a flower again. Children liked to take it apart kindly and watch it become whole. Today Ruya counted four petals in the ring. The fifth, painted with a soft pink edge, had slid under a cluster of real lily leaves. Only a corner showed, then hid. "I can get it with a stick," she said, already reaching for a long twig.

The fountain minder, a grown-up with rolled sleeves, lifted a hand, not to scold, but to offer a pause. "A stick can push the petal deeper into the stems. The water here walks in a slow circle. If we wait, it often brings lost light things back to the rim." Ruya held the twig and did not drop it in. Waiting sounded like doing nothing, and the petal was right there, almost. "What if it stays under the leaves until tomorrow?" she asked. "Then we will still have been kind to it," said the minder. "But watch first. Tell me what the corner does."

Ruya set the twig on the dry tiles, parallel to the rim, a tool at rest. She put her chin on her folded hands. The fountain's small jet made a polite sound. The wooden petals in the ring turned like slow boats. Under the leaves, the pink corner appeared, vanished, appeared a little more to the left. "It is walking," she whispered. Her knees wanted to bounce. She pressed her shoes flat. The clock tower chimed one kind note, and still she watched. The petal's corner caught the light like a small shell.

"It is closer," she said. "Not close enough for hands." The minder nodded. "Patience is not a frozen person. It is a person who lets the next moment arrive." Ruya breathed with the water's circle. She noticed other things while she waited: a cork boat with a folded leaf sail resting on the far rim, a paper sun dried on the bench, the way the real lilies held still while the wooden one traveled. The pink corner cleared the leaves. The whole petal turned, lazy and safe, into open water. Ruya's hands lifted and then stopped. "Not yet," she told herself. "If I splash, I make a new trip for it."

The petal drifted along the tile curve and touched the rim near her knee with a soft tap. She slid her fingers under it and lifted. It was cool, light, and unhurt. "You came," she said, as if it had chosen courtesy. The minder smiled. "You let it." Ruya fitted the petal into the ring. The five wooden pieces made a lily again, loose enough to float, snug enough to be a flower. She did not spin them into a whirl to celebrate. She gave them one gentle turn and watched them stay together.

"I thought fast was helpful," she said. "Fast would have been a stick and a farther petal." The minder rinsed a leaf that had stuck to the wood. "You tried waiting, which is a kind of trying again, only the trying is quiet." Ruya liked that sentence. She told it to a younger child who arrived with a grown-up and wanted to poke the fountain. "We can watch the circle," Ruya said. "It brings things if they are light and we do not boss them." The child knelt and watched. They saw a tiny petal-shaped reflection, only light, travel the same path. Both of them smiled when it reached the rim and disappeared into sparkle.

Ruya dried her fingers. The wooden lily floated, complete. She felt the twig still on the tiles and moved it to the minder's bundle of safe sticks, not for poking puzzles. Some tools were for other jobs. The job today had been eyes and time. The plaza sounded ordinary and good: a ferry bell far off, a page turning on the barge, someone laughing once at the mural. Ruya's hurry had sat down beside her and become company instead of a boss.

She stayed for one more slow circle of the flower, to be sure the petal would not slip its ring. It stayed. She stood and shook the pins of waiting out of her legs. They tingled, and then they felt like her legs again. Patience had a body feeling, she decided: still feet, busy eyes, hands that could wait their turn. She could use that at the studio or the orchard or anywhere a small thing hid.

Ruya told the fountain minder, "I will leave the twig for jobs that are not puzzles." The minder said the bundle would be glad of an honest stick. Ruya watched the lily make one last slow turn and did not reach. Waiting, she decided, could be a finished action, not an empty gap. Her hands stayed dry. The petal stayed in the flower. That was the whole success, quiet as water.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the fountain minder's window as the early evening gathered in a friendly way. Ruya looked from that glow to the five-petal lily, whole on the water. Waiting for the slow circle had brought the puzzle petal back without a splash. She took her grown-up's hand, the twig left behind, her patience coming along.
