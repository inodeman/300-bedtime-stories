Arun heard the sound before he saw any musician. It was a small bright note, then a hum under it, then something like a flute far away. The three did not crowd one another. They braided. He stood on Auntie Priya's porch with his lantern still unlit, the paper shade cool in his hands. The town's evening festival had begun in its quiet way: doors open, food shared on steps, lanterns walking slowly toward the square. No one called for silence, because the evening was already soft.

"Where is that bell living?" Arun asked. Auntie Priya tilted her head. "It might be living in more than one place. Shall we walk and find out, without hurrying the answer?" Arun nodded. They lit his lantern with a match that smelled like a tiny spark of pine. The light made the porch look honeyed. He carried the lantern low so he could still hear over its faint paper tick. Their shoes found the lane. A bowl of roasted chickpeas was offered from a step. Arun took three, salty and warm, and chewed quietly so his ears could work.

At the first corner the bright note grew clearer. It came from Mr. Bell's wind chime, a row of short copper tubes under the eaves. The breeze was small. The tubes touched only sometimes. Ting. Then a rest. Then ting. "Found it," Arun whispered, but he did not feel finished. The hum was still somewhere else, and the flute still farther. Mr. Bell sat in a chair with a cup of tea. "You caught my chime being shy," he said. "It only speaks when the air asks." Arun smiled. "I think it is singing with other voices. I am going to listen for the rest of the braid."

They walked on. The hum grew in Auntie Priya's direction of pointing, toward a kitchen window where Mrs. Adeyemi washed spoons. She was humming without words, a low comfortable line, the kind a person hums when the water is warm and the work is good. The spoons chimed more sharply, but her hum was the long thread. Arun did not interrupt the phrase. When she paused, he said, "Your humming fits the copper chime. I heard you both from the porch." She looked surprised and pleased. "I thought I was only keeping myself company. How nice to be part of a larger quiet."

To test the braid, Arun covered one ear gently with his palm, then the other. The chime jumped forward when the flute side was covered. The hum grew when he turned toward the kitchen window. Nothing disappeared. The sounds only took turns being near. "They are like lanterns," he told Auntie Priya. "When you face one, it looks brightest, but the others are still walking." She said that was a fine theory for a festival night. A moth visited his lantern and left again, unbothered. Arun was glad he had not chased it. Wonder did not need to be caught in a jar.

The flute was the last to show itself. A girl was not playing it. Miss Pell sat on a stool outside the bakery, playing so the notes went down the lane like polite guests. When the breeze moved, the copper chime answered her phrase with one bright dot. Mrs. Adeyemi's hum, still faintly reaching, held the floor. Arun stopped and shut his eyes. He could hold all three in his attention the way a hand holds three smooth stones. "They do not know they are a trio," he said. Auntie Priya answered, "Maybe they know in their own way. The air knows."

He opened his eyes. Lanterns were passing, and each paper shade seemed to glow in time with the slow phrases, though that might have been his wonder painting the rhythm. He did not need to be certain. They sat on the bakery step and shared a piece of soft bread with butter. The bread tore like cloud. Butter salted his lip. Arun kept listening while he ate, which made the food taste more awake. "If I hum, will I spoil it?" he asked. Miss Pell shook her head. "Add a very small thread, and stop if the braid feels crowded."

Arun hummed three notes, no more, matching Mrs. Adeyemi's key as best he could. The chime tinged once, as if agreeing. He stopped and let the flute finish the sentence. His chest felt wide and calm. Wonder, he decided, was not a puzzle that ended when you found the first bell. It was the pleasure of noticing how sounds visited one another. Auntie Priya brushed a crumb from his knee. "You followed without grabbing," she said. "That is good listening." Arun swung his lantern gently and watched the light sway in the same tempo.

On the bakery step he drew the braid in the air with one finger: a high dot for copper, a low curve for the hum, a long line for the flute. Miss Pell watched the line and played it back as a softer phrase. The match was not perfect, and the almost-match was beautiful. Arun laughed once, quietly, at the pleasure of being answered. Crumbs dotted his knee. He brushed them to the step for the morning birds, not into the lane where feet would mash them. The evening felt larger than the street and smaller than a secret, which was exactly the size he liked best of all on a calm night.

They thanked Miss Pell and walked back past the humming window, where they waved rather than knocked. Mr. Bell lifted his teacup as they passed the chime. At home Arun set his lantern down and tried to remember the braid well enough to tell it. He described copper, water-hum, and bakery flute. Auntie Priya said the house cricket might join tomorrow. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and Arun held still beside it, listening to the tiny tick of cooling glass and the far, almost imagined ting of copper in the night.
