Paloma carried the wooden bucket with both hands because the glass set into its bottom made it precious. Uncle had fitted that round window himself, smooth at the edges, clear as a held breath. "It is for looking," he had said on the cottage step. "Not for scooping up anyone's home." Paloma liked that rule. She also liked questions, and the tide pool ahead was full of them. It flashed and wrinkled. Every time she thought she saw a starfish, the surface scribbled the picture away. Gulls stitched white loops in the sky above the friendly lighthouse, calling in bright helpful notes as if the morning had good news.

She knelt on a flat rock where her knees could be steady. The pool smelled like cool stone and a pinch of salt. Tiny waves, hardly waves at all, kept folding the view. "I know you are in there," Paloma told the water, polite as a guest. She did not poke with a stick. She did not stir with her fingers. Instead she lowered the bucket until the glass kissed the surface and the wooden sides made a little room of quiet. Ripples still bumped her wrists. Then they calmed inside the circle. A window opened under her eyes, and the pool stopped being a blur.

The starfish was the color of a ripe apricot, resting with all five arms easy, not bothered, not performing. Around it, a small shore crab the size of a coin side-stepped under a lip of rock and paused, as if listening. Green weed lifted and settled like hair in a slow dance. Paloma's mouth opened. "Oh," she said. "You were here the whole time. The wiggles were only a curtain." She shifted the bucket a thumb's width, careful not to knock the rock. A spiral shell, empty and pearly, sat near a cluster of tiny snails. She counted the snails in a whisper. She lost count. She started again, grinning.

Uncle crouched nearby but did not lean over her shoulder. "What does the glass say?" he asked. "It says the pool is busier than the sparkle," Paloma answered. "There is a crab wearing the shade like a hat. There is a starfish being very serious about resting." She wanted, for one hot second, to lift the starfish into her palm just to know its weight. Her hand even left the bucket handle. Then she remembered the rule that was also a kindness. Wild arms belonged in wild water. She put her hand back. The wanting did not make her a thief. Noticing it made her a careful scientist of the shore.

A gull landed on the next rock and peered down, as curious as Paloma and just as unwilling to splash the pool into confusion. "You may look," Paloma told it. "We are both guests." The gull blinked a yellow eye and stayed. Paloma tilted her head until she could see the starfish's tiny tube feet, a soft fringe she had never known about. She felt a fizz of happiness that was better than owning. Questions bloomed. Did the crab like morning light? Did the weed taste salty to the snails? She did not need to answer every question by touching. Some answers were simply patterns.

She drew what she saw in a small damp notebook, using a pencil that smudged when her wrist dripped. The starfish became a lopsided star, and she laughed. "I am a better looker than a drawer," she said. Uncle nodded. "Looking is the first art." Paloma moved the bucket to a second pool, then a third, never scraping the glass on the life below. In one pool she found only sand and a dancing thread of light. That was a discovery too. Empty of animals did not mean empty of wonder. She told Uncle about the light thread as if it were a storybook page, and he listened as if it were.

When her knees complained, she sat back and let the bucket rest on dry stone. The glass fogged, then cleared. The real pools went on with their bright private business. Paloma rinsed her fingers in a place where no crab sat and flicked the drops away. "I thought curiosity meant collecting," she said. "Today it meant getting lower and quieter." Uncle tapped the wooden side. "The bucket is a good teacher if we let it stay a window." Paloma agreed. She could still see the apricot star in her mind, more vivid than if she had carried it up the dune in a slosh of water. Memory, she decided, was a softer jar.

They walked the rock line back toward the sand, Paloma matching her steps to the gull's occasional hop ahead. She described the crab's sideways bow again, adding a detail she had almost missed, a speck of shell stuck like a button on its back. Uncle asked what she would wonder tomorrow. "Whether the starfish chooses a new resting spot," she said, "and whether I can find it without being noisy." The cove flashed. The little wooden boats ticked against their ropes. Paloma's questions felt friendly, like pebbles in a pocket, not like holes she had to fill by taking something home.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Paloma set the glass-bottom bucket on the cottage bench to dry. She wiped the rim with a clean cloth and left the window clear for the next look. Outside, the tide would return and rewrite the pools, and that was all right. She had not taken the starfish, the crab, or even the empty spiral shell. She had taken a careful picture in her mind and a page of smudged stars. Curiosity, warm as the lantern, sat beside her. It did not need a captured prize. It only needed another morning and a gentle way to see.
