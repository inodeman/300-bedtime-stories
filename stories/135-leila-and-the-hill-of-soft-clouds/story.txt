The hill was a broad green back that the sky leaned on. Leila climbed with Papa and a picnic blanket rolled under her arm, the kind of climb that made her knees happy and her breath bright. She had a plan. From the top she would point to the town clock, the bakery roof, and the thin blue line of the creek. She had practiced the pointing at home with a drawing. The grass smelled like sun and crushed stems. A grasshopper ticked and vanished. "The top keeps its promises," Papa said, "though sometimes the promises change clothes."

At the top the town was not a picture. A soft mist, pale as milk in tea, lay in the valley and buttoned the roofs out of sight. Leila's pointing finger hovered and then lowered. The disappointment was a small stone in her pocket, not a storm. "I cannot show you the clock," she said. Papa spread the blanket anyway. It made a red island on the grass. "Then the hill can show us what it brought instead," he said. Leila sat. The mist did not smell like much. It felt cool on her cheeks, a cloth the air had forgotten to wring out.

She lay back because the sky above the mist was clear and busy. A cloud like a loaf drifted. Then a cloud like a boat. Then one like a sock with a hole, which made her laugh. She did not need the town for the laugh. "The sky is doing a play," she told Papa. He pointed at a cloud that was only a cloud, and they allowed it to be nothing famous. Wonder, Leila found, was a door that opened when the first door was misted over. She took out her drawing and, instead of crossing out the clock, added a loaf in the margin.

They ate bread and cheese that tasted stronger in the open air. Crumbs invited a polite ant, and Leila moved the napkin so the ant could have a crumb of its own without visiting her knee. The grass under the blanket tickled through the cloth in places. She listened. Far below, once, a bell sounded, round and small, the town clock speaking even though she could not see its face. "There," she whispered. Papa nodded. "The hill gave you the sound instead of the sight. Both are true." Leila closed her eyes and let the next bell, when it came, draw the clock in her ears.

A butterfly the color of toasted bread landed on the blanket's corner and opened its wings as if showing a map. Leila did not touch it. She counted the spots: four, then it closed, then it opened and there were still four. "It knows its own number," she said. The butterfly left on a zigzag that seemed to have a reason. Leila added four dots to her paper. The mist thinned in one place and thickened in another, like a slow animal rolling over. For a moment a roof flashed, red and tiny, and then the milk closed again. She did not chase the glimpse. She thanked it.

Papa told a short tale about a shepherd who had learned the weather by watching sheep ears. Leila was not sure the tale was science, but it was wonder-shaped, and she liked it. She watched a line of trees at the hill's edge comb the mist into strands. The strands looked like scarves hanging to dry. She thought of all the people in the hidden town doing ordinary things, tying parcels, stirring pots, waiting for bells. She was with them by listening. The stone of disappointment had become a pebble she could turn over and set down.

They rolled the blanket when the light went honey-colored. Leila shook it first so the ant's relatives could go home. The hill path downward showed her boots the way. She looked back once. The top was still there, green and unbothered, keeping the sky. "I did not point to the clock," she said, "and I still had a full picnic." Papa agreed. "Plans are baskets. Wonder is what you find when a basket changes." Leila held her drawing, loaf and dots and a bell drawn as a circle with lines. It was a truer map than the one she had practiced.

At home she set the drawing on the table and told Mama about the mist scarves and the butterfly's four spots. Mama listened as if the hill had come indoors. Leila's hair smelled like grass. The problem had been a view that would not perform on cue. Listening and looking up had solved it without pushing the mist aside. She drank water that tasted cold and serious after the cheese. The town bell, she knew, was still doing its job under the milk-soft air.

Leila stood and turned in a slow circle, giving the hill every direction a moment of her eyes. East was brighter. West held a darker hedge. North was the mist. South was the path home, a paler green where feet had been kind to the grass. She memorized the circle so she could tell it at bedtime without leaving anything out. A seed head brushed her wrist and let go of one white fluff, which traveled farther than she could follow. She wished it a soft landing and did not need to know which field. Wonder, she thought, includes a goodbye to a traveling speck. Papa added that the speck was a tiny kite, and Leila agreed without needing a string.

The lantern by the window glowed softly near the drawing of clouds. Leila watched the light and imagined a loaf drifting over a hidden clock. She did not mind that the roofs had stayed shy. The hill had shared its own treasures: a bell, a butterfly, a blanket island, a sky at play. Wonder had been the path when pointing could not be. She let the mist become sleep, gentle and pale, with four bright spots she could still count.
