Paloma pointed at a wide cloud over the meadow. "That one is mine to name first," she said, then heard herself and softened. Davin lay on the blanket with chalk dust still on his fingers from an earlier drawing game. The sky was a gentle blue, and the clouds moved like slow sheep. Paloma felt a small grabby feeling. She had seen the cloud first, and she wanted the best picture to be hers alone. Davin did not grab back. He waited, which gave her room to choose a kinder next sentence.

She took a breath. "I want to share the sky, not spend it," she said. "May we take turns, and may both pictures stay?" Davin sat up at once. "Yes. Sharing a cloud does not break it." Paloma went first. "It is a bridge from the hill to the harbor." Davin went second. "It is also a long loaf, warm and pale." Paloma felt the grabby feeling shrink. The cloud was big enough for a bridge and bread. Her first look was still hers. It had been joined.

They made a shared page on the board. Paloma drew the bridge on the left. Davin drew the loaf on the right. In the middle they left a white space and wrote the word and. Paloma liked that word more than the word only. She passed the chalk without being asked, then Davin passed it back. The turns were short, so nobody held the sky like a closed fist. Above them the cloud slowly grew a bump. "Your bridge has a tower now," Davin said. Paloma grinned. "Your loaf has a raisin. We both get the bump."

A smaller cloud followed, and Paloma noticed the grabby feeling try again. It was a perfect mitten shape, and she wanted it as a private treasure. She told the truth in a simple way. "I feel like keeping this one." Davin nodded. "You can love it first. Then we can still share the saying of it." That idea helped. Paloma described the mitten all the way through, thumb and cuff and soft wool look. When she finished, she invited Davin in. He added that the mitten could wave. Paloma laughed. The picture was still hers, and also theirs. Sharing had not erased her joy. It had given it a friend.

They played a giving game. Paloma offered Davin the next cloud no matter how wonderful it was. Davin offered the one after that back to her. The offers made the meadow feel wide. One cloud was a boat. The next was a whale with a kind eye. Paloma discovered that waiting for her turn was sweet, because she got to enjoy his sentence before making her own. "I thought sharing meant I would have less," she said. "I have more words in my ears now." Davin tapped the board. "The page is fuller too."

When two children from the path paused to look up, Paloma felt a new test. There was only one blanket. She scooted and patted the corner. "The sky is not a seat you can use up," she said. "Come share the view." They stayed for one cloud, named it a hat, and wandered on, content. Paloma's blanket was a bit more wrinkled and a bit more friendly. She did not feel robbed. She felt like a host of the air. Davin bumped her shoulder. "That was the same sharing, just with more shoes nearby."

They copied the best shared shapes onto the path in small chalk marks, each picture signed with two tiny initials. Paloma wrote P and D under the bridge. Davin wrote D and P under the loaf. The order changed, the credit did not. Paloma stood and looked from path to sky. The wide cloud was thinning into light. She let it go without clutching. "Thank you for not racing me," she told Davin. "Thank you for making room," he said. The breeze was respectful, just enough to move hair and grass, not enough to smudge their chalk before they had seen it.

On the walk home they retold both pictures, bridge and loaf, mitten and whale, taking turns by trees along the lane. Paloma kept the and in the middle of every telling. The day felt generous. A sky, she decided, was the easiest treasure in the world to share, because looking did not wear it out. It only woke it up.

They rested on the blanket and practiced sharing without any cloud at all, just a single blade of grass held up to the light. Paloma described one edge. Davin described the other. The blade was small, and still there was enough to divide kindly. Paloma laughed at how easy it was once she had started. "If I can share a blade, I can share a sky," she said. Davin tucked the grass into the notebook as a bookmark for their cloud page. The meadow hummed with ordinary insects. Nothing demanded a winner. The wide blue above them kept offering new shapes to anyone willing to look together.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the board of shared clouds. Paloma traced the little word and with her finger. Outside, the evening sky held one last pale puff and then a clear, calm blue. She felt light and glad, sure that sharing had made the meadow bigger than any single name for a cloud.
