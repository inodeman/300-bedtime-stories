Wheels made of empty thread spools clinked softly in Helena's carton. The afternoon smelled like warm paper and the lemon soap on the porch rail. Ivo sat cross-legged with a stack of picture books, their covers smooth under his palms. One showed a red boat. One showed a moon. One showed a cat asleep in a basket. "Jamila has been home with a quiet sniffle and a pile of blankets," Helena said. "We could bring her stories. My arms are not long enough for all of them, and I do not want the corners to bend."

Ivo patted the shallow wooden crate. "A book cart," he said. "Cardboard sides, spool wheels, and a pull string. If a wheel could pinch, we ask Uncle to set the pencils that hold them." Helena nodded hard. Sharing felt bigger when the books could travel like passengers instead of tumbling in a hug. She sniffed a page. It smelled faintly of crayons. A ladybug landed on the moon cover, thought about it, and flew off toward the roses.

Uncle came when they called, wiping his hands on a cloth. "Show me the plan," he said. They did. He slid smooth pencils through the spool holes and fixed them so the wheels could turn without catching fingers. "You two tape the sides. I will watch the rolling parts," he said. The tape ripped with a cheerful sound. Helena pressed a strip until it lost its shine. Ivo drew a little book on the front of the crate and wrote, "Stories to share," in careful letters. The crate smelled like fresh wood and glue.

They loaded three books and one folded blanket for a porch reading. "Ready," Ivo said, and he pulled the string. The cart rolled. It also wobbled. At the first sidewalk crack the moon book slid, flopped open, and landed with a papery sigh. A page corner creased. Helena scooped it up at once and smoothed it with a gentle palm. "No more riding without walls," she said. Her voice was kind, not cross. The cart sat still, as if it felt sorry. A warm gust turned another page by itself.

They sat on the step to think. The sun made a bright stripe on Ivo's knee. "The sides are too short," he said. "The books can see over them." Helena laughed. "Books do not have eyes, but they do have slippery covers." They cut nothing themselves. Uncle trimmed two taller cardboard panels, and the children taped them on like a snug fence. Helena added a soft cloth strap across the top, the way a seat belt would hug a friend. She tested it with her hand. The books stayed even when she gave the crate a small shake.

They tried the sidewalk again. The spool wheels hummed, a tiny rolling song. Over the crack, the books bumped and settled, still inside. Ivo walked slowly so the cart would not rush. Helena walked beside it with one hand ready, not grabbing, just near. Porch chimes tingled. Someone's sprinkler whispered two yards away, and the air tasted cool where the mist drifted. At Jamila's steps they stopped. The cart had done its job. Their arms were free to knock, softly, in case the house was resting.

Jamila opened the door in fuzzy socks, her nose a little pink, her smile wide. "Books," she breathed, as if the word smelled sweet. They spread the blanket on her porch, not inside, so the fresh air could help. Helena offered the moon book first because of the creased corner. "This one had a small adventure," she said. "We can read it gently." They took turns. Ivo did the boat voice, low and bobbing. Helena did the cat voice, almost a purr. Jamila held the cat book against her cheek and then passed it back. "Your turn again," she said. Sharing went around like the wheels.

When the last page closed, they left the cat book for Jamila to keep until tomorrow and rolled the others home. The crate seemed lighter, even though two books were still inside. The sidewalk gave a gentle tick under the spool wheels. Helena touched the letters on the front. "Stories to share," she read. Ivo added, "And a cart that knows how to hold them." Uncle waved from his window, the curtain lifting in a small puff of air. The sidewalk gave back a little warmth through their shoes, and the lemon soap smell met them at their own steps, bright as the moon on the book.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as Helena parked the book cart in the hall. The spool wheels made one last quiet click against the floor. Ivo slid the moon book onto a shelf and smoothed the creased corner one more time, patient as a slow page turn. Helena set a spare label beside the crate that read, "Back to Jamila tomorrow," so the sharing would remember itself in the morning. Outside, Jamila's porch light answered their lantern, and the evening felt full of stories that had found another pair of hands.
