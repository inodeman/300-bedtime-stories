Parvati sat on the rug with a blue ribbon across her knees. The ribbon was her pointer, long enough to aim at the window without poking the glass. Family night-looking was about to begin. Each person would say one true, gentle thing about the sky. Parvati had practiced a sentence about the North Star, the steady one that stays put while other stars seem to travel. Her sentence was ready. Her voice felt like it had gone to hide in her slippers. Crickets sang outside as if they had no trouble being heard. Hot milk steamed in cups. The window lamp made everything look brave already, which helped a little.

Mama squeezed her hand. "You can speak in a small voice. Small is still a voice." Parvati nodded. Grandpa would go first. He pointed the cardboard telescope at the moon and said, "I see a bright round friend." Auntie said, "I see paper stars keeping our window busy." Then it was Parvati's turn. She stood up. The quilt slid off her knees and pooled like a soft cloud. She lifted the blue ribbon. Her tummy fluttered the way a curtain flutters near an open door, not a storm, just movement.

"The North Star is the one that stays," she said. The words came out clear, if not loud. She aimed the ribbon toward a bright point Mama had shown her earlier, above the balcony rail. "It helps people know where they are facing. It does not run away." She lowered the ribbon and found that her hands had remembered what to do. Mama's eyes were shiny with pride. Grandpa whispered, "Well pointed." The room did not laugh in a sharp way. It made a warm murmur that felt like a quilt for her ears.

Parvati sat down and let out a long breath she had not noticed she was holding. Courage, she realized, had been the size of one sentence. The sky had not required a speech. Her family passed the telescope. When it reached her, she looked and did not narrate the whole night. She only said, "I see it again." That second time was easier. The flutter had become a smile. She tied the blue ribbon in a loose bow on the tube so the pointer and the telescope could be a team.

They drank the hot milk. Parvati wrapped her palms around the cup until they were steady and warm. Mama explained, gently, that the North Star sits almost still in the north because of how our world turns, and that the other stars look like they wheel around it. "You do not need the big science tonight," Mama said. "You already shared the heart of it. It stays, so we can find our way." Parvati liked having the heart of an idea. She stuck a glow-in-the-dark sticker at the end of the ribbon so her pointer would have a tiny star of its own.

Before bed she asked to say the sentence one more time from the balcony, where the crickets could hear. Mama came too. The boards were cool under slippers. Parvati held the rail with one hand and the ribbon with the other. She spoke to the garden and the moon, not shouting, only offering. "You stay, and I can say so." The mint leaves nodded in a small breeze. She felt taller in her courage, though her feet had not left the floor. Coming back inside, she hung the ribbon where she would see it in the morning and remember the sound of her own clear words.

Grandpa gave her the soft corner of the quilt. "Speakers need a cozy landing," he said. Parvati laughed and tucked her toes under. She watched the paper stars turn. She did not wish her voice had been louder. She wished only to keep it honest and kind, and she had. The cardboard tube rested with its new blue bow. The cups were nearly empty. Night settled around the house like a listener who had heard enough and was pleased.

Parvati whispered the sentence into her pillow once, not as a drill, but as a keepsake. The North Star stayed where it should. She stayed with her family. Both of those truths felt good to hold at the same time. She closed her eyes while the crickets finished their evening program.

Parvati asked Mama if she could teach the sentence to her own slippers, which made everyone smile. She stood the slippers on the rug like a tiny audience and pointed the blue ribbon. "The North Star stays," she told them, stronger this time. The practice was playful, and it helped her body remember that speaking could be safe and even fun. Grandpa asked for an encore for the paper stars. Parvati gave it, ribbon steady, voice warm. Then she drank the last of her hot milk and pressed a second glow sticker beside the first, so the ribbon would have a pair of small lights. Courage, she saw, could be repeated without wearing out. Each saying made the next one feel like coming home.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the blue ribbon and its little sticker star. Parvati's sentence still seemed to hang in the room, light as paper and strong as a rail. She slept with courage folded neatly beside her, the way slippers wait, ready for the next time she chose to speak.
