Leif's glass jar knocked softly against his leg as he climbed down to the rocks. He had washed it until it sang when he tapped it, a high clean note. Grandpa walked behind him with a slow step and a brimmed hat. The tide was out, and the pools lay in the stone like spilled sky. "Just looking," Grandpa said, the way he always did, a reminder and a smile at once. Leif nodded. In his pocket the jar's lid clicked against a button. Looking was the plan. A small secret hope came along too, the hope of a passenger.

He found the crab in the second pool, no bigger than a walnut, gray-green with careful eyes on little stalks. It held its claws close, not as a warning, only in the tidy way crabs hold themselves when the world is large. Leif's breath snagged. "Hello, small neighbor," he whispered. The water was warm at the top and cooler when he dipped a finger at the edge, away from the crab. A starfish the color of cream sat farther off, utterly relaxed. Gulls drifted above, quiet for once, as if the pools had asked for a library voice. Leif unscrewed the lid. The glass mouth looked exactly crab-sized.

He pictured the jar on his bedroom sill, the crab waving, a private tide just for him. He even lowered the jar and let a little water lip inside. Sand swirled. The crab side-stepped, alert and not interested in being a story on a sill. Leif's hand hovered. Grandpa did not snatch the jar. He crouched and spoke to the pool as much as to Leif. "That crab knows which rock is a door and which shadow is a nap. A jar knows how to be clear. Those are different talents." Leif felt the hope in his pocket tug once, then loosen its fingers.

"What if I only keep it for today?" he asked. The question was honest, and it still felt a little wrong in his mouth, like a sweet that was mostly salt. Grandpa pointed at the weed waving over the crab's back. "Today the pool feeds it. Today the tide will refresh it. Today has a job here." Leif set the jar on a dry rock. He lay on his stomach, chin on his wrists, and watched instead. The crab cleaned a bit of shell with busy hands. It found a speck Leif could not even name and ate with perfect manners. It was already having a full day. It did not need Leif to improve it.

A gull landed, saw the humans being calm, and lost interest in any chance of scraps. It preened and left. Leif counted the crab's steps. Fourteen to the weed, three back, a pause like a comma. He noticed a tiny fish, hardly a comma itself, flick through a stripe of light and vanish. The pool was a town. Taking the crab would have punched a hole in the town. Leif's chest ached in a soft way, the ache of choosing the bigger kindness. "You can keep your doors," he told the crab. "I will keep the memory."

He poured the small scoop of water back in a slow ribbon so it would not blast the sand into the crab's eyes. Then he filled the jar again only to see the color, held it up to the sky, and poured that back too. The glass stayed beautiful and empty. Empty, he realized, was not a failure. Empty was a promise. Grandpa passed him a handkerchief for his damp wrists. "Some care carries things home," Grandpa said. "Some care leaves them thriving. Both are real. The second one fits this pool." Leif screwed the lid on so sand would not sneak in, a tidy ending.

They walked the long way past the sandcastle beach, where yesterday's towers had softened into friendly mounds. Leif did not hunt for another crab. He let the pools be pools. On the dune he sat and told Grandpa every detail he could still see behind his eyes, the stalk-eyes, the fourteen steps, the cream starfish that never once hurried. The telling made the visit larger, not smaller. If the crab had been sloshing in glass, Leif would have been busy worrying about air and heat. Instead he was full of pictures and the clean smell of the rocks still on his sleeves.

At the cottage tap he rinsed the jar until it was only itself again. He set it upside down to dry, a bell of light. "I might use you for collecting smooth pebbles that are not anybody's house," he told it. That felt fair. Pebbles did not have stalk-eyes. Grandpa hung his hat by the door. They ate apple slices that tasted sharp and sweet. Leif looked out at the shore and felt the crab still out there, small and competent, finishing its tide-pool chores. The feeling was quieter than owning and, he decided, much better company.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the drying jar, putting a round shine inside the glass. Leif touched the cool side once and left it empty on purpose. Out on the rocks the evening water would come in and refresh every pool, including a walnut-sized crab who would never know it had been almost borrowed. Leif knew. He carried that knowledge like a smooth stone he did not need to show anyone. Nature had trusted him with a look. He had given it back, whole, and the giving made the lantern hour feel especially gentle.
