The house was extra quiet because the new baby was napping. Fatima stood in the hallway in socks and felt how the floor could tell on noisy heels. Oscar held a stack of small washed cloths that smelled like clean cotton and a drop of lavender. From the bedroom came a tiny sigh, then silence, the kind that feels important. Mama's whisper reached them from the doorway. "If you want to help, help softly. No surprises near the crib. You may build something useful in the hall."

Fatima nodded so hard her hair brushed her cheek, then she made the nod smaller. "A nap basket," she whispered. "Cloths for spills and chins, on a low cart, so you do not have to hunt for them with a baby on your shoulder." Oscar liked useful. He also liked the baby, who had a warm head and a serious mouth and did not yet care about carts. They looked at a shallow box with four little wheels that had once moved groceries. One wheel gave a tiny squeak when Oscar turned it. The squeak sounded enormous in the quiet hall.

"That wheel is telling secrets," Fatima said. "Babies do not want secrets squeaked at them." They did not take the cart apart. Papa came on quiet feet and showed them how to wrap a strip of soft cloth around the squeaky wheel's edge, tied where fingers would not be caught. "I will knot it," he said. "You two fold." The cloth made the wheel look dressed for a gentle job. Oscar rolled the cart a short way. It whispered instead of squeaking. The whisper was almost a hush. Fatima put her ear close and smiled.

They folded the cloths into squares, corner to corner, palms smoothing each one. The stack grew like a small white hill. Oscar set them in the box. Fatima added a tiny bottle of plain water that Mama had already closed tight, and a paper fan for warm cheeks, the fan made yesterday from a folded page that had no sharp edges. Then they made a sign. Oscar drew feet in socks. Fatima wrote, "Soft steps, please. Baby is resting." The letters were big and calm. They taped the sign to the wall at their own eye level, with Papa pressing the tape so the paper would not flutter and tick.

The cart's first trip down the hall went well until it passed the bedroom door and the dressed wheel met a bump in the rug. A cloth slid. Fatima caught it before it could sigh onto the floor. They paused. Inside, the baby made a small bird noise and settled. Oscar's eyes were wide and careful. "The hill of cloths is too tall," he whispered. They made two shorter stacks and laid a ribbon across them, loose, not tight, so nothing would tumble. Papa checked the ribbon knot. The cart rolled again. The rug bump was still there, but the cargo stayed put. Patience was in the second try.

Mama stepped out, saw the sign, and touched Fatima's shoulder in thanks. "Would you like to help with a slow mobile?" she asked. "I will hang it on the wall beside the crib, not above the baby, and not where little hands can pull it later. You may choose the shapes." They chose paper birds, light as crackers, colored with the yellow of morning and the blue of a quiet cup. Mama threaded them while the children held the ends flat on the table. When she hung the mobile, the birds turned once in the room's small breeze and then moved like a lullaby you see instead of hear. The baby slept on. A fist the size of a walnut rested on the blanket.

Fatima and Oscar parked the cart where Mama's hand could find it without a search. They practiced walking past the sign in a new way, heel then toe, arms quiet. Oscar felt a wiggle in his chest, the wish to cheer, and he let it out later in the kitchen, a silent fist raised, a grin against his sleeve. Fatima poured water for them both, the jug close to the cup so it would not splash. The house still smelled like lavender and warm milk. Helping, they were learning, could be almost invisible and still be real.

They sat on the back step for a minute of ordinary noise, birds and a far wagon, so their bodies could stretch. Then they slipped inside again, because the sign was also for them. The cart waited, neat and hushed. The paper birds held their slow dance. Fatima looked at Oscar. Neither needed to speak. The baby's nap kept going, a small peaceful engine, and the hallway kept the secret of how to be quiet on purpose.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when evening came and the baby woke into a calm room, not a startled one. Fatima rolled the cart one whisper-length closer. Oscar touched the sign and then let his hand fall. The paper birds turned in the low light, and the house felt proud in a gentle way, as if patience itself had learned to walk on sock feet.
