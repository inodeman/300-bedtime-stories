Finn sat at the kitchen table with a blank paper that looked bigger than the town. His cousin Ivan lived far enough away that a visit needed a long ride and a night bag. Finn missed him. The missing felt like an empty chair pulled out and waiting. The chair was real. Ivan had sat there last spring, making silly pancake faces. Now the chair held only a folded napkin. Finn put his hand on the paper and did not know which word should go first.

Grandma Noelle set a yellow cup of warm milk near his elbow, not too close to the paper. “A letter can be a little porch light,” she said. “It tells someone, I see you, even from another town.” Finn nodded. He still felt shy of the blankness. “What if my news is too small?” he asked. “Ivan has buses and a big library near his building. I have a balcony and a spoon.” Grandma Noelle smiled. “Small news is a kindness when it is true. It gives him a picture he can hold.”

Finn drew a square that was the table. He put a circle for Ivan’s chair and wrote, This chair still knows you. Then he stopped, because he missed Ivan so much his eyes felt hot. The hot feeling was not a storm. It was love with nowhere to walk. He breathed. He told Grandma Noelle, “I feel sad-missing, and also full of things I want him to know.” She said, “Both can ride in the same envelope. Start with one true picture.”

So Finn wrote about Sunday. He wrote that the pancake he flipped looked like a cloud with a bite taken by nobody yet. He wrote that mint on the balcony smelled like cold green rain. He wrote that a wooden spoon drummed a soft hello on a pot while soup warmed. He did not try to sound fancy. He tried to sound like himself. The words came easier after the first three lines, like feet finding a path. He even wrote, I saved you the story of the cloud pancake so you can hear it in my voice later.

Kindness asked for one more thing. Finn thought about what Ivan might miss. Ivan liked the blue bowl. Ivan liked the way the family hummed while they washed spoons. Finn added, Your humming spot is still by the sink. If you hum there in your kitchen, we can hum here on Sunday and it will match, almost. He drew the sink, the bowl, and two little music marks that were not perfect and did not need to be. He felt the missing change shape. It was still there, but it had a job.

Grandma Noelle brought an envelope and a stamp with a leaf on it. Finn wrote Ivan’s name slowly. He checked the town name twice, the way Grandma Noelle had taught him, so the letter would not get lost and lonely. He tucked the page in and sealed the flap with careful pressure. His hands felt steady. “This is me reaching,” he said. Grandma Noelle answered, “And it is you making a seat for him in his own day.” Finn liked that. A letter could be a chair that folded flat and traveled.

They walked down to the corner box together. Finn held the envelope against his sweater so the wind would not snatch it. At the box he paused. “Will he know I am not mad that he lives far?” he asked. “He will know you thought of him on purpose,” Grandma Noelle said. Finn slid the letter in. The box made a soft metal hush. Finn stood a moment with his palm on the cool side. The empty-chair feeling was still real, and now it sat beside a sent-kindness feeling, which was warmer. On the way home Finn picked up a fallen leaf from the sidewalk, not from a neighbor’s pot, and held it like a souvenir of the walk. “Ivan would like this shape,” he said. “I can draw it next time if I forget to tuck one in.” He practiced the next letter in the air with his finger, so the kindness would not be only once. Grandma Noelle squeezed his hand. “Missing someone can be a soft path,” she said. “You just walked it.” Finn swung their hands a little. The town felt closer to Ivan’s town, even though the streets had not moved.

Back in the kitchen he set a second cup on the table, not because Ivan could drink it, but because the place looked friendly. He would tell Ivan about the second cup in the next letter. Missing someone, he decided, could include making something kind for them to find. He drank his milk and listened to the quiet, which no longer felt so wide.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the empty cup and the place where the paper had been. Finn watched the glow and imagined Ivan opening the cloud pancake, the mint, and the humming sink. The little porch light was on its way, and the kitchen felt kindly connected to another town.
