Naomi stood in her soft gray slippers while the pot of hot milk breathed a sweet smell into the kitchen. The window lamp laid a round pool of gold across the table, where two blue cups waited side by side. Crickets kept a tiny ticking song in the garden. Above the balcony the moon hung like a calm white button. Papa was still outside, folding paper stars for the window string, and his shoulders looked busy and a little tired from a long, gentle day.

Naomi took one small sip from her own cup. The milk was warm and just right, with a faint taste of honey. Then she touched the second cup and found it only lukewarm. Papa had poured it and forgotten it while he worked. A paper star had slipped off the table too, and it lay under a chair with one point bent like a sleepy ear. Naomi looked from the cup to the star and did not call out yet. She wanted her help to be ready before she asked him to come inside.

She poured the cool milk back into the pot so it could grow warm again. She rinsed the blue cup and set a folded cloth beside it for Papa's hands. Then she reached under the chair, picked up the fallen star, and smoothed the bent point with careful fingers. "I can help," she told the quiet room. On the balcony the air was cool and friendly. Naomi stayed near the open door, where the lamp light still reached her slippers. "Papa, your cup is getting ready again," she said. "And a star tried to hide under the chair."

Papa looked up and smiled. "I wondered where that little one went. Thank you for looking." He showed her the row of paper stars clipped with wooden pins. Each star had a tiny hole and a loop of string. "Would you like to press this last fold?" Naomi pressed the crease flat. The paper made a soft crisp sound and held its shape. "The moon looks as if it is watching us work," she said. Papa nodded. "That bright group beside it is called the Big Dipper. People say it looks like a big spoon that could hold a sip of milk. We can find it when the cup is ready."

Inside, Naomi poured the hot milk slowly and watched the white line rise until it nearly touched the rim. She carried the cup with two hands and watched the surface so it would not spill. Papa sat in the chair by the window and drew the quilt over his knees. He tasted the milk and let his eyes rest for a moment. "This is just right," he said. "You noticed before I did." Naomi felt glad in a way that was quieter than a cheer. She taped the smoothed paper star back onto the string. The yellow points rested against the glass in a complete row, and the crickets went on with their song.

"Can we look with the tube?" Naomi asked. Their telescope was a cardboard tube from a roll of paper, with a paper ring taped at one end so the mouth stayed round. Papa held it steady. Naomi looked through, and the tube made a small frame around the moon, like a picture of a pearl. Then Papa tipped it toward seven stars. "Bowl and handle," he said. "The spoon in the sky." Naomi whispered the shape so she would remember it. She did not rush. The night was already doing its gentle work, and the balcony rail felt cool under her free hand.

When Papa's shoulders dropped into an easier rest, Naomi tucked the quilt corner closer to his wrist. She set his slippers beside his feet so he would not have to search for them later. None of the jobs was loud or large. Together they made the room feel looked after. She stuck one spare glow-in-the-dark sticker inside the cardboard tube, a tiny green dot near the rim. "So the next person who looks will find a helper star," she said. Papa nodded. "That is a kind thing to leave behind." Naomi liked the idea of a surprise that would wait kindly for someone else.

Naomi rinsed both cups and turned the pot off. She listened once more to the crickets and to the small creak of the balcony chair. The garden was safe and awake in its own soft way. She was glad she had watched for the cold cup and the fallen star instead of thinking only of her own sip. A little care, given before anyone asked, had made the whole room feel kinder. She shuffled toward her bed with the moon still bright beyond the glass, and the paper stars kept their neat places on the string.

The lantern by the window glowed softly, painting a quiet gold on the quilt and the empty blue cups. The Big Dipper waited outside like a spoon at rest. Inside the cardboard tube, the little green sticker was already drinking the lamplight, ready to shine when the room grew dim. Naomi's help stayed in the evening the way warmth stays in a cup, long after the milk is gone.
