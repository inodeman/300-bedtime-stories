Penelope's pocket felt friendlier than it had that morning. The light picnic was over. Moss had carried the basket home without strain, the cloth was shaken, and the cups were rinsed. On the porch Penelope reached into her pocket for a smooth stone she liked and found, instead, a second apple. It was small, red, and cool. She had packed one apple for the tin. This one must have rolled in beside it when she stood at the bowl, and then it had traveled all the way to the oak and back without being seen.

Her first thought was a happy private one. A secret apple is a little feast. She could eat it by the pump and tell no one, and the picnic would still have been fair because nobody had missed what nobody knew about. The thought was tidy. It was also not quite true. The apple had come from the shared bowl. The picnic list said the fruit was for the group. A thing can be in your pocket and still belong to the plan.

Penelope turned the apple in her hands. Jasper had talked about spilled oats and the relief of a true sentence. She borrowed some of that courage, even though no one was waiting with a dustpan. She walked into the kitchen, where Corin was pinning the list back up and Lorca was retelling the rain. "I found an extra apple," Penelope said. "It hid in my pocket. I almost ate it alone. I think it should come back to the picnic people."

Corin looked surprised, then pleased. Lorca laughed in a kind way, not a poking way. "A pocket stowaway," Lorca said. "Thank you for introducing us." The grown-up washed the apple and set it on the board. "Hidden food cannot be part of a share until someone is honest about it," the grown-up said. "Now it can." The knife made neat slices, thin enough for everyone who had walked, including the grown-up and Penelope, and one slice set on a plate for Rafi, who had gone to wash Moss's water bucket and would return.

Penelope took her slice and tasted it. It was the same apple it would have been by the pump, and it was also better, because the taste did not have a secret sitting beside it. She told them about the moment the private feast had seemed easy. Corin nodded. "I almost packed a heavy book once," Corin said. "Second thoughts made the basket kinder. Your second thought made the sharing kinder." Penelope felt the hot pinch in her cheeks cool into ordinary skin.

They did not tease her pocket as if it were a rascal who needed scolding. They checked the other pockets together, as a game of making sure the list and the reality matched. Lorca found a bottle cap that belonged in the recycling tin. Corin found nothing but lint and a laugh. Penelope found the smooth stone at last, which had been under the apple all along. No one found another piece of the picnic trying to stay hidden. The day snapped back into clear lines.

Rafi returned, accepted the saved slice, and said, "A true apple is my favorite kind." Moss could be seen from the window, resting, uninvolved with fruit. Apples today were for people. That had been the rule, and saying it again kept kind habits from blurring.

Penelope set her smooth stone on the sill next to Jasper's memory of the step. Different lessons, same bright center. She wrote on the picnic list, in small letters, check pockets before you say the sharing is done.

Penelope washed the board and set the smooth stone back in her pocket, where it belonged more honestly than fruit. She told Jasper the apple story because he had told the oat story, and truths like to travel in pairs. Jasper said a pocket can be a surprise drawer, so a check is part of tidying, not part of scolding. They looked out at Moss, who had carried only what was listed, and they felt the day line up. Hidden things, once spoken, became ordinary slices and ordinary smiles.

She added a tiny apple drawing next to the words on the list. If another fruit tried to travel unseen, the drawing would remind her before the oak. Honesty did not make her less fun. It made the fun shareable. When Rafi finished his slice he tapped the list and said the stowaway had excellent timing, arriving in time to be fair. Penelope laughed, light as the ribbon on an empty basket, and let the laugh be the last word on the matter. She checked the bowl on the counter and counted the fruit that remained, so the next picnic would start with a number and not a surprise. The number was small and honest, which suited her just fine.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the red skin of the last saved slice, waiting only a minute more before it was eaten too. Penelope watched the light and felt light herself. Honesty had taken one apple out of hiding and given it back to the friends who had walked the same lane, which was exactly where it had belonged from the start.
