Fatima tapped the side of a clear jar and listened to the soft tick of rain on the porch roof. The morning smelled like wet soil and mint. Gus stood beside her in socks, because his boots were still dripping by the door. Out in the garden, the leaves shone. A puddle by the path held a piece of sky. "How much rain is that?" Gus asked. "A little? A lot? Enough to skip watering the beans?" Fatima's eyes were bright. She liked questions that had answers hiding in plain sight.

"We can catch some and see," she said. She peeled a strip of tape and stuck it up the outside of the jar. With a pencil she made small lines, as even as she could, using her finger widths as a guide. "Each mark is a drink for the jar," she said. Gus laughed. "Jars do not drink." "This one will," Fatima said. "It will drink the sky, but only a sip, so we can measure." They set the jar on the open porch step, away from the roof drip, where rain could fall straight in. The first drops tapped the glass like tiny fingers.

Grandpa looked out, a towel over his shoulder. "Good thinking to keep it off the rail where it might fall," he said. "If you want, I can help you carry a second jar. Glass is slippery when the rain is on it." They nodded. Grandpa's hands were steady. He placed another jar under the big leafy bush, where the children wanted to compare. "Same marks?" Gus asked. Fatima made them match, tongue peeking at the corner of her mouth. The pencil smelled like school, warm and woody. Rain freckled their sleeves while they worked, cool and quick.

They went inside for toast. Butter melted. The kitchen window fogged at the edges. Fatima kept peeking. "It is filling," she said. Gus drew a picture of a jar with a little ladder of lines. He labeled the bottom "socks dry" and the top "boots very glad," which made Grandpa chuckle. They were not in a hurry. Curiosity, Fatima said, was a slow friend. You had to let it finish its sentence. The rain softened from a patter to a whisper and then to almost nothing. The clouds looked thinner, like worn blankets.

When the drops stopped, they put on boots. The garden path was dark and squashy underfoot. Fatima crouched by the open jar. Water sat at the third mark, clear as a window. A leaf speck floated, and she lifted it out with a twig so it would not confuse them. "Three sips of sky," she announced. Gus hurried to the bush jar and then stopped. "This one is only at one mark," he said, puzzled. "Did the rain forget the bush?" The bush leaves were shiny, and the soil beneath them was damp, but the jar had less.

Fatima tipped her head, thinking. She held her palm over the bush jar, then over the open one. "The leaves are umbrellas," she said slowly. "The rain landed on them and slid away. Our open jar told the truth about the sky. The bush jar told the truth about the bush." Gus's puzzled face turned into a grin. "So both are right, but they are right about different places." He touched a wet leaf and sniffed his fingers. They smelled green and peppery. A worm gleamed in the soil and slipped back down, busy and unbothered. They let it be.

They carried the jars to the porch with Grandpa walking beside the slippery one. Fatima wrote on a card, "Open garden, three marks. Under bush, one mark." Gus added, "Do not water the beans today. The sky already did." They propped the card by the herb pots. Mint trembled when a last drip fell from the roof. Fatima listened to it and smiled. The question that had started as a puzzle now sat quietly on a card, answered by two jars and a pair of careful eyes.

They poured nothing on the beans. Gus looked at the bean row, lifted the jar, and then remembered the card. He laughed and set it down. "The beans are already drinking," he said. They saved a cup of the clear water to look at in the light, where it held a bright wobble of window, then poured it on the mint, which did not mind a little extra. The tape marks stayed, ready for the next gray morning. Fatima washed her pencil smudges off her thumb. The air smelled cleaner than before, as if the rain had rinsed the day and left the mint wide awake.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while the clouds drifted apart. Fatima and Gus sat on the rug and looked at their card. The third mark and the first mark were only pencil lines, but they felt like a small discovery you could hold. Grandpa set two mugs nearby, warm and sweet, with steam that smelled like honey. Outside, the open jar waited empty and shining, curious as the children, ready to count the next rain. A drip from the roof tapped once, like a tiny yes.
