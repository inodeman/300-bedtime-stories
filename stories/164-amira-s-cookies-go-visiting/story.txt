Amira carried a square tin against her sweater so the date cookies would not slide. The tin was still warm at the corners. Inside, the cookies lay in paper cups, each one round and brown, smelling of orange peel and cinnamon. The small town evening festival had a long table under the elm trees, and the rule this year was simple: bring something to share, take something you like, and leave plenty for the people still walking in from the far lanes. Amira had helped stir the dough. She knew there were twenty cookies, because she had counted the cups twice.

Papa walked beside her with a jug of cold milk wrapped in a towel. "The table may already look full," he said. "Full can still make room." Amira nodded. She liked the weight of the tin. She also liked the cookies so much that a small wish tugged at her: maybe she could keep four at home, the prettiest ones. She looked at that wish and did not scold it. She just carried it along with the tin and decided to see the table first. The path was scattered with dry elm leaves that whispered instead of cracking loudly. A guitar somewhere played a walking tempo, no faster than their feet.

The table was a patchwork of cloths. There were cucumber spears, cheese cut into moons, rice balls wrapped in seaweed, and a bowl of grapes that shone. A card in front of each dish said what it was, in clear letters. Amira's cookies did not have a card yet. She set the tin near the middle, where hands from both ends could reach, and Papa wrote "date cookies with orange" on a card. He did not write Amira's name in large letters. "The food is the guest," he said. "We are the ones who open the door." Amira lifted the lid. The cinnamon smell stood up and greeted the grapes.

Mrs. Okonkwo, who watched the table, smiled. "Those look like they took patience. Would you like to offer them, or shall I?" Amira surprised herself by saying, "I can offer." She stood by the tin with a small stack of napkins. The first person to come was Mr. Ellis, who used a cane and liked to look before he chose. "What is the round one?" he asked. "A date cookie," Amira said. "Soft, not crumbly. There is orange in it." She waited while he decided. He took one. "I will save my second wish in case someone behind me has not seen them," he said. Amira felt the sentence land like a good example.

More people came, unhurried. A musician with the guitar took a cookie and left a tune in trade, six notes that sounded like thank you. Amira counted without announcing the numbers. When ten cookies were gone, she saw the far end of the table still empty of sweet things. She moved half of the remaining cookies onto a small plate and walked them down, slowly, so she would not bump the grapes. "Dates and orange," she told the people there, who had been talking quietly over cups of tea. One woman pressed a hand to her chest, pleased. "We thought the sweets had finished their walk at the other end. Thank you for bringing them visiting."

Amira kept two cookies in the tin, not four. One would be for Papa. One would be for her. The wish for extra prettiness had grown smaller beside the woman's smile. She ate hers on the bench under the elm, and it tasted deeper than the ones she had sampled at home, maybe because the air was cool and the guitar was kind. The date was rich. The orange was bright. Papa clinked his cup of milk against her smaller cup. "You counted," he said. "I saw you counting with your eyes." "I wanted the end of the table to have a turn," Amira said. "Turns taste good."

Amira walked back to her first place and found Mr. Ellis still nearby, talking with Papa about the elm shade. "Did the far end get a visit?" Mr. Ellis asked. "Yes," Amira said. "They had tea and no sweets until the plate arrived." She felt the warmth of the tin against her sweater again, lighter now. A breeze moved the elm leaves in a soft hush, like many small hands clapping very far away. Papa poured a little more milk into her cup. It was cold and sweet and made a quiet click against the tin when she set it down. She listened to the guitar choose a slower walk, as if the song had eaten a cookie too.

They stayed to hear one more song. The guitar player sat so the sound went outward, not into anyone's ear too strongly. Lanterns in the branches gave the leaves gold edges. Amira closed the tin and felt how light it was. Light did not feel like loss. It felt like a job finished. Mrs. Okonkwo brought back the empty plate, clean and a little warm from washing. "Every cup found a hand," she said. "That is the festival working." Amira tucked the plate into Papa's basket and brushed a crumb from her sleeve. The crumb smelled like cinnamon even then.

Before they left, Amira helped Mrs. Okonkwo match lids to tins. Some lids were snug. Some needed a gentle turn. Amira read the cards aloud in a small voice so the words stayed with the right foods. "Grapes." "Rice balls." "Date cookies with orange," she said, even though her tin was nearly empty. Saying it felt like leaving a path for next time. Mrs. Okonkwo gave her a damp cloth for a sticky spot, and the cloth smelled like lemon soap. Amira wiped until the wood showed again. "A clean place is also a share," Mrs. Okonkwo said. "It welcomes the next basket.

On the way home she hummed the six-note thank you so she would remember it. Papa hummed a lower note and did not try to make the song bigger. Their kitchen was dim until he turned the small lamp. Amira set the tin on the counter and washed the cookie smell from her fingers with warm water. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and she looked at the two empty paper cups she had saved by accident, glad that most of the sweetness had gone out walking with other people.
