Tariq held two folded notes in the sunny hallway and could not remember which kindness mailbox was which. One note said, "Your steady hands help the banner stay brave." He had meant it for Ruth. The other said, "Thank you for telling the truth about the button." He had meant it for Omar. In his hurry to help before reading time, he had already slipped both notes through slots. A small honest feeling told him they might be swapped. The hallway smelled like oranges and warm wood. His heart tapped like a shaker that had skipped its rest.

Omar came along with a star pencil from the lost-and-found tray, meaning to return it to the side cup. He opened his mailbox because the lid looked cheerful, and out came the banner note. He read it and blinked. "My hands did not hold the banner," he said, puzzled, not hurt. "This sounds like Ruth's careful holding." Tariq stepped forward before the moment could grow confusing. "I think I mixed the slots. I am glad you are here. May I have the note back so it can find Ruth, and we can look in her box for yours?" His voice stayed kind to himself and to the paper. Friendship could include fixing a mix-up without a fuss. Omar smiled. "Of course. The words can travel to the right pocket."

They opened Ruth's mailbox together. Inside was the button note, a little crooked from the slide. Ruth arrived just then, wiping chalk from her fingers, and saw them with the lid up. Tariq did not hide the paper behind his back. "I swapped your notes," he said. "Omar got the banner thank-you, and you got the button thank-you. We were about to trade them. I am sorry the mix-up made a moment of puzzle." Ruth looked at both notes and then at Tariq's face. "Thank you for saying so while we are all here," she said. "A puzzle with friends is just a puzzle." She handed Omar his true note and took her own.

Omar read his button note and tucked it into his pocket, where it rustled. "This fits," he said. "My ears know it is mine." Ruth read the banner words and pressed the paper flat on the bench so the letters would not stay curled. "This fits too. I can feel the cloth in the sentence." Tariq's shoulders dropped. He had expected the day to feel tangled for longer. It felt smooth already, because nobody had kept a note that was not theirs. He wanted to mark the mailboxes so his next delivery would be easier. "Could we add tiny pictures, if the box owners like it?" he asked. "A banner sketch for Ruth, a button sketch for Omar, so a hurried friend can see with eyes and not only with memory."

Ruth and Omar liked the idea. They did not draw on anyone else's box. They each drew on their own. Ruth's banner was four lines and a smile. Omar's button was a blue circle with four dots. Tariq drew a small shaker on his own box, because friends often thanked him for starting the fair sounds, and a picture would help the next writer. The chalk they used was the fat kind, easy to see, and it dusted their fingers white. They blew the extra dust outside the door. The blow made a tiny cloud that vanished in the sun. "Now the boxes speak before we open them," Omar said. Tariq nodded. "And if I still mix them up, we will trade again. Trading is friendly."

They checked the other mailboxes, not to snoop, but to see if any note had fallen on the floor or stuck halfway. One corner peeked from Ada's slot, and Tariq pushed it gently all the way in so it would not drop. He did not read it. Friendship with the whole hallway included privacy. "In it goes," he said. Ruth wiped a smudge off the bench. Omar returned the star pencil to the lost-and-found side cup, because that was the errand he had started. The cup clicked.

Tariq wrote one new note, this one for both of them, and he did not trust the slots with it yet. He handed it over in person. "Thank you for helping me undo a mix-up. You made the fix feel easy." Ruth and Omar read it together, heads bent, and then they decided it could live on the bench for the afternoon in a little stand, so Tariq would not have to choose a single box. The stand was a folded card. Tariq felt the last tap in his chest settle into a walk. Friends were the ones who helped a good idea arrive, even if hands took a wrong turn first.

He practiced the slots once with blank paper, naming the pictures as he went. "Banner. Button. Shaker." The blanks came back out so they would not pretend to be real notes. The practice helped his eyes and his hands agree. Omar patted the button drawing. Ruth smoothed the banner drawing. They walked toward the reading rug together, notes safe, boxes clear. The orange smell followed them a little way. Tariq saved them each a corner of the rug by patting the fuzz the way friends did. There was room.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the row of kindness mailboxes, lighting the new chalk pictures so banner, button, and shaker were easy to tell apart. Tariq looked back once, smiled at the rightness of it, and sat between Omar and Ruth, ready to hear a story with the people who had helped his thanks land where it belonged.
