Barak set one round lump of clay in the middle of the table, between himself and Celine, and covered it with a damp cloth for a moment so it would stay cool and willing. The lump smelled like rain on a path. Celine's hands hovered. "Two cups," she said. "From one piece. I do not want my cup to steal your clay." Barak nodded. "Then we share before we shape. Sharing at the start is easier than sharing at the end, when the cups already have feelings." They grinned, because cups do not have feelings, and also because the work did feel personal once your thumbs were in it.

They divided the lump with a piece of thread, pulling the thread through in one smooth pull that was only string. Grandpa watched from the sink, washing greens, the water running. The two halves were not equal. Barak's was larger, a hill beside Celine's smaller mound. He saw it at once. The bigger half felt lucky in his hands, cool and heavy. He also saw Celine's eyebrows lift and then try to be polite. "Yours is thin," he said. "Let us put a palmful back." He pinched off a generous piece and set it on her side. Celine added a smaller pinch back to him until both mounds felt like cousins, not like a giant and a pebble. "Now they match enough," she said. "Enough is fair."

They each opened a cup, thumbs in the center, walls rising. Barak's walls went tall and thin and then folded. He laughed once, short, and started the opening again, slower. Celine's cup stayed short and sturdy, with a thick bottom that would forgive a bump. Clay squeaked softly under their fingers. A bowl of water sat between them, and they shared it without splashing, dipping only fingertips. Droplets shone. The tablecloth drank a few dark dots. From the sink came the bright smell of cut greens and the peppery scent of a radish Grandpa had sliced.

Celine reached for the blue paint jar out of habit, then stopped. "There is one blue and one white," she said. "If I take all the blue, your cup will be only white, and white is pretty, but I know you like sky color." Barak had been hoping for blue. He said so, because honesty helped sharing. "I do like sky color. What if the outside of both cups is blue, and the insides are white, and we share the jars turn by turn?" Celine's face opened. "Yes. You first, three strokes, then me." They painted that way, the brush handle getting a little slick, the blue going on in bands. When the blue jar ran low, they switched to a thinner coat instead of one person using the last of it. The last drop became a tiny dot on each rim, like a shared star.

While the cups dried to a firm leather feel, they made a small dish from the scraps they had pinched off, a shared leftover, not a secret extra for one of them. The dish was wobbly and then, with Celine steadying and Barak smoothing, it sat flat. "For beads or for lemon pits," Barak said. "For both," Celine answered. "It lives in the middle of the table, like the lump did." They pressed a thumbprint on the underside of each cup and on the dish, three prints, so the set would show that hands had visited. The prints were ridges and whorls, cool and precise.

Grandpa poured water, only a sip, into each cup when the clay could hold it. Both cups kept their sip. Barak's was taller. Celine's was wider. Neither one was a mistake. They clinked the rims very gently, a soft tock, and laughed when a drop jumped onto the cloth. "The tall one can hold the spoons," Barak said. "The wide one can hold the sugar." Celine shook her head, smiling. "Or we can both drink. Sharing does not end when the making ends." They took turns with one sip of water each, cool and clean, tasting a little like the clay smell still in the air, or maybe that was just their hands near their faces.

They set the cups and the scrap dish on a tray. No one ranked them. Barak said, "I like the thick bottom. It feels trustworthy." Celine said, "I like the tall walls. They look like they are singing a high note." Compliments went outward, not upward into bragging. Grandpa put a radish slice on the dish, pink and white and crunchy, and they ate it in halves. The crunch was loud and funny. Clay dried pale on their knuckles. At the sink they shared the soap, passing it without needing to be asked, bubbles smelling like lemon.

The finished cups waited, blue outside, white inside, thumbprints hidden underneath like quiet signatures. The shared dish sat between them. What had been one lump was now a little family of useful things, divided fairly and painted in turns. Barak and Celine wiped the table until the dark water dots were gone. Their hands smelled like lemon and earth, a good combination, like a garden after washing.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the tray, and the two blue cups held small cups of light in their white insides. Barak and Celine stood close, not crowding, and looked at what sharing had made. Nothing was stolen. Nothing was hoarded. The clay had been enough, because they had decided it would be.
