Wren set the chipped oak-bark cup on the mossy windowsill of her mushroom cottage and turned it in the warm morning light. The chip was small, like a tiny moon nibble at the rim, yet it made the cup lean whenever she poured. A sweet smell of yesterday's berry tea still hung in the little kitchen. Outside, oak leaves clicked softly, and a ribbon of sun rested across the ferns. Wren slid her thumb along the bark and found the rough edge. "You are still a good cup," she said. "We will mend you before our guest arrives."

She gathered what the woods offered for a gentle fix. Soft moss, damp with dew, pressed into a tiny cushion. A thin strip of pale leaf, dried and bendy, could wrap the rim. Wren also found a thread of grass strong enough to tie, but not so sharp that it would scratch. She laid everything on the wooden table beside a paper map of the wood, which she used as a clean mat. The map showed the rope footbridge, the picnic clearing, and the hollow tree library. Wren hummed as she worked, and the mushroom cottage smelled of warm bark and crushed berries.

"First the moss," she whispered. She tucked a small green pinch under the chip so the rim would sit level. Then she wound the leaf strip around and around, the way a fox wraps its tail on a cool morning. The grass thread held the leaf in place. Wren poured a spoon of cool water into the cup and watched. The water stayed inside. It did not dribble down the side. She smiled so widely that her cheeks felt warm. "There," she said. "You are ready for tea."

A soft scratch came at the round door. Wren opened it and found a gentle fox sitting on the moss, ears tipped with sunlight, paws neat and still. The fox had walked the path under the oaks and carried nothing but a single ripe berry in its mouth. It set the berry on the step like a small gift. Wren knelt so they were eye to eye. "I hoped you would visit," she said. "The kettle is singing, and I mended a cup just for sharing."

The fox blinked a slow, friendly blink and followed her inside. Its paws made almost no sound on the floor. Wren poured berry tea into the oak-bark cup and into her own little bowl. Steam rose in curls that caught the light. She set the cup where the fox could reach it without stretching too far. The fox sniffed, then lapped carefully. Tea did not spill. The moss and leaf held the chip steady. "You drink so politely," Wren said, laughing. The fox's tail gave one happy sweep across the rug.

They sat by the open window. Wren broke a small acorn biscuit and offered half. The fox took it with tidy teeth and crunched in a quiet way that made Wren giggle again. Outside, a firefly rested early on a fern, pale in the daylight, as if practicing for dusk. Wren told the fox about the paper map and the hollow tree library where leaves were stacked like books. The fox listened with its chin on its paws. When Wren's voice grew soft, the fox's ears stayed up, which felt like a kind of answer.

After the tea, Wren noticed a smear of moss on the fox's whiskers. She did not laugh in a sharp way. She dipped a leaf in warm water and held it out. The fox leaned in and let her dab the green away. "Thank you for trusting me," Wren said. The fox nudged the mended cup with its nose, gentle as a pat. Wren understood. The cup had needed care, and so had the visit. Kindness was not a loud thing. It was warm tea, a steady rim, and a soft cloth for a messy whisker.

Before the fox left, Wren tucked an extra biscuit into a broad leaf and set it by the path. "For your walk home," she said. The fox touched its nose to her hand, then trotted toward the oaks, tail bright in the sun. Wren washed the cup and set it back on the sill, the leaf wrap smooth and green. She traced the paper map with one finger and felt glad that her cottage sat on a path where friends could find her. The ferns moved in a small breeze that smelled like rain far away, though the sky stayed blue.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as the afternoon leaned toward warm dusk. Wren lit it with a twig from the stove, and the light made a honey circle on the oak-bark cup. She could see the tiny mend, neat as a stitch, and she felt proud without needing anyone to clap. Somewhere under the oaks the gentle fox was walking home with a biscuit wrapped in a leaf. Wren rested her chin on her hands and listened to the kettle tick as it cooled. Kindness, she thought, could be as small as a cup that did not spill, and as wide as a door opened for a friend.
