The pine woods held a clean sharp smell, like the inside of a green bottle of air. Kit walked with a small pack that bumped a steady rhythm, and Dad walked a little behind, whistling almost nothing. Jules was already at the bend, tossing a cone from hand to hand and catching it most of the time. "I brought my legs and my curiosity," Jules said. "I think I left my lunch on the kitchen chair. I remembered the chair. I did not remember to pick up what was on it." The cone missed a catch and rolled. Jules bowed to it. Kit felt the weight in the pack: two sandwiches, wrapped in cloth, because Dad liked an extra for a long walk, and Kit had made both that morning with careful mustard.

They reached the flat picnic stone while the sun was high and kind. Needles made a soft floor. A jay scolded someone else far away and then lost interest. Kit opened the pack. The sandwiches smelled like bread and cheese and a little like the apple packed beside them. Jules drank water and tried to look as if a walk could be lunch enough. Kit knew that look. It was politeness covering a growl. "I have two," Kit said, holding up the cloth bundles. "I planned an extra, and you are not an accident. You are the reason an extra makes sense. Share with me." Jules's eyebrows went up. "If I eat it, you will not be hungry later?" "We can split both," Kit said. "Then nobody has the only one, and nobody has none."

They unwrapped on the stone. Crusts were golden. Cheese stuck out in friendly flaps. Kit tore each sandwich into halves, not perfectly, but fairly. A crumb fell into a crack of the stone and became a future ant story, which Kit decided was acceptable. Jules took a half and waited until Kit had a half too, then bit. "Mustard," Jules said, delighted. "You are a person of bold condiments." Kit grinned with a full cheek. They traded halves so each would taste both the cheese side and the tomato side. Juice from the tomato made a spot on the cloth. The spot looked like a small map. Dad sat on a log and ate an apple of his own, close enough to help, far enough to let the sharing be theirs.

After the bread, they still had one apple and a handful of walnuts. Kit poured the walnuts into the lid of the tin and set the lid in the middle. "Common bowl," Kit said. Jules echoed, "Common bowl," as if it were a toast. They ate slowly. The pines made a hush that was not empty, a hush full of needles touching. A shaft of sun warmed Kit's knees. Sharing had turned a forgotten chair into a story they would tell later without any sting. Jules talked about a book with a boat on the cover. Kit talked about the mustard recipe, which was mostly "do not be shy." Their hands met in the lid and did not grab. Fingers learned a little dance of taking turns.

They wrapped the cloth again, crumbs shaken over the needle floor for the ants who had a map. Kit's pack was lighter. Jules's pockets were still empty of lunch, but Jules's middle was not. They walked on to the creek overlook and sat where they could see water moving like spilled light. "If I remember my lunch next time," Jules said, "I will bring the extra, and you can forget on purpose or not." Kit nodded. "Forgetting on purpose is just planning with a funny hat. Bringing extra is the real magic." Dad's whistle returned, a little tune now, pleased. The woods seemed to approve. Even the jay, heard once more, sounded less like a scold and more like a neighbor.

On the way back they found the cone Jules had dropped and set it on a stump where a cone might like to be, visible and not lost. Kit thought about chairs and bundles and common bowls. It was easy to share when you had two. The slightly braver part had been saying so before Jules had to ask. Offering first had kept the day smooth. Jules bumped Kit's shoulder, a thank-you without a speech. Kit bumped back, lighter, so nobody stepped off the path. Their shoes scuffed pine scent up into the air. It smelled like a good ending and also like a good middle.

At the trail post, Jules's parent was waiting with a sheepish smile and a lunch that was no longer needed. Jules held up the cloth Kit had folded into a square. "I already ate a feast of halves," Jules said. "We can have this one tomorrow, and I will not leave it sitting." Everyone laughed. The unused lunch would keep. Nothing was scolded. Kit felt the satisfaction of a pack that had done its job. Dad squeezed Kit's shoulder. "Bold mustard and a kind count," he said. "That is a fine way to walk."

Home was downhill. Kit's legs were pleasantly used. The idea of the common bowl stayed, round as a tin lid, big enough for walnuts and for people. Kit decided to pack two on purpose next time, not only by habit. Purpose made sharing feel like a path you laid, not a leftover you discovered.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when they reached the kitchen and washed the tomato-spot cloth in cool water. Kit set two plates in the drying rack even though supper was later, a joke and a promise. Under the pines, a friend had gone from empty pockets to a full smile because an extra sandwich had been opened and halved. Sharing had made room on the stone, and the woods had felt like a table big enough for everyone who came.
