One mango sat in the blue bowl, gold and fragrant, and two cousins stood over it with the same hungry hope. Lotta had come to visit Finn for the weekend. The mango was the last one, and it was perfectly ripe, which somehow made sharing feel trickier. Finn’s fingers curled, then uncurled. “I feel grabby,” he admitted. “Grabby is not my favorite feeling, but it is here.” Lotta nodded. “I feel the same. If we split it in a hurry, I might watch your piece to see if it is bigger. I do not want a sour afternoon.”

Grandma Elena heard them from the sink and dried her hands. She did not take the mango away. She set out a board, a safe knife for herself, and one wide bowl with two spoons. “Sharing can be a game instead of a race,” she said. “I will cut. You two decide the rules.” Lotta and Finn looked at each other. The grabby feelings did not vanish, but they had to sit down and make room for thinking. The mango smell made thinking delicious and difficult.

Finn spoke first. “Rule one, the pieces go in one bowl. No secret piles.” Lotta added, “Rule two, we take turns choosing the next piece. The other person does not say hurry.” Finn said, “Rule three, if a piece breaks uneven, it still belongs to the bowl, not to an argument.” They both laughed, because argument had almost been invited and they had closed the door. Grandma Elena sliced the mango into sunny cubes and slid them into the bowl. The cubes shone. Lotta placed the two spoons side by side like friends, not like swords.

They sat at the table. Finn went first because Lotta had arrived on the train and he wanted her to see him be a good host. He chose a middle-sized cube, not the biggest, and felt a small victory over grabby. Lotta chose next. She picked a bright corner piece and hummed. The turns made a rhythm. Sometimes they described the piece before eating it. “This one looks like a tiny pillow,” Lotta said. “This one is a gold button,” Finn said. The descriptions made the bowl last longer, and lasting longer felt like more mango, not less. Sharing had stretched the fruit.

Halfway through, Finn saw the biggest cube still sitting there. Grabby stood up again. His spoon drifted. Lotta saw the drift and did not snatch. “You can choose it,” she said. “It is your turn, and I will be glad it is in our bowl.” Finn paused. The offer made the cube feel less like a prize and more like a gift moving in a circle. “Let’s split that one with the spoon edge,” he said. They pressed gently. The cube became two jewels. Each cousin took one. Nobody checked the size twice. The grabby feeling sat back down, embarrassed and softer.

They made a second game when only four cubes remained. Each cousin would give one cube to Grandma Elena and eat one. Grandma Elena acted surprised, though she had sliced the fruit. “I get mango manners and mango,” she said. “Lucky me.” Lotta and Finn clinked spoons, a tiny toast. The bowl emptied. Finn checked his feelings the way he checked his pockets. Grabby was gone. Full and friendly were there instead. Lotta licked a drop from her spoon and sighed. “One bowl was more fun than two piles,” she said. “I got your pillow stories with my fruit.”

They washed the bowl together, bumping elbows on purpose, a cousin joke. Finn put the two spoons in the drawer in the same nest. “Tomorrow if there is only one orange, we already know the rules,” he said. Lotta nodded. “And we can invent new descriptions. An orange piece can be a boat.” The kitchen smelled sweet and soapy. Sharing had not made anyone smaller. It had made the table wider, with room for grabby feelings to be named and then gently shown the door. Lotta asked for a card before the sweetness left their memory. They wrote the three rules in big letters and added rule four, describe a piece before you eat it, because descriptions make a bowl feel longer. Finn drew two spoons holding hands, which was a silly picture and also true. They did not hide the card in a pocket. They taped it inside the cupboard, beside the bowls, where the next hungry afternoon could find it. Lotta said, “If I feel grabby again, I can read this and remember we know how.” Finn said, “And I can remember your gold button piece.” They bumped elbows one more time. The rules were short. The friendliness they made was long. Finn found one more cube of mango that had hidden under the spoon rest, and he did not pocket it. He held it up like a discovered coin. “Shared?” he asked. Lotta nodded. They each took a nibble from opposite corners until the cube was gone, laughing when their bites met in the middle. The last bite tasted like the whole game. Grabby did not even stand up. It stayed in its chair and watched them be fair.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the empty blue bowl. Two spoons gleamed beside the sink, still partners. Lotta and Finn watched the light and felt the sweet leftover of a fruit that had been enough, because they had decided to make it enough together.
