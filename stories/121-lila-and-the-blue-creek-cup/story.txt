Lila walked the stone path beside the creek with her shoes in one hand. The water was clear enough to show pebbles the color of bread crust and pennies. It made a small busy sound, as if many spoons were stirring cups of tea. Willow branches trailed in the shallows and drew slow lines. The air smelled like crushed mint and warm dust. "We will wade only where I can see the bottom," Auntie said. Lila nodded and stepped in. The creek hugged her ankles, cool and polite, then let go.

She had three leaf boats in a tin. Each leaf was broad and still a little damp, and each had a twig mast tied with a grass knot. Lila set the first boat in a quiet pool behind a stone. It turned once, found the light, and drifted. The second followed like a shy friend. Lila smiled so widely her cheeks felt sunny. Then her toes met something smooth under the grass at the edge. She lifted a blue tin cup. White dots marched around the rim. A thread of water ran down her wrist and tickled.

The cup would make a fine harbor. She could fill it and float the last leaf inside, a boat on a little pond of its own. The idea felt bright. She dipped the cup and watched the water climb the dots until they shone. "What did you find?" Auntie asked, kneeling so her shadow cooled the bank. "A cup," Lila said. "It was hiding by the mint." Auntie touched the rim. "Someone painted those dots with care. Cups do not grow beside creeks." Lila looked at her last leaf, still waiting on her knee. She wanted one game. She also saw that the cup was not a creek treasure. It was somebody's.

They walked toward the footbridge, and Lila carried the cup in both hands so it would not swing and bump. The path stones were warm, then cool where the willow shade lay in patches. A dragonfly rested on a reed and rose again like a blue stitch in the air. Near the bridge a small boy sat on a flat rock with a towel over his knees. His grandmother stood beside him, scanning the grass. "I set it down," the boy said, "right where the mint smells loud." Lila's steps grew smaller. The cup seemed to ask to be carried the rest of the way.

She stopped in front of him. The creek clicked behind her. "Is this yours?" she asked. The boy looked up so fast his hair flopped. "My blue cup," he said. "The dots are mine. I rinse smooth stones in it." His grandmother's face softened. "We were going to look one more time before we walked home." Lila set the cup in his hands. It tapped his palm gently. For a breath she still pictured her leaf spinning inside. Then she saw his shoulders settle, easy and glad. "Thank you," he said. "I thought the water had borrowed it for a boat."

Lila sat on the edge of the rock, her feet drying in the sun. "I almost used it for a harbor," she said. "My last leaf wanted a pond of its own." The boy studied the leaf and then the open creek. "Leaves like room," he said. "We can share the slow pool, if you want." He placed the cup on the towel, far from the splash line, and pointed to a pocket of water behind a wide stone. They knelt together. Lila's leaf touched the surface and turned. The boy added a curl of bark. The two little crafts bumped once, nodded, and went on.

They stayed until the apple slices on Auntie's cloth smelled sweeter. The grandmother offered water from a big bottle, cool and clean, not from the creek. Lila drank and tasted a hint of lemon. The boy showed her how to stack flat stones into a low wall so the boats would not rush into the fast ripple. When the wall tipped, they laughed without anyone feeling silly and built it again, slower, pressing each stone until it agreed to stay. A wren sang one bright thread of notes in the willow. Lila's fingers smelled like mint and creek.

Before they left, the boy held out a white pebble with a gray stripe. "For your boat day," he said. Lila put it in her pocket and did not ask for the cup. She watched him loop a blade of grass through the handle so he would feel it in his hand on the walk. On the path she told Auntie, "Next time I will look with my hands and with my thinking." Auntie squeezed her shoulder. "You already did both. You found it, and you walked it back to the right person. That is a whole kindness."

At home Lila washed her feet in a basin that smelled faintly of soap and set the striped pebble on the sill. She told Mama about the leaf boats and the blue cup, and she did not hide the part where she had wanted to keep it for one game. Mama listened while the kettle hummed a low note. The garden outside the window held the last gold of the day. Lila's toes were warm. The problem had been small and real, and it had been mended by a walk, an honest sentence, and an open hand.

On the path home the striped pebble clicked once against a button in Lila's pocket. She stopped at the last bend where the creek widened into a bright sheet. Auntie pointed out a place where the mint grew thickest, a green ruffle along the bank. Lila breathed it in and let the smell mean the cup, the boy, and the boats. A leaf they had not launched turned slowly by itself, in no hurry to be anyone's toy. Lila did not chase it. She liked knowing the water could play without her hands.

The lantern by the window glowed softly, and its light rested on the striped pebble. Lila listened to the house grow quiet. Somewhere the creek still clicked over stones, and somewhere a blue cup with white dots sat safe and dry. She closed her eyes and saw two little boats turning in a slow pool, bumping once like friends, then going on. Even here the day smelled a little like mint. She was glad she had given the cup back.
