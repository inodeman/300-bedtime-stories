The cove held its stones in a long pale ribbon where the tide had given them a polish and then gone out. Hana walked the ribbon with her eyes down and her pocket growing heavier, in the best way. She chose only stones that were already loose, none that anchored a tiny pool. A gray oval. A white one with a freckle. A green one like sea glass that was really stone. They clicked softly when she walked. Auntie Pearl shook a picnic cloth onto the dry sand above the line. "Ten more minutes of choosing," she called, "then we eat cherries." The cherries sat in a jar, red as small lanterns, waiting.

Bram came down the path in city shoes that he had already traded for bare feet at the last rock. His pockets were empty on purpose, he said, because he had not known the cove gave away art supplies. He watched Hana's pocket swing. "You have a whole gallery in there," he said. "I have toes and a good mood." Hana felt the pleased weight of her stones and, beside it, the pinch of a plan that had room for only her hands. She had meant to make a spiral on the flat sand, a surprise for Auntie Pearl. Spirals needed many stones. If she kept them all, Bram would watch. Watching was all right. Joining was better. She stopped clicking her pocket and looked at his hopeful face.

"We can share them," Hana said. "You pick the center stone, since you are the guest. I will pass you half, and we will both build." Bram's smile arrived before his words. "Half of a gallery is still a gallery." Hana poured the stones onto the cloth, not onto the sand, so none would hide. They sorted together. Bram chose a white freckled stone for the center. Hana did not argue, even though she had loved that freckle first. Love of a stone could stretch into love of a pattern. She gave him the green one too. "For your second ring," she said. "It looks like a pea from a sea garden." Bram laughed. "Then your ring can be the gray path around it."

They worked knee to knee. The sand was cool under the dry top layer. Hana placed a stone, then waited so Bram could place his. The spiral grew. A gull paced far off and did not shop among their supplies. When Bram's stone wobbled, Hana showed him how to nestle it with a twist, not a slap. "Like this, so it sits." Their fingers were dusty white at the tips. The spiral began to look like a snail that had studied geometry. Auntie Pearl glanced over and put a hand on her heart in a theatrical way that made them both snort. "Do not finish before I see it," she said. "I am only getting the cups." "We will save the last stone for you," Hana called.

The last stone was the smallest, a chip of cream. Hana held it out on her palm. "You should put it in," Bram said. "You did the finding." Hana shook her head. "You did the center. Auntie does the last. That way the picture has three makers." Auntie Pearl knelt, careful of the rings, and set the chip where the spiral opened, like a door. They all leaned back on their heels. The pattern was better than Hana's solo plan. It had a freckle heart she would not have been brave enough to spend, and a green pea, and a door. Sharing had not thinned the beauty. It had given it more ideas.

They ate cherries on the cloth and kept an eye on the art. Juice stained their thumbs. Bram told a story about a fountain in his square that had no stones you were allowed to touch. "This is better," he said. "Here the rule is be gentle and leave the living pools alone." Hana nodded. "And leave the spiral for the tide if the tide wants it. We do not own the cove. We only borrow the loose ones." She liked hearing herself say it. When a cherry pit was all that remained, they planted nothing. Pits went back in the jar to be discarded properly. The spiral stayed.

Before they left, Hana divided the leftover stones into two small piles. "Take a pocket home," she told Bram. "Not the spiral. The extras. So your city window can have three cove guests." Bram chose three, including one she had hoped to keep, and she let the hope go with a breath that felt clean. He pressed a different favorite into her hand, the green pea stone. "Trade," he said. "So we each carry the other's pick." The stone was warm. Hana slipped it beside her handkerchief. The spiral would meet the next tide and become stones again, which felt honest.

They climbed the path with salt on their ankles and cherry on their smiles. Auntie Pearl carried the empty cloth. Bram's pocket clicked now, a cousin sound to Hana's lighter one. At the top, the cove was a blue bowl holding a pale spiral no bigger than a hand from here, and still it mattered. Hana thought of doors and centers and guests. She had come down to make a picture alone. She went up having made one together.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the seaside cottage when they rinsed their feet in a basin and lined the three kept stones on the sill. Hana touched the green one. Sharing the pocket had given Bram a way into the day, and it had given her a spiral she could not have built with tight hands. Outside, the tide began its long thoughtful return, and the children were glad the loose stones would be free to shine again for someone else.
