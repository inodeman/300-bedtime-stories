Edgar bounced on his toes at the little platform, his ticket held so tightly that the corner curled. The village train was not in yet. Papa sat on the green bench and patted the wood beside him. "We are early," Papa said. "The clock over the ticket window still has a whole song left in it." Edgar looked at the round clock. The hand moved so slowly he wondered if it had forgotten its job. "I want the train now," he said. "My feet are already going, and the train is being late on purpose." Papa shook his head kindly. "It is not late. It is on its way, the same as bread in the oven. Waiting is part of the ride."

A poster on the shelter showed three ducks in a row. Edgar tried to stare past it, down the track, but the track only showed a gentle curve and some tall grass. No engine. No whistle. He sighed so hard his fringe lifted. "How many minutes?" he asked. Papa pointed at a small chalkboard. "Three stops away, then two, then us. Watch the board, not the empty rail." A woman in a blue coat changed the number with a piece of chalk. The squeak was a tiny sound. Edgar watched the number become a two. "That is something," he admitted. "But my bounce does not know about chalk."

A small child nearby held a ticket upside down and looked ready to fold it into a fan. The child's father was tying a shoe. Edgar glanced at Papa, who nodded. "You can help without leaving the platform," Papa said. Edgar walked two steps, still close. "The shiny side faces you," he told the child. "And if you hold the corner, it will not flop." The child copied him. "Like a flag," the child said. Edgar smiled. "Like a flag that stays still until the train says go." Helping used up some of the wiggles. His shoulders dropped a little.

The board changed to one. Edgar's bounce tried to return. He sat, then stood, then sat again. Papa offered him a smooth stone from his pocket, the kind they picked near the canal dock. "Pass it from hand to hand," Papa said. "Each pass can be a count." Edgar passed the stone. One. Two. Three. He listened between the numbers. A bicycle ticked past the fence. A bird said the same word four times. Somewhere a kite on the common made a soft papery sound, too far to see. "The world is not stopped," Edgar said. "Only the train is still around the bend."

"That is a patient sentence," Papa said. "Say what you notice, and the minutes stop feeling like a wall." Edgar tried. "I notice the bench is warm. I notice the chalk dust. I notice that my ticket fits in my pocket if I do not bend it." He slid the ticket in and kept one finger on it so it would not hide. The small child waved the ticket the right way up. Edgar waved back. His hurry was still there, but it had a chair to sit on.

Then a bell rang once, clear and round. The rails began a low hum, like a big cat purring under the ground. "Now," Papa said, not loudly. The little train came around the curve, green and cream, with a polite whistle that was more hello than hurry. Edgar stood up without jumping. He felt proud of that. Doors opened. A conductor with a kind face leaned out. "Meadow Stop," she called. "All aboard who are ready, and no rushing on the step." Edgar showed his ticket. The conductor clipped it and handed him the stub. "You kept this tidy," she said. "Tidy tickets ride well."

Inside, the seats were soft and a little faded. Edgar chose the window because he had waited and wanted the prize of looking. The train rolled so gently that his stone did not roll off his knee. Fields moved like slow pages. At the next stop, only a short way, people got on with baskets and smiles. Edgar did not ask if they were there yet. He watched a horse lift its head and a line of laundry wave. "We will know the hill lookout platform when the sign has a painted bench," Papa said. Edgar nodded. "I can wait for a sign. I practiced."

When they stepped down, Edgar still held his stub. He looked back at the little train as it hummed away, unbothered and on time. "It came when it was meant to," he said. "And I was not a grumble the whole time." Papa laughed. "You were a small grumble at the start. Then you became a helper and a listener. That is how patience grows, a minute at a time." They walked the short path home with the stone and the stub clicking softly in Edgar's pocket like two quiet friends.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as Edgar set the ticket stub on the sill. He did not glue it yet. He just let it rest where the light could find the clipped edge. "Tomorrow I might wait for something else," he told Papa. "Maybe for the kettle, or for my turn on the swing." Papa warmed his hands near the glass. "Then you already know what to do with your eyes and your hands," he said. Edgar passed the smooth stone once more, just to hear the quiet, and the evening felt wide enough for waiting.
