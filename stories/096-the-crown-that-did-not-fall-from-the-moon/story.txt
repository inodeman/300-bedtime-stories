Soren set a silver paper crown on the table where the window lamp could find every shiny scrap. He had cut the points himself and glued them to a band that fit his head. For a moment a pretend story rose in him, bright as the scraps. He could say the crown had fallen from the moon, a medal for star watchers. The sentence felt sparkly in his mouth. Grandpa was on the balcony with the cardboard telescope, and the crickets were singing, and the room was too honest for a sparkle that was not true. Soren touched the crown. It crackled like paper, because it was paper.

Grandpa came in, cheeks cool from the night air, and saw the crown. "That is handsome," he said. "Where did you find it?" The question was kind. It still asked for a real answer. Soren's toes wiggled in his slippers. He put the crown on, then took it off again. "I was going to say the sky gave it to me," he admitted. "That sounded grand. The true thing is I made it at this table from silver paper and glue. The points are a little uneven. I want the true thing to be the one I tell."

Grandpa sat down on the quilt and patted the cushion beside him. "Thank you. A true story is stronger than a shiny pretend one. Making is already wonderful. You do not need to borrow the moon's fame." Soren felt his shoulders drop. Honesty had not popped the evening like a balloon. It had set the crown more firmly on the table, where it belonged. He showed Grandpa the glue smudge and the point he had redone twice. Grandpa admired the redo. "That second try is in the story too," he said.

They carried the crown to the balcony, not to fool the garden, but to see it under the real moon. The paper looked brave and homemade, which Soren now liked better than magic he had not earned. He held the cardboard tube and found the moon, then lowered the tube and looked at his crown. "Different shines," he said. Grandpa agreed. They spotted the Big Dipper. Soren said, "I will not claim I hung those stars either." They both laughed, a warm laugh that made room for the joke and the lesson.

Inside, hot milk waited. Soren poured two cups and told the whole making story from the first snip to the last point. He did not skip the part where a scrap stuck to his sleeve. Grandpa listened as if the tale were a constellation worth learning. Then Soren stuck one glow-in-the-dark sticker inside the crown's band so a small true glow would hide there, charged by the lamp, ready for a dim moment. "The glow is from the sticker," he said carefully. "Not from a star I caught." Grandpa tapped the table once, like a seal of approval.

Soren hung a paper star on the window and wrote his name small on the back, plus the word made. He wanted future Soren to remember the good feeling of telling the truth quickly. The pretend story had been a pebble in his shoe. The true story was a slipper that fit. He sipped his milk. The crickets did not demand medals. They simply sang. He decided the garden was a good teacher of plainness.

Before bed he let Grandpa try the crown for one silly minute. It sat high on Grandpa's hair and made them both grin. Then Soren put it on the shelf, not in a secret box. Honest things could be seen. He folded the leftover silver scraps into a square so they could become another project, maybe a small moon for the telescope, and he would say he made that too. The lamp hummed. The quilt welcomed him. His chest felt light.

He looked out once more and spoke to the moon without pretending it spoke back in words. "You stay real. I will too." Grandpa heard and nodded. They turned the cups upside down on the rack. Soren lined his slippers under the bed. The crown's silver points caught a last bit of gold from the lamp and then rested. Honesty had kept the shine and added something better, trust.

Soren almost added one extra fancy line while Grandpa rinsed the cups. He wanted to say a famous star helper had guided his scissors. He caught the line and replaced it. "A ruler guided the band, and my own hand guided the ruler," he said. Grandpa turned, smiling. "That sentence fits a maker." Soren wrote it on a scrap and tucked the scrap under the crown so the true version would stay close. They looked once more through the cardboard tube, and Soren described only what he really saw: a bright moon, a calm garden, and crickets he could hear but not see. Honest looking matched honest telling. He felt the paper crown become more his, not less, because the story around it finally matched the glue and the scissors.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the paper crown and the little sticker hidden in its band. Soren climbed under the quilt with a true story for a blanket. Outside, the crickets and the moon went on being themselves, and that was more than enough glory for one kind night.
