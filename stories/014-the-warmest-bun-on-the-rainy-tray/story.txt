Rain stitched silver lines on the bakery window. Inside, the air was golden and smelled of honey buns. Bea stood at the counter with paper bags stacked like pale envelopes. Papa pulled a tray from the oven, and the buns shone, each one glazed and dotted with sesame. Steam rose and fogged his glasses. He laughed and set them lower. "The street is a river today," he said. "Anyone who comes in will want a bun that still remembers the oven." Bea nodded. Her own stomach answered with a polite interest. She had permission to choose one bun for her afternoon plate. The nearest bun, front left, was the puffiest, the warmest, the one her hand wanted.

The bell over the door did not ring yet. Rain did. It drummed the awning and made the lane smell like wet stone and yeast when the door cracked open for a delivery boy and then closed. Bea wiped the counter. She lined up the bags. She thought about Mr. Sato, the mail carrier, whose blue cap darkened at the brim on days like this. He always came after the second clock-chime, letters dry inside his sack, his cuffs often not dry at all. He never asked for the best bun. He asked for whatever was simple. Bea touched the puffiest bun's paper cup and then did not take it. "This one can wait for him," she said. Papa heard. "Then it is a kept promise, not a leftover."

She moved that bun to a small plate and covered it with a clean bowl so the glaze would stay soft and the counter drafts would not cool it. Under the bowl it was a secret sun. Bea served other customers: a girl who bought rolls for soup, a man who wanted the crustiest loaf, a neighbor trading a joke for a smile. Each time the bell rang, Bea looked up. Each time it was not the blue cap. She did not give the covered bun away. Kindness, she was learning, sometimes looked like saving. Her own snack could be a corner piece that was already cool. Cool was still sweet. Warm was a gift with a direction.

The second chime came, muffled by rain. Then the bell, and Mr. Sato, shaking his sleeves outside the threshold so the floor would not become a pond. "Permission to enter dripping politely," he said. Bea laughed. "Granted." His hands were pink from the wet air. He set his sack on the dry bench and flexed his fingers. "A plain roll if you have one, and a minute of your warm room." Bea lifted the bowl. The bun underneath sent up a curl of honey smell. The glaze caught the light. "This one has been waiting," she said. "It is the warmest. It has your name on it, even though the name is invisible." Mr. Sato blinked. "Invisible names are my specialty," he said softly. "I deliver them all day."

He did not eat standing up in a rush. Papa poured tea. Mr. Sato wrapped both hands around the cup first, then around the bun, and the paper cup crackled. "Heat in the palms is a kind of mail too," he said. Bea felt proud in a quiet pocket of her ribs. She took her cool corner piece and ate it at the end of the counter, not asking for a trade. The honey was enough. Sesame snapped gently between her teeth. Rain applauded the awning. Mr. Sato told them about a dog who supervised his route from a dry porch, serious as a mayor. Bea listened and refilled the tea without being asked, because refilling was a cousin of saving the bun.

When he stood to go, his cuffs were still damp, but his shoulders sat lower. Bea tucked an extra napkin around a second bun, not the warmest this time, just a good one, in case the route was long. "For the far end of the street," she said. "If you do not want it, give it to the porch mayor." Mr. Sato chuckled. "The mayor accepts biscuits, I happen to know." He paid fairly. He always did. At the door he looked back. "Thank you for thinking of me before I arrived. That is a warm thing all by itself." The bell rang him out into the silver rain. Bea watched the blue cap until it turned the corner by the bookshop.

She washed the secret-sun bowl. The plate underneath had a ring of glaze she ate with a fingertip, a last sweetness, and she did not feel she had missed out. The tray was nearly empty, which meant the bakery had fed a wet day. Papa bumped her shoulder gently with his elbow. "You noticed the person still out in the weather," he said. "That is baking too." Bea straightened the bags for the next rush. Her hands smelled like honey and paper. Outside, puddles held pieces of sky and pieces of awning, shaken into rings whenever a drop landed.

She stayed until the rain thinned to a sparkle. The oven ticked as it cooled. Bea hung her apron and put her cool-bun crumbs in the compost, nothing wasted. She thought of pink fingers warming, of a dog on a porch, of a bun that had waited under a bowl like a kind secret. The street shone. Someone's bicycle hissed through a puddle and laughed with its bell.

The lantern by the window glowed softly against the rainy glass as Bea buttoned her coat. She looked once more at the empty place where the warmest bun had rested. Kindness had been a cover, a wait, and a plate set toward the door. She stepped out under Papa's big umbrella, dry enough, glad that somewhere down the lane a blue cap was warmer than the rain.
