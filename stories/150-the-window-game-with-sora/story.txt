Mireia climbed the bus steps with Papa and chose the seat that framed the lane like a moving picture. They were riding three stops to the hill path, not to climb today, only to meet Auntie where the lookout trail began and walk her home the flat way. The ride was short, familiar, and full of windows. A child Mireia did not know sat across the aisle with a grandmother, knees together, hands on a paper ticket. The child looked at the glass as if it were a book. Mireia knew that feeling. "Papa," she whispered, "may I talk if I am polite?" "If the words are gentle and the other child wants them," Papa said.

Mireia leaned only a little. "I am Mireia. I like the window. Do you?" The child blinked, then nodded. "I am Sora. I like the window because it brings things and then lets them go without me having to chase." The grandmother smiled. "We are visiting the common stop, one before yours, I think." Mireia felt a small click of gladness. A friend did not have to be an old friend. A friend could start as a person who also loved a pane of glass. "Want to play spotters?" she asked. "We name what we see, and we do not shout."

Sora's shoulders loosened. "I will start. Red door." Mireia found it. "Red door. Now a bicycle with a yellow basket." Sora found the basket and added, "And a dog sitting as if the bicycle were a sofa." They laughed softly, the bus kind of laugh that does not bounce other passengers. Papa and the grandmother traded a look that meant this was fine. Stop by stop, the game built a string of shared seeing: a letter box, a kite too far to hear, a canal gleam, a bench with nobody on it and still looking useful. "The bench is waiting the way friends wait," Sora said. Mireia liked that sentence so much she repeated it once, to keep it.

At the common stop, Sora did not get off in a rush. "We have one more minute," the grandmother said. "The driver is helping a stroller, slowly and kindly." Mireia pulled a spare pencil from her pocket. On the back of her own ticket stub she drew a tiny window and wrote, "Red door, yellow basket, patient bench." She held it out. "You can keep the list. It is not the whole ride. It is the part we did together." Sora took it as if it were a ticket to somewhere good. "Then you need the next things," Sora said, and on a corner of the stub the grandmother still held, they had no room, so Sora spoke them instead. "I will remember green hedge and your green sleeves."

"Green sleeves," Mireia said, looking at her arms, surprised to be part of the view. The idea made friendship feel like a window that worked both ways. She was not only the girl who spotted. She was also a color in someone else's ride. "If your grown-up says yes, we could play spotters again another Thursday," she said. "We are often on this bus when the library van is out, but today is the hill path day." Sora nodded hard. "I will ask. Asking is how a small hello becomes a plan." The grandmother nodded too. "We know this route. We can look for green sleeves."

The stroller was settled, and the bus rolled. At the next stop Sora and the grandmother stood. Sora lifted the stub list. "Thank you for not keeping the window to yourself." Mireia felt the sentence land in a warm place. "Thank you for playing without making it a contest." They did not hug across the aisle. They waved, which was the right size. Papa said, "That was well done," as the doors folded shut. Mireia watched Sora on the pavement until the bus turned, and even then she could picture the red door and the patient bench as if they were still shared.

Auntie was exactly where the path sign stood, not a step farther, with a scarf the color of tea. The walk home was flat and known. Mireia told her about Sora between the hedges. "We made a list out of looking," she said. "I think that is a kind of friendship." Auntie tucked Mireia's hand into the crook of an elbow. "The best kind for a bus. It does not need a long journey. It needs a person who makes room in the view."

At home Mireia set her own leftover stub on the table and tried to remember Sora's half of the game. She drew the green hedge and a small pair of green sleeves, then a bench with two dots for sitters. Papa looked over her shoulder. "You left a seat empty on purpose?" "No," Mireia said. "Both dots are sitting. One is me and one is a new friend. The bench looked lonely, but it was only waiting."

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the little drawing. Mireia did not tape it high, only leaned it where the light could read it. "Next time I will save a wider scrap," she said, "so Sora's words and mine can fit on one page." Papa turned the lantern a breath so the glow spread. "Then your friendship will have room to grow without leaving the lane." Outside, an evening bus hummed past, full of windows, and Mireia hoped one of them held a careful list and a child named Sora.
