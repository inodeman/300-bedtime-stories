Wendy waited on the dock beside the canal boat Little Reed, which was staying tied today, fenders nudging the wood with soft thumps. Cousin Bea was visiting from the next street but one, close enough to walk, and Bea was shy about new seats, even seats that did not move. Grandpa was already on the bench with a tin of biscuits. Wendy had saved the best dry cushion, the blue one with no damp corner, and she had set it where a person could see the water and still hold the rail. "This seat is yours if you want it," Wendy said when Bea arrived, shoes careful on the boards. "If you do not want it, the bench loves company too."

Bea looked at the cushion, then at the ropes, then at Grandpa. "The boat will not go?" "It will not go," Grandpa said. "Ms. Fenn left it napping on purpose. We are here to sit, snack, and watch. Napping boats are very good at that." Bea sat. Her shoulders stayed near her ears for a moment, then lowered when the cushion proved steady. Wendy sat beside her, not so close that elbows bumped. "I saved it because friends make room," Wendy said. "I did not save it to be fancy. I saved it so you would not have to wonder where to be."

They watched a duck negotiate a reed and decide the reed could wait. Bea almost smiled. Wendy offered the biscuit tin. "Plain ones on the left, oat ones on the right. The right side has more crumbs, which means it is popular." Bea took a plain one. "Thank you for saying which side. Choices are easier when they are narrated." Wendy liked that word, narrated. She tried it. "I can narrate the canal. That ripple is a bug. That shine is sky lying down. That red fender is so the boat does not bump too hard while it naps." Bea nodded, and the smile finished arriving. "You make a new place feel labeled. Labels are kind."

Wendy pulled a folded paper and a short pencil from her pocket. "Want to draw a staying-still map? Not for going far. For remembering this dock." Bea's eyes brightened in a quiet way. "I am good at small lines." They drew the rail, the coil of rope, the bench with Grandpa as a friendly rectangle, the duck as a careful oval. Bea added a cushion colored in with the side of the pencil. Wendy added two dots and, after a glance, asked, "May I label the dots us?" Bea said yes. The label was small: Wendy, Bea, same seat. It was not a big declaration. It was a true note.

A bicycle ticked past on the towpath. The rider waved, and both children waved, which made them a team for a second. Bea laughed, a short bell. "I did not know I was going to wave. Your wave pulled mine out." Wendy said, "That is friendship, I think. A wave that was ready." They added the bicycle as a tiny wheel on the edge of the map so the visit would include the world going by. Grandpa offered the oat side at last. Bea took one crumbly biscuit and managed it neatly over the water without dropping any in, because Wendy had mentioned that crumbs were better in tins than in canals. "You remembered the rule for me," Bea said. "Remembering is a seat too."

They stayed until the duck napped and the map was full enough. Ms. Fenn arrived to check the ropes, saw the children seated safely, and approved with a nod. "Best passengers I have had all afternoon," she said. "You respected a resting boat." Wendy felt a shared gladness. Bea had trusted the cushion, and that trust was worth carrying carefully, like a tin with the lid on. "Come again on a tied-up day," Wendy said. "Or a slow ride day, if grown-ups come. I will save the blue cushion if it is clean." Bea touched the map. "And I will bring a better green for the reeds. This green is a little shy, like I was."

They walked Bea to the corner where Uncle met them, all in sight of the dock. Nobody had to search. Wendy gave Bea the map because Bea was the visitor, and visitors deserve the souvenir. "I can draw another at home," Wendy said. "Then we will have twins, and twins can visit each other." Bea held the paper flat. "I will put mine by our window. When I see it, I will think of the labeled seat." They waved until the corner turned into ordinary street. Wendy's hand stayed warm from the waving.

On the way back to their own lane, Grandpa said, "You did not fill every quiet with noise. You left room for Bea to arrive." Wendy thought about the cushion, the narrated ripples, the two dots. "Friendship is a saved seat and a pencil," she said. "And a biscuit side that is explained." Grandpa chuckled. "Add patience to the list, and you have a dock full." The thumps of the fenders faded behind them. Home was a few minutes of familiar pavement, no more.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on Wendy's second map, the twin she drew at the table so Bea would not be the only one with a paper dock. She labeled the dots again, neat as she could. "The boat stayed, and the friendship started anyway," she told Grandpa. He set the biscuit tin beside the glow, empty and crumb-free. Outside, the evening was calm enough for napping boats and new cousins, and the light in the glass kept a seat warm in the window, the way Wendy had kept a cushion ready on the dock.
