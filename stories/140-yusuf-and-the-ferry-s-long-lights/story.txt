The ferry window was a wide rectangle of evening water. Yusuf sat with Nana on the bench beside it, a blank postcard on his knee and a pencil that had not done much work. The ride to his uncle's landing took the slow path. He had expected games or at least a parade of boats. Instead the water repeated itself, dark blue with a few gold lines. "There is nothing happening," he said, not rudely, just honestly. Nana's knitting needles clicked a calm code. "Nothing loud," she said. "Look until the quiet shows its stitches." Yusuf was not sure quiet had stitches. He looked because she had asked, and because the postcard was embarrassing in its emptiness.

First he noticed the smell, which was a kind of picture. Rope, salt, and a sweet hint of the orange Nana had peeled. He wrote the word rope in the corner of the card, very small. Then he noticed their reflections floating on the glass over the real water, two soft copies who waved when he waved. He liked them. They were the ferry's way of sketching him back. He added a tiny pair of reflected shoulders. The card stopped being a blank. It became a start. "The window is drawing us," he told Nana. She smiled without losing a stitch.

The gold lines were lights from the shore, stretched by the water into long brushes. A red light became a red road. A white light became a trembling ladder. Yusuf tried to copy one ladder before it broke into sparkles. His pencil made a zigzag he liked better than a perfect copy. A bell buoy out in the dark said one word at a time, patient as a clock that only spoke when a wave asked. Yusuf wrote bell and drew a circle. Wonder was arriving in pieces, like the knitting growing row by row in Nana's lap. He no longer wanted a parade. The parade was made of lines and smells and one-word bells.

A deckhand walked past with a coil of rope and nodded. Yusuf asked, in a voice that fit the cabin, what the rope was for. "For keeping us friendly with the dock," the deckhand said. "Loose enough to breathe, tight enough to stay." Yusuf liked that so much he wrote breathe in the sky of his postcard. The deckhand went on. The ferry's engine hummed under the bench, a cat-purr large enough to sit inside. Yusuf put his palm on the wood and felt the purr. He added a small wave-line for it. Nana glanced over. "Your empty card is getting crowded in the best way," she said. Yusuf grinned. Crowded with noticing was a good crowd.

They passed a low island that looked like a sleeping animal covered in bushes. Yusuf did not need it to be a real animal. He drew its back as a long curve and gave it three bush-dots for texture. A gull rode alongside for a minute, then chose another plan. He let it leave the card. Not every wonder had to be captured. Some were just for eyes. He told Nana that, and she said it was the sort of sentence uncles liked to hear. Yusuf practiced it quietly: some lights are for keeping, some for letting go. The gold ladders trembled and reformed. He watched a whole one shine and fade, beautifully, without needing it to stay. It was only light, playing.

When the landing lamps grew from seeds into lanterns, Yusuf held the postcard up to the window and saw his drawing and the real water overlap. Shoulders, rope-word, bell circle, island back, zigzag ladder. The ride had not been empty. It had been a slow book he had almost refused to open. Nana packed her knitting. "You found the stitches," she said. Yusuf nodded. The problem had been a long ride and a blank card and a wish for noise. Wonder had been the pencil that moved once he let the quiet count. He tucked the card into his pocket where it would not bend.

On the dock the air was cooler and the ropes did their friendly job. Yusuf touched one with a finger and felt the braid. He would tell his uncle about loose enough and tight enough. The ferry sighed in the way big boats rest. Lights still stretched on the water behind them, a last show for anyone still looking. Yusuf looked until Nana's hand suggested the path. He could carry the show in the postcard and in his head. Both pockets, he thought, were full enough.

At home, after the visit's stories and a bowl of soup, he set the postcard on the sill where it could dry its little pencil shine. He told the whole list without rushing. The blank feeling was gone, replaced by a map of a ride he would take again on purpose. Nana patted his shoulder and said the window would be there next time. Yusuf corrected nothing. He only added, "And the bell that speaks when a wave asks." That sentence felt like a good last stitch.

Yusuf sharpened nothing and erased nothing. The postcard was allowed to keep its zigzag and its small words. He read them in order: rope, bell, breathe, and the island curve that had no word at all. Nana's knitting rested in its bag like a sleeping row of stitches. He decided the next ride could start with the pencil already willing, so the first minute would not be a blank. Willing, he thought, was a kind of wonder you could pack beside an orange. The bench at home was not a ferry, yet the window still held a rectangle of night worth a long look.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the postcard's zigzag ladder. Yusuf watched the real lantern and the drawn lights and let them be cousins. Somewhere a ferry hummed like a great cat, and ropes kept a dock friendly. He had thought the evening was empty, and it had been full of quiet stitches. Wonder had sat down beside him until he noticed. He closed his eyes on gold roads that trembled and stayed beautiful.
