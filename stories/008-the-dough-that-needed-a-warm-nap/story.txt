Flour dusted the bakery table like a light snow that smelled sweet. Ellis stood on his stool with his sleeves rolled, watching the bowl. Inside it, the dough was a smooth pale mound, smaller than Auntie June had promised it would become. "It looks finished," Ellis said. "It looks like we could bake it now and have bread for the early customers." Auntie June shook her head in an easy way, not a scolding one. She draped a clean cloth over the bowl. "It is only at the start of its nap. Yeast is a tiny worker. If we rush the nap, the bread will be a brick that smells nice and chews sad." Ellis touched the cloth. It was warm from the bowl. He wanted to peek.

The kitchen held other smells: butter from the morning buns, a hint of rosemary, the metal warmth of the oven that was not yet hot enough for loaves. A clock on the shelf ticked. Ellis tried to think of the ticking as a helper. He washed the sticky scrapes from his fingers. Dough glue pulled at his skin in soft strings, then let go. He dried his hands and lined up the scrapers, the big one and the little one, until their edges were even. "If I cannot peek," he said, "what job is a good job?" Auntie June pointed her chin at the seed jars. "Toast the seeds slowly in the dry pan. Low flame. Stir. That is patience with a spoon."

Ellis stood at the stove. Sesame seeds slid and whispered in the pan. He stirred so none stayed too long in the hot middle. They turned a shade deeper, the color of tea with milk, and they smelled nutty and round. "They are ready," he said, and slid them into a bowl before they browned too far. Auntie June nodded. "You stopped at the right minute. That is the same skill the dough needs, only the dough's minute is longer." Ellis glanced at the covered bowl. The cloth did not move. He imagined the yeast waking up under there, making tiny bubbles, lifting the dough the way a breath lifts a chest. He could not hear it. He believed it anyway, a little more than he had a moment ago.

He swept flour from the table into his palm and dropped it back into the flour bin, so nothing tasty was wasted. A customer knocked at the front, and Auntie June went to sell buns. Ellis stayed with the dough like a quiet guard. The hardest moment was the middle, when sweeping was done and seeds were done and the clock still had the same face. He pressed his hands under his arms to keep them from lifting the cloth. "Nap means nap," he told the bowl. A fly buzzed near the window and left when it found no sugar spill. Ellis wiped a spot of honey he noticed on the jar rim, a small shine, and the rag smelled golden.

Auntie June returned with empty trays and flour on her eyebrow. "Time to look," she said. Ellis lifted the cloth as if it were a curtain at a tiny play. The dough had grown. It filled the bowl in a soft dome, smooth and puffed, with a few pale bubbles along the side. He laughed. "It did get bigger without me poking it." "That is its talent," Auntie June said. She showed him how to flour his fists and fold the dough gently, once, twice, so the big bubbles became small even ones. The dough sighed, a soft pillow sound. It was warm and alive in a bakery way, not a pet way, and Ellis treated it with both palms and no squeezing.

They shaped a round loaf and set it on a peel to rise a second, shorter time. Ellis watched the oven thermometer climb. He did not ask to put the bread in early. He sprinkled the toasted seeds on top with a light hand, then misted them so they would stay. When Auntie June nodded, he slid the loaf in. Heat breathed over his face, a dry hug, and the door closed with a firm kind sound. "Now another wait," he said, proud that he knew. "The baking wait." They cleaned the bowl together. Warm water, no rush, circles of the sponge. Through the oven window the loaf slowly blushed gold.

The smell changed from dough to bread, a deeper toast smell that filled the lane when the door opened. Ellis tapped the bottom of the loaf the way he had been taught, with a knuckle, and it sounded hollow and pleasant. "Done," Auntie June said. They set it on a rack. The crust crackled as it cooled, tiny clicks like secret applause. Ellis did not cut it at once. Steam needed to settle or the middle would gum. He waited, counting clicks, until Auntie June passed him the serrated knife. The slice showed an even crumb, holes like small windows. Butter melted and ran. The taste was nutty from the seeds and soft from the nap.

Ellis carried a second slice to the front for the next customer who wanted to taste the new loaf. He felt taller than the stool. The clock still ticked, and now he liked it. Waiting had not been empty. It had been full of seeds, sweeping, and a dome growing under a cloth. He wiped the counter until it showed his blurry smile.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the cooling rack as the morning tipped toward noon. Ellis looked at the remaining loaf, round and fragrant, and at his buttered crumb on the plate. Patience had turned a small mound into bread that sounded hollow when it was ready and tasted like time well kept. He hung his apron up, shook flour from his cuff, and stepped into the lane still warm from the oven, glad he had let the dough finish its nap.
