Nura pulled a brown sock over her hand and flexed the heel until it made a mouth. Oona pulled on a blue sock with a white toe that looked like a chin. Felt ears, already glued and dry, stood up on both. Yarn tails hung down. Mama had sewn the button eyes earlier, firm and safe, and now the needle tin was closed. "A play needs two," Nura said. "If I do every voice, it is just me in a tunnel. If we team, the socks can meet." Oona nodded. "I will be the river keeper. You be the bridge builder. They both want the picnic to cross."

They sat on the rug and planned with words before they performed, because teamwork starts in the planning. Nura said, "The bridge builder has planks of cardboard." She set three smooth scraps down. Oona said, "The river keeper knows where the water is deep. The deep parts can be the blue scarf on the floor." They laid the scarf out, a soft river that smelled like the drawer, clean and cool. The rule was simple. No puppet would push the other into the river. They would solve the crossing together. "If we disagree," Oona added, "we pause and use our real voices, then go back to puppet voices."

They began. The bridge builder bowed so low the yarn tail tapped the scarf. "I brought planks," Nura said in a rumbly kind voice. "I know a shallow line," Oona answered, higher and bright. "Put the first plank where I pat." The blue sock patted the rug. The brown sock set a cardboard plank. It worked. Then Nura, excited, set the second plank far ahead, into the deepest blue. Oona's puppet shook its head. "That is the fast water." Nura felt a little sting, not a mean one, just the sting of being wrong in public, even if the public was only Oona. She remembered the rule. She slipped the sock off halfway. "Real voice. I wanted a long brave plank. You are saying it lands in the deep. I believe you. Where should it go?"

Oona's shoulders loosened. "Real voice. Thank you. Put it here, closer, and we can add a third." They smiled and went back into the play. The planks made a path. A button berry, the picnic, crossed one plank at a time, pushed by both puppet paws. The button clicked, a tiny drum. When the last plank wobbled, they both pressed it flat, four hands counting as two puppets. "Team press," the river keeper said. "Team press," the bridge builder echoed. The berry arrived at a napkin island. They cheered in whispers so the scarf river would not get splashed by excitement, a silly care that made them laugh. Nura wiggled her sock until the felt ear flopped, and Oona answered with a small bow that made the white toe nod.

They ran the play a second time for Papa, who sat on the floor and became a respectful audience of one. The second time was smoother. The pause still happened, because they kept it in the story as a moment when the builders checked a map. Papa clapped softly at the end. Nura wanted to say they had made the best play in the house. She said, instead, "Oona knew the river. I knew the planks. The picnic needed both." Oona said, "And the pause saved the plank." Papa nodded. "That is a strong team. Strong can be gentle." The words fit the room. The socks looked pleased, as much as socks can, ears up, buttons shining.

They put the puppets on the windowsill to rest, mouths closed, tails neatened. Nura retied a yarn tail that had loosened, a small repair with a bow. Oona folded the river scarf and returned it to the drawer, because the river's job was done and the scarf still had a job as a scarf. The cardboard planks went into the craft box, labeled with a pencil scrap that said bridge, so the team could find them again. The button berry went back to the tin. Nothing was lost. The story stayed in their heads, ready to be built once more.

Nura flexed her fingers, free of the sock, and felt the warm print the wool had left. Oona did the same. "Same hands, different jobs," Oona said. "That is why it worked." They drank milk from the blue cup and the wide cup that lived on the tray, a sip each, and wiped a milk smile off with the backs of their wrists. Teamwork had tasted like cardboard clicks and a pause and a berry that arrived. It would work on other projects too, they thought, whenever two people had two kinds of knowing.

The lantern by the window glowed softly between the brown puppet and the blue one, and their button eyes held little dots of light. Nura and Oona looked at the pair they had made and the play they had carried together. The socks were finished. The story was finished. The team was the part they could keep using, even after the river was folded away.
