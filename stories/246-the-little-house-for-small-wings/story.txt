Kofi stood on the sunny back step with a wide brush and a pot of sky-blue paint, while Laila sorted the wooden pieces Uncle had already cut and smoothed. The wood smelled like fresh pencils and a little like the shed, clean and dry, not dark or frightening. Each board had soft edges from Uncle's sanding. There was a front, a back, two sides, a floor, and a roof in two slopes. A round doorway had been made ahead of time, just big enough for a small bird and not for a hand to go exploring. "We are a team," Kofi said. "I can paint. You can check the plan. Uncle fits the parts, because the screwdriver is his job."

Laila spread a paper drawing on the step, held down with a stone. "Walls first, then roof, then the perch stick," she read. "And we do not put the house where the afternoon sun bakes it." She looked across the yard. A leafy bush made a kind shade near the fence, and the kitchen window could see that spot. "There," she said. "Birds like a quiet branch and a way to look around." Kofi dipped his brush. The first stroke whispered over the grain and turned the pale wood into morning. Paint speckles dotted his wrist. "Your turn to choose the roof color," he said, because a team shares the deciding.

Laila chose a deep green, like the underside of a leaf. She painted the roof slopes with short, careful strokes so the drips would not run onto the blue. When a drip did start, Kofi caught it with a rag before it could make a river. "Good team catch," Laila said. They left the pieces on a rack of sticks to dry and drank cool water that tasted like the metal cup and a squeeze of orange. Bees worked in the clover. A sparrow landed on the fence, flicked its tail, and flew, a brown blur with a bright peep. Laila whispered, "We are making a porch for someone like you."

Uncle came out with the screwdriver in his apron pocket and let the children bring him the dry boards in order. Kofi held the side piece steady. Laila held the front. Uncle turned the screws, the metal ticking softly, while the children kept the corners square. "A little higher, Kofi," Uncle said. Kofi lifted. "Now still." Being still was part of the work. Laila's arms trembled, and she breathed out slowly until they steadied. The walls became a small room. The smell of paint mixed with the green smell of cut grass. "Doorway facing the bush," Laila reminded them. They turned the house together, three pairs of hands, until the round door looked toward the leaves.

The roof was next. Kofi and Laila held the green slopes while Uncle set them in place. A gap showed on one side, thin as a pencil line. "We can close that," Uncle said, and he pressed a thin wooden lip he had prepared into the gap. Laila passed him the piece without being asked, because she had seen it on the step and remembered the plan. "That was the thinking part," Kofi said, grinning. "I was the holding part. Both parts make a house." They slid the perch stick beneath the door, and Uncle fixed it so it would not spin loose. Neither child picked up the screwdriver. Their hands stayed on wood, brushes, and the plan.

They carried the finished house to the shady spot, Uncle lifting, the children guiding so it would not bump the fence. Under the floor they set a flat stone so damp ground would not soak the wood. Laila scattered a small handful of seeds on a low dish nearby, not inside the house, so the seeds would not go moldy in the nest place. Kofi filled a jar lid with clean water and set it a few steps away. "A drink and a snack, and a room if they want it," he said. The blue walls looked calm against the green bush. The round door was a dark, friendly circle.

They sat on the step and watched without shouting. Waiting was also teamwork. A breeze moved the bush. Paint dried a little more in the sun that reached the step but not the house. Kofi wiped a blue thumb on a rag. Laila folded the plan and tucked it into a tin. "If a bird does not come today, the house is still ready," she said. "Ready is a good job." Uncle nodded. "You two kept the order. I only turned the screws." Kofi shook his head, smiling. "You turned the screws and we did the rest. That is a whole team, not a contest."

No one called the birdhouse the finest in the neighborhood. They called it finished, square enough, and placed with care. The paint was smooth under a careful finger, still smelling a little sharp if you leaned close, and the wood was warm on the sunny roof edge and cool in the shade. Laila thought of the sparrow's peep. Kofi thought of the way their hands had matched the corners. Pride sat quietly in their chests, like a bird that did not need to sing to prove it had wings.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when they went inside to wash the brushes, and from that window the blue birdhouse showed as a small calm shape by the bush. Kofi and Laila set the clean brushes in a jar and stood shoulder to shoulder. Their team had made a little house with a round door, a green roof, and a perch. If wings came, there would be room. If wings waited, the house would wait too, steady and kind.
