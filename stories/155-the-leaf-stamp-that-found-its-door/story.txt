Rosa pressed both palms on the post office counter and peered at the wall of cubbies as if they were a puzzle with polite manners. "Mr. Pell," she asked, "how does a letter know which house is mine when so many doors look square?" Mr. Pell's glasses flashed in a friendly way. "Letters do not know. People and patterns do. If you have a little time, and your uncle does too, I can show you the short route our lane uses. No mystery tunnels. Just eyes, labels, and feet." Uncle folded his newspaper. "We have time. Curiosity is the errand today."

Mr. Pell set a blank envelope on the counter. "Write your own name and your house, as if you were sending a note home to yourself." Rosa printed carefully. She did not seal it yet. "Now the stamp," Mr. Pell said, and Rosa chose a green one with a leaf, pressing it flat. "Stamps tell us the letter is allowed to ride. The address tells us where the ride ends." He slid the envelope into a tray marked Lane. "This tray is not a mouth. It is a pause. From here, letters go into cubbies if they are staying nearby, or into the little train sack if they are going one village over. Yours is staying."

Rosa watched him find the cubby labeled with her house number. The card was handwritten and a little faded. "It looks like my door," she said. "Numbers are doors you can read," Mr. Pell replied. He did not drop the letter in to stay. "Would you like to walk the last part yourself, so your curiosity gets fresh air? We will pretend the cubby is the station, and your slot at home is the platform. I will come as far as the corner. Uncle will do the rest." Rosa bounced once. "A letter walk. Yes."

Outside, she held the envelope against her sweater. Mr. Pell narrated like a gentle guide. "First the sorter looks at the town. Ours is this town, so it does not leave. Then the street. Then the number. If a number is missing, the letter waits and asks a question instead of wandering." Rosa nodded hard. "So clear writing is a kindness to the letter." "Exactly," Mr. Pell said. At the corner he stopped, because his counter needed him. "You know the rest of the pavement. Deliver it to your own slot, and you will have seen the whole near journey."

Uncle and Rosa passed the red door, the smile-shaped crack, and the flower tubs. Rosa read the address aloud at each, checking it still matched. "I am still on our lane," she said. "I am comparing." Uncle smiled. "Comparing is curiosity with its shoes on." At their gate she paused. "If I put it in the slot, it has finished, but I will not have read it." "Then read it on the step, and we can still put it through after, so the flap can have its little ceremony," Uncle said. Rosa sat. Inside, her note said, "Dear Rosa at home, the cubby has your number, and the stamp is a leaf. From Rosa at the counter." She laughed. "I sent myself a fact."

They posted it through the slot and heard it land on the mat. Then they walked back in through the door like people, not like envelopes, and picked it up. The loop was so small it felt like a joke the town had told kindly. Rosa turned the envelope over. "I understand the tray, the cubby, the street, and the slot. I do not understand the far-away sacks yet." Uncle tapped the green stamp. "Those are for another curious day, with a grown-up and a timetable. Near is enough for a Tuesday." Rosa agreed. Near had been full of tiny true things.

Back at the post office, because the corner was truly close, she returned the borrowed tray idea as a thank-you drawing: a square, a leaf, a house, and arrows. Mr. Pell stuck it inside the counter, not over anyone's mail. "You asked a how question, not only a what question," he said. "How questions make the world less muddy." Rosa traced an arrow in the air. "Tomorrow I might write to Grandma two streets away and watch which tray she belongs in. I will not open other people's letters. Curiosity has a fence." Mr. Pell looked pleased. "The fence is respect. It keeps wonder welcome."

They bought no extra stamps, because one leaf was the teacher today. On the step at home Rosa told the story again to the empty hall, which did not mind. Uncle made toast. The envelope sat on the table like a graduate of a very short school. Rosa sharpened a pencil and wrote the steps on a card: town, street, number, cubby, feet, slot. She dotted the i's so they would not look like mistakes. "I can teach this to my cousin without leaving the rug," she said.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the card and the leaf stamp. Rosa leaned close until the green ink seemed to drink the light. "A letter finds a house because careful people point it," she said. Uncle set the toast downwind of the paper. "And because a curious girl walked the last bit herself." Outside, the post box stood in its usual place, red and patient, ready for the next question that could be answered close to home.
