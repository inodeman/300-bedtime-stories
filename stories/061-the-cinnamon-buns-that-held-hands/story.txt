Saturday light slid across the neighborhood bakery and made the flour on the counter look like fresh snow. Bea stood on a sturdy stool so she could see into the wide pan. Cinnamon buns crowded together there, golden on top and glossy with orange glaze. Hugo leaned in until his scarf almost touched the steam. Tessa, who had mixed the dough before the market opened, set her wooden spoon down and blinked at the pan.

"They held hands," Hugo said.

Bea smiled. "They stuck. Every bun is hugging the next one."

The buns had risen into one soft spiral. Edges had melted together until the pan held a single cinnamon cloud. Nobody frowned. The bakery was full of bread, and the soup pots on the back stove already hummed for later. The only puzzle was how to share the buns without tearing the pretty swirl into crumbs.

Bea reached toward the pan. Heat touched her fingers, and she pulled them back. "If I tug now, the middle will rip," she said.

"Then we wait," Tessa said. "Waiting is a kind of baking too."

They carried the pan to the shared table by the front window. The table was long enough for neighbors, with room for picnic baskets underneath. A small lantern sat beside a stack of clean plates. Bea could hear the bread ovens ticking as they cooled. Hugo brought a bowl of warm water and a clean cloth. The water smelled faintly of the orange peels Tessa had simmered for the glaze.

Hugo dipped the cloth and wrung it until it was damp, not drippy. "Steam can be a quiet helper," he said.

"We can ask the buns to let go," Bea said. She liked that. It felt polite, like holding a door.

They laid the warm cloth over the pan. The cinnamon smell grew stronger, sweet and woody. A tiny bead of glaze slid into a seam. Bea wanted to peek. Her hands twitched over the cloth.

"Not yet," Tessa said gently. "If we lift it too soon, the warmth runs away. Count the oven ticks with me."

They counted softly. One, two, three, all the way to twenty. Hugo's foot bounced. Bea's eyes stayed on the cloth. Outside, Saturday market voices drifted past the window, bright as spoons tapping honey jars. Still they waited. The waiting felt long, and then it felt like a song they already knew.

When Tessa nodded, Bea lifted a corner. Mist rose, and the seams looked shinier. She tried a gentle pull. The buns wiggled and held on.

"Still friends," Hugo said, grinning at the shiny seam.

"Then more patience," Bea said. She settled the cloth again. This time she hummed so her hands would have a job. The hum was low and round. Hugo hummed a little higher. The two notes made the waiting feel like mixing, even though their hands were still.

They checked once more. Bea slid a buttered spoon into a seam and rocked it, slow as a boat on a calm pond. The bun sighed apart. A curl of cinnamon stayed on both sides, neat as a ribbon. Hugo clapped once, then covered his mouth, as if a loud clap might startle the rest of the pan.

One by one, the buns loosened. Some needed another breath of steam. Some let go at the first polite nudge. Bea did not rush the stubborn corner. "You can take your time," she told it. "We have time, and we have plenty."

Soon twelve buns sat in a loose circle, still warm, still glossy. Cinnamon sugar dusted the table like freckles. Tessa brought the honey jar that glowed the color of late sunlight. She did not pour yet. She let Bea and Hugo decide.

"Do we each take a pile?" Hugo asked.

Bea looked at the long shared table. "Let's keep them in the middle," she said. "Then anyone can choose a piece, and the spiral still feels like one hello."

They did. They broke one bun only to taste the seam. The crumb was soft. The cinnamon was warm on the tongue, and the orange glaze made the last bite bright. Hugo got a sticky thumb and laughed. Bea licked a honey drop from her knuckle and passed the jar so Tessa could have the next turn.

"Patience tastes soft," Tessa said. "Not torn."

Bea nodded. "The buns were stuck, but we were not in a hurry. That was the cheerful step."

Hugo slid a bun toward the center so the pattern looked whole again. Flour still freckled his sleeve. Outside, a market bell made a friendly clang. Inside, the ovens ticked more slowly, full and finished, while the soup pots whispered under their lids.

The lantern by the window glowed softly, a small amber pool beside the empty pan. Bea, Hugo, and Tessa sat with cinnamon on their fingers and honey in the air. The buns waited in the middle of the shared table, ready for anyone who wanted a warm piece. Nothing had been ripped. Nothing had been rushed. The morning felt wide and golden, and the quiet they had practiced sat between them like another loaf, enough for every hand that reached with care.
