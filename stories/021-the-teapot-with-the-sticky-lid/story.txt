Mira carried the blue enamel teapot up the last sandy rise, both palms curved around its warm middle. A cloth napkin kept the handle from biting her fingers. Salt had dried in a pale crust along the lid, fine as sugar, left there by yesterday's bright spray. The dunes smelled of sun-warm grass and the deep clean salt of the shore. Ahead, a gray-winged gull paced the path, stopping now and then as if to show her the firmest sand. "I am watching my feet," Mira told the gull. The gull tipped its head, helpful and calm, then hopped aside so she could pass.

In the hollow below, a yellow picnic blanket waited, its corners pinned with smooth stones. The friendly lighthouse stood white and steady at the edge of the cove, and little wooden boats nodded against their ropes. Nana had set out mugs, a small jar of honey, and a tin of plain biscuits. The lighthouse keeper sat on the blanket's far edge with his shoulders rounded, rubbing his hands. His coat was damp at the cuffs from a morning of checking the tide chart by the rocks. "The wind found every button," he said, trying to smile. "I am glad you brought something hot."

Mira knelt and set the teapot in the center of the blanket, where no one would kick it. Steam should have whispered from the spout. Instead the spout stayed quiet. She reached for the lid to give the tea a stir, the way Nana had shown her, but the lid did not turn. Salt had sealed it. Mira pressed gently, then a little more firmly. The lid stayed stuck, round and stubborn as a shell stuck to a rock. Honey light slid along the blue enamel. Her cheeks grew warm, not from the tea, but from wanting everything to go right.

"Oh," Mira said. "It will not open." Nana leaned in, smelling of soap and orange peel. "Salt loves a tight place," Nana said. "We can warm the rim. Hurry is how lids chip." The keeper rubbed his hands again and looked out at the water, pretending he was not cold. Mira noticed. The biscuits could wait. The view could wait. A person with damp cuffs could not drink the smell of tea. She felt the small problem settle in her chest like a pebble, not heavy, but important. Kindness, she thought, was not a big trumpet sound. It was the next careful thing.

She thought about pouring seawater from the wooden bucket over the lid, but Nana shook her head in a gentle way. "Not the sea for this. Cold water can crack a warm pot." So Mira wrapped the dry napkin around the lid and held the teapot where the sun could rest on the salt. She waited and counted gull wingbeats. A gull landed near the biscuits and did not peck them. It only stood, as if keeping the plate company. "Thank you," Mira whispered. "Those are for sharing after the tea works."

When she tried again, the lid gave a tiny gritty sigh and turned. A ribbon of mint-sweet steam rose, and the picnic hollow smelled suddenly like a kitchen that had gone for a walk. Mira's first wish was to fill her own mug to the brim. The handle was ready. Her hands knew the way. Then she saw the keeper's fingers, still pink from the wind, folded around nothing. She turned the spout toward his mug instead. "Yours first," she said. "You were out by the tide pools before the sun was high." The tea came out amber and steady. She did not spill a drop on the blanket.

The keeper closed both hands around the mug and let out a long, happy breath. "That is the best weather I have felt all day," he said. Steam softened the lines near his eyes. Nana held her mug out next, and Mira poured for her too, then a smaller cup for herself. The gull stepped closer, curious about the steam, and Mira set a biscuit on a clean stone a little way off, not to tame the bird, only to say thank you for the company. The gull took it neatly and flew to a post by the boats, still wild, still free, a crumb bright on its beak.

They ate slowly while the cove glittered. Mira told them how the salt had made a little door and how the sun had opened it. The keeper nodded toward the lighthouse. "People think the big lamp is the only helpful thing out here," he said. "But a warm cup at the right moment can guide a person just as kindly." Mira looked at the boats, the shells along the high-tide line, and a far rock pool where a starfish rested with every arm loose and safe. She felt proud in a quiet way. The tea tasted like mint and honey. Her own mug was not the fullest, and somehow that made each sip sweeter.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the lighthouse cottage as the afternoon leaned toward gold. Mira walked the path with the empty teapot ticking as it cooled, the lid loose and clean against her palm. Nana's hand swung beside hers. Behind them the keeper lifted his mug in a small goodbye. The gull called once, a bright note and not a scold. Mira touched the napkin in her pocket and smiled at the wide glittering shore. She had not stopped the wind. She had only opened a lid and poured for someone else first, and the whole beach felt warmer because she had noticed.
