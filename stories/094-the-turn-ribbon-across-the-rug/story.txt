Luca placed a blue ribbon across the middle of the rug, a gentle line between two sitting spots. The cardboard telescope lay on one side. The other side held two cups and a pitcher of hot milk. "This is the turn ribbon," he told Papa. "When you have the tube, I wait on my side. When I have the tube, you wait on yours. We hum one cricket song, then we trade." Papa's eyebrows rose with pleasure. "That is a fair plan. Who begins?" Luca wanted the first look so much that his fingers tapped. Then he saw Papa's hopeful face and made a sharing choice. "You begin. I will pour."

The window lamp painted the ribbon a deeper blue. Paper stars clicked on the glass. Beyond the balcony, real crickets offered plenty of songs to measure by. Luca poured the milk carefully, a little in Papa's cup, a little in his own, so neither person had a bigger share hiding in the pitcher. He sat on his side of the ribbon with both hands around his cup. Papa lifted the tube, rested it near the door, and looked at the moon. Luca hummed. He did not stretch the song long to steal time, and he did not rush it to grab the tube. He hummed it honestly.

"Trade," Luca said, smiling. Papa passed the telescope with two hands, the way people pass something precious. Luca's turn showed him the moon's bright face and a hint of gray plains. He wanted a second song. The want tugged. He remembered the ribbon. A deal was a kind of promise, and promises were part of sharing. He hummed once, lowered the tube, and passed it back. "Your turn for the spoon stars," he said. Papa found the Big Dipper and described the handle so Luca could enjoy it even while waiting. Sharing, Luca noticed, could include sharing words, not only the object.

They moved the ribbon game onto the balcony for one round, slippers careful on the boards, quilt draped over the indoor chair so it would not get damp. The air was cool and fresh. Luca held the tube while Papa counted the song this time. A cricket near the mint seemed to know it was being used as a clock and sang especially well. They both laughed. Luca gave Papa the last look at the moon without being asked. "You like the bright part," Luca said. "I like that you like it." Papa's thank-you was quiet and full.

Inside, Luca divided four oat biscuits by breaking each into two. Crumbs tried to escape. He corralled them onto a napkin for later, a shared later. He stuck a glow-in-the-dark sticker on each end of the blue ribbon so both sides of the turn line would glow the same. "No favorite side," he said. Papa pressed the stickers flat. They drank until the cups matched in emptiness too. The room felt even, in a cozy way, like a quilt pulled up on both shoulders.

Luca still felt a small pinch when the tube rolled back to Papa's knee. He picked it up and set it on the ribbon's center. "Neutral territory," he announced, a phrase Papa had used for the middle of the table. Papa chuckled. "You are learning the geography of fairness." Luca was not sure about geography, but he was sure the center was a friendly country. He put a paper star on the tube as a flag of that country. Either person could pick the flag up when the next song began.

Before bed they folded the ribbon and stored it in the telescope basket, on top, so tomorrow's sharing would be easy to find. Luca rinsed the cups. Papa wiped the pitcher. The jobs were split without a chart. Luca noticed that sharing had started as a ribbon game and turned into a feeling that spread. He was not poorer for giving the first turn away. He was full of moon pictures and Papa's pleased eyes. The crickets kept singing free songs that did not need to be divided at all.

He stood at the window and touched one paper star. "Goodnight to the things we can share and the sky we do not have to own," he said. Papa rested a hand on his shoulder. They turned the lamp a little lower. Luca's slippers waited, side by side, equal. He climbed under the quilt and left room on the pillow's edge, a silly extra share for his stuffed cloud, who had watched the whole fair game.

Luca noticed the pitcher still held a few sips and almost poured them all into his own cup. The blue ribbon idea tugged his sleeve. He split the last milk, a little stream for Papa and a little stream for himself, until the pitcher was truly empty. "Even the ending can be fair," he said. Papa raised his cup. They sipped together and listened to the crickets count a song nobody had to win. Luca rolled the cardboard tube to the exact center again and set a paper star on top as a paperweight. Sharing, he was learning, showed up in leftovers as much as in first turns. The room stayed peaceful, with no one keeping score and both of them full.

The lantern by the window glowed softly along the place where the blue ribbon had lain. The cardboard tube rested in the basket, ready for two. Luca closed his eyes, glad he had offered the first turn, and the night felt wide enough for everyone he loved.
