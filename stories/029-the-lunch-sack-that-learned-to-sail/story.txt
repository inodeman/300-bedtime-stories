Briony sat on the warm boards of the little dock with a toy boat in her lap and a problem the size of a handkerchief. The boat was real wood, small enough for one hand underneath, with a mast made from a straight bit of reed. What it did not have was a sail. The cloth scrap she had planned to use was now a bandage for Uncle's thumb after a splinter, and the thumb mattered more. A gull walked the dock's edge, peered at the empty mast, and offered no cloth either, only friendly company. The cove lay smooth and willing. "You look ready," Briony told the boat. "I am the one who is not dressed for the job."

Her lunch sack, brown and wrinkled, waited beside an apple core she had already wrapped. She held the sack up to the light. It was thin, but it was a sheet if she cut one side with the round-tipped scissors from the cottage. "A sail does not have to start as a sail," she said. The idea fizzed. She would need color so she could see it against the bright water, and she would need it to be a little sturdy. Berry juice from breakfast still stained the bottom of a cup. Shells in her pocket had ridged edges. The shore, she realized, was a art box that nobody had to buy.

She spread the sack on a clean board. With the scissors she opened one seam so the paper lay almost flat, then trimmed a square that did not include the greasy corner. The berry juice went on with a stub of sponge, soft purple clouds and a stripe the color of low sun. While it dried she pressed a small scallop shell into the damp places, lifting it to leave a pale fan. Again, and again. The pattern was not perfect. One fan looked like a surprised butterfly. Briony liked it more for that. "You are a sail with opinions," she said. The gull tilted its head, approving the butterfly, or perhaps the purple.

Uncle returned, thumb neat under a plaster, and did not laugh at the paper. "May I see the invention?" he asked. Briony showed him how the square might stitch to the reed with two bits of thread from her hem, not sewn into the wood, only looped so the paper could come off if it tired. Uncle held the mast steady. His big hands were careful around her small knots. "Creativity is a kind of knot," he said. "It ties what you have to what you hope." Briony pulled the last loop snug. The sail stood, purple and fanned, slightly crackly, entirely hers.

They set the toy boat in the water at the ladder, where the cove was clear and no one's swimming spot. For a moment the paper hung shy. Then a breeze, small as a sigh, found the berry clouds. The sail rounded. The boat slid, not fast, not far, a polite journey toward a floating cork that Briony had set as a finish line. She walked along the dock to keep level with it. "Go on," she whispered. "You used to hold a sandwich. Look at you now." The hull made a tiny bow wave. The butterfly shell-print flashed. Briony felt as if she had taught the air a new word.

Near the cork the breeze forgot them. The boat turned in a lazy circle, sail trembling. Briony did not scold it. She thought. A second small flap, cut from the sack's leftover strip, might keep the nose honest. She lifted the boat, drips ticking on the boards, and added the flap at the back with one more thread. The gull supervised from a piling. When the boat went in again, it held its line better and kissed the cork with a soft tock. Briony clapped once, then remembered not to startle the nearby tide pool, where a starfish rested like a quiet audience. She clapped in her mind instead, just as loudly.

Uncle sketched the boat in the margin of a tide paper so they would remember the shape of the flap. Briony added the butterfly from memory. "If the paper softens, I can make another," she said. "Sails can have many chapters." They let the boat have three more trips, each one a little different as the breeze practiced. Briony changed nothing on the last trip. Knowing when to stop improving was part of making, she decided. The boat arrived, proud and damp. She lifted it and poured the water from its belly back into the cove.

On the dune path the sail rustled against her shirt. People might have seen a wrinkled sack. Briony saw wing, signal, and afternoon. She told Uncle the sandwich joke again because it had gotten funnier. At the outdoor tap she dabbed mud from the hull and left the purple alone. Color was supposed to stay. The apple smell still lived faintly in the paper, a secret history under the berry paint. Briony thought of other plain things at home, a box, a string, a smooth stick, and felt rich without adding a single new object to the cottage.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the toy boat as Briony set it on the sill to dry, sail lifted so it would not stick to itself. The purple clouds turned deeper in that honey light, and the shell fans looked like tiny footprints of the shore. She touched the mast once, lightly, proud of the reed and the thread and the lunch sack that had agreed to become wind. Outside, the real wooden boats rested bigger and quieter. Inside, a small one held the whole idea of trying. Creativity, Briony thought, was just paying attention until a new use waved back.
