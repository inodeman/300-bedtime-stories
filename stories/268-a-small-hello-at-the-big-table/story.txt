The big table wore its longest cloth, and every chair had a cousin in it or a cousin almost in it. Una stood beside the doorway with her cup in both hands. The room sounded like spoons and happy questions. She knew these people. She loved these people. Still, her voice felt as small as a pea. The feeling had a name. It was shy. Shy was not naughty. Shy was a soft animal that wanted a minute before it stepped onto the rug.

Grandma Soraya saw her and did not call, “Speak up,” across the room. She patted the chair beside the bread basket. “This seat has a job,” she said quietly. “The bread likes a neighbor.” Una slipped into the chair. The cloth brushed her knees. Cousin Tomas, on her other side, was already buttering a roll. He looked tall and easy. Una looked at her cup. She wanted to say hello and also wanted to become part of the curtain. Both wants tugged. She chose the smaller brave thing. She set her cup down without clinking.

Tomas turned a little, not all the way, so she would not feel stared at. “The soup smells like carrots,” he said. “Do you like carrots?” It was a question with room for a short answer. Una swallowed. Her shy feeling fluttered. “Yes,” she said. The word was tiny and true. Tomas smiled as if she had handed him a good stone. “Me too. Especially soft ones.” Una felt a warm spot start behind her ribs. Courage, she realized, could be one word long. It did not have to be a speech. It did not have to be loud enough for the far end of the table.

The bread basket sat between them like a friendly boat. Una saw that Grandma Soraya’s plate had no bread yet. Her hands tingled. Passing food was something she could do without a long sentence. She lifted the basket with two hands so nothing rolled. “Bread?” she asked, and her voice reached Grandma Soraya. “Yes, please, neighbor of the bread,” Grandma Soraya said. A few cousins chuckled in a kind, including way. Nobody copied Una’s small voice to tease it. The chuckle sounded like welcome. Una’s shoulders dropped from their high place near her ears.

She tried one more brave pebble. When there was a quiet gap, she said, “My balcony mint grew a new leaf.” She had practiced the sentence in her head on the way to the table. It still felt risky in the open air. Tomas leaned in. “What color is a new leaf?” he asked. “Lighter green,” Una said. “Like a sweater just washed.” Tomas nodded, serious and interested. “I want to see it after lunch, if you want to show me.” Una’s shy animal did not run away, but it sat down. She had a plan for after lunch that included a friend and a leaf. The big table felt less like a stage and more like a garden of chairs.

Bowls were passed. Una took soup and said thank you to the hands that offered it. She listened to a story about a pancake that looked like a boat, and she did not force herself to top the story. Listening was allowed. When someone asked who wanted the last roll, Una surprised herself by saying, “Tomas and I can share it.” The words came out joined, and Tomas bumped his cup lightly against hers, a toast with no fuss. The shy feeling watched this and seemed proud of her, the way a small animal is proud when it finds the sunny square.

After lunch, they really did visit the balcony. Una showed the pale leaf. Tomas did not poke it. He just looked. “You were brave at the table,” he said. Una thought about that. “I was shy and brave together,” she said. “The chair by the bread helped. Your carrot question helped.” They watered the mint with a small pour. Back inside, the big table was being cleared by many hands. Una carried two spoons and felt she belonged in the clatter. Her voice was still not the loudest. It did not need to be. It had been heard. Later, when a cousin at the far end asked who knew where the napkins were, Una felt shy flutter again. The table was wide, and the question was public. She put a hand on her tummy, breathed, and lifted a small stack of napkins she had noticed by the sink. She walked them over without rushing. “They were here,” she said, and set them down. The far cousin said, “Thank you, Una,” using her name, which felt like a chair being pulled out just for her. Tomas whispered, “That was another pebble.” Una whispered back, “I still have shy, and I still have feet.” They both smiled into the clatter of clearing. Courage, she decided, could be a walk across a room with napkins and a true sentence.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the bread basket’s empty place. Una touched the chair she had used and gave it a private thank-you pat. Shy could come to lunch. Courage could sit right next to it, passing bread, naming a leaf, and making room for a shared roll.
