Agnes pressed her thumbs into the cool gray lump and felt it give way like bread dough that had not been baked. The kitchen smelled faintly of orange peels in a bowl and the earthy scent of clay. Barak sat beside her, rolling a small snake of clay between his palms until it grew longer than his hand. "Mine keeps getting thinner," he said, watching the snake stretch and shine. Agnes nodded, the tip of her tongue at the corner of her mouth. "Mine is supposed to be a cup, but it looks like a sleepy hill." She pinched the sides upward. They rose, then sighed back down with a soft wet sound.

She tried again, scooping the walls higher. A little crack opened near the rim, thin as a hair. Agnes felt her shoulders climb toward her ears. "It split," she whispered. "I want to squish the whole thing flat." Barak did not laugh. He dipped one fingertip in the bowl of cool water and traced the crack the way someone might close a picture book gently. "My hands get rushy too," he said. "When I rush, the snake snaps. Want to go as slow as the clock?" Agnes let out a breath that tasted like the orange peel on the counter.

They worked with tiny rubs. Barak smoothed one side while Agnes turned the base, the clay turning like a little wheel under their fingers. The wall grew taller than a spoon. It leaned left, and Agnes stopped before the lean could become a flop. She counted three quiet breaths. Then she pressed the thick side with the pad of her thumb until the cup remembered how to stand. "There," she said, almost afraid to bump the table. Barak smiled with clay on his cheek. "It looks like it is listening." Water dripped from her wrist and made a dark star on the cloth. They both watched the star fade.

While the cup rested on a plate, they washed the gray from their hands and watched it swirl toward the drain. The clay on the plate changed from shiny to a softer pale color. Agnes peered into the hollow. "It looks empty." "Empty is what a cup is for," Barak answered. He had rolled his snake into a tiny handle and pressed it on with a dab of water. It stuck, a little crooked, like a friendly ear. Agnes touched it and felt the join. "If we bump it now, it might fall off." "Then we will not bump it," Barak said. "We can wait. Waiting is still making."

When the clay was firm enough for color, Mama set out two small brushes and jars of paint that smelled a bit like rain on a sidewalk. Agnes chose a blue the color of morning sky after a shower. Barak chose a yellow like the inside of a lemon. They did not hurry the bristles. Each stroke whispered against the cup. Blue went around the outside in patient bands. Yellow went around the rim, a sunny ring. A drip tried to run. Agnes caught it with the corner of a soft cloth before it could streak the side. "Good catch," Barak said. "I almost painted my thumb into the picture."

They left the cup by the open window, where a breeze carried the smell of mint from a pot on the sill. Agnes wanted to poke the paint to see if it was dry. Her finger hovered. Barak shook his head, not bossy, just sure. "If we touch it, we will wear our work on our skin instead of on the cup." So they set the table for a snack instead, folding napkins, listening to a bee think about the mint and then change its mind. Waiting felt longer when they stared, and shorter when their hands had a kind job.

By late afternoon the paint was dry and smooth under a careful knuckle. Mama poured a sip of warm milk into the cup to test it. The milk stayed where milk should stay, not a single leak at the bottom. Agnes wrapped both hands around the cup. It was not perfectly round. One side was a breath thicker, and under the blue she could feel the tiny ridge where the crack had been mended. "It still has a quiet story in it," she said. Barak nodded. "A story about slow thumbs." They did not say it was the finest cup in the town. The words would have been too loud for such a small, true thing. Agnes only said, "I like how it holds."

They carried the cup back to the windowsill beside the mint. The leaves smelled sharp and green when Agnes brushed them by accident. Barak set his extra clay snake, now a little painted spoon rest, next to the cup so it would not feel alone. "We can drink from it at breakfast," he said. "One sip for you, one sip for me, if you want to share." Agnes liked that. Sharing the cup felt like sharing the afternoon. Inside, the blue cup held its warm sip and did not wobble.

The lantern by the window glowed softly, laying a honey-colored circle across the blue cup, the yellow rim, and the mint leaves. Agnes and Barak sat with their chins on their hands and listened to the house settle into evening. The cup simply stood, made by patient thumbs and a friendship that knew how to wait. Nobody needed to brag. The milk stayed warm a little longer in the clay, and the glow of the lantern made the mended ridge look like a silver thread. They smiled at their work, proud in a quiet way, and left the cup to shine until morning.
