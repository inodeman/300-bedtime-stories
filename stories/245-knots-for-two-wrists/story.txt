Imani tipped a handful of cotton threads onto the rug and let the colors spill like a quiet rainbow. There was coral, leaf green, gold, and a blue so deep it looked like a pocket of evening. Jules sat cross-legged, already knotting a starter loop around two fingers. The threads smelled faintly of the wooden drawer where they lived, a dry sweet smell. "Friendship bracelets," Imani said. "One for your wrist and one for mine. We make them for each other, so the knots are like little promises we can see." Jules smiled. "Then I will use the blue you like, and you can use the gold I like. The colors can visit."

They measured the threads from fingertip to shoulder, the way Jules's older cousin had shown them, and tied the tops to the leg of a sturdy chair. The chair did not mind. Imani began a forward knot, right thread over the middle, through the loop, snug but not tight enough to bite. The first knots were lumpy. "Mine looks like a caterpillar," Imani said. Jules leaned over. "Mine looks like a caterpillar who learned to dance. We can undo the lumpy part. Undoing is not losing. It is making room." They picked the knots loose with careful nails. The threads went wavy, then smooth again under a thumb.

As they worked, they talked about small true things. Jules liked the sound of rain on the porch roof. Imani liked the way bread smells when the crust is just brown. The bracelet grew, row by row, a stripe of color that warmed under their fingers. Then the green thread tangled with the gold and made a knot that was not the kind they wanted. It sat there like a stubborn berry. Imani felt her patience thin. "I could yank," she said. "Please do not yank," Jules said quickly, soft and not bossy. "Yank turns a tangle into a tighter secret. May I hold the bracelet still while you loosen one loop?"

Imani nodded. Jules cupped the woven part. Imani used a blunt needle from the sewing tin, the safe kind with a round tip, to lift one loop. The tangle sighed open. "There you are," Imani told the gold thread, as if it were a small animal that had been stuck in a sleeve. They laughed, and the laugh loosened their shoulders. The next knots came easier. They checked that the bracelet was not too tight by curving it around a wrist and sliding a finger underneath. "Room to be a person," Jules said. "Not a knot that pinches."

They switched jobs halfway so each bracelet would have both of their hands in it. Imani finished the blue-and-coral one for Jules. Jules finished the gold-and-green one for Imani. The ends became braids, a little uneven, with tiny knots to close them. Imani's braid was thicker on one side. She looked at it a long moment. "I will not pretend it is even," she said. "It is even enough, and it is finished, and it is for you." Jules tied it on her wrist. The threads felt soft and slightly fuzzy against skin. "It ticks when I move," Jules said, shaking the wrist gently so the knots tapped.

Imani wore the gold one. It sat warm from Jules's hands. They held their wrists side by side. The colors did not match, and that was the point. Each bracelet remembered a different favorite. "If a thread comes loose later," Jules said, "we can mend it together. Friends can re-knot." Imani liked that more than any shiny perfect thing. They swept the snipped tails into a cup. One short piece of coral was left. "Too small for a bracelet," Imani said. "Just right for a bookmark string," Jules answered. They tied it around a paper scrap that said, in pencil, "Rain on the roof, bread in the oven, knots on the wrist."

They showed the bracelets to Papa, who was slicing pears in the kitchen. The pears were pale and juicy, and the smell was sweet. Papa did not get a long performance. Imani said, "We made these for each other." Jules said, "We undid the lumpy parts and the tangle." Papa nodded, a smile in his eyes. "That is friendship you can wear to breakfast." He offered pear slices. Juice dotted the table. They ate and turned their wrists so the lantern light, still unlit this early, waited in the glass for evening, and the threads looked bright in the daylight instead.

Neither of them said their bracelets were better than anyone else's. They were simply theirs, made slowly, given freely, and comfortable enough to wear while washing hands, reading, or waving across a yard. Imani traced one of Jules's knots and felt the tiny ridge. "I can tell which knot you redid," she said. "It is the neatest one, because you went slow." Jules shook the blue bracelet. "Then the lumpy ones can be the funny uncles of the row. They still belong."

The lantern by the window glowed softly once evening came, and the two bracelets caught points of gold on coral, green, and blue. Imani and Jules sat on the rug with pear-sweet fingers and quiet smiles. Friendship, they had learned, could be a row of knots that stayed gentle, a tangle patiently opened, and a gift that fit without pinching. The threads rested against their wrists like small, steady hellos.
