Otto liked honey on warm bread, a thick gold ribbon that sank into the crumb. Reina liked orange peel, bright and a little wild, scattered so every bite woke up. They stood in the neighborhood bakery with one ball of dough between them and two ideas pulling at it. Hugo dusted the counter with flour and waited, friendly and floury, beside the bread ovens.

"We could bake two loaves," Otto said. "One honey. One orange. Then we each get our favorite."

Reina nodded, then paused. "We could. There is enough dough for two. There is also enough for one loaf that knows us both." She looked at Otto. "I sit at the far end of the shared table most Saturdays. You sit at the near end. Our breads never touch."

Otto rubbed his chin and got flour on it. "I thought you wanted the far end."

"I thought you wanted the near end," Reina said. They both laughed, surprised and soft. Friendship had been sitting in the room like an extra loaf, and they had not sliced it yet.

Hugo tapped the dough. "One loaf can wear two coats," he said. "Honey on this half. Orange and a little cinnamon on that half. Where they meet, the flavors can shake hands. We will not rush the line."

They tried. Otto warmed the honey jar in a bowl of water until it poured in a ribbon instead of a lump. He brushed his half with slow strokes. Reina pressed thin peels into her half, then dusted cinnamon with a light hand so it would not cross the middle like a cloud. They left a pale stripe between the sides, a meeting place.

"Does the stripe mean we are still separate?" Otto asked.

"It means we can see both gifts," Reina said. "When it bakes, the stripe will still be there, and the smell will mix anyway."

Into the oven the loaf went. They waited on the shared table bench, not at opposite ends this time. Hugo brought cups of water and a story about the soup pots, how two pots could simmer side by side and make the whole bakery smell like one kitchen. The oven hummed. Orange and honey braided in the air before the bread was even done. Otto's foot wanted to tap fast. He matched it to Reina's slower swing. Patience made the waiting feel like company.

The crust came out shining on one side and freckled on the other. They set it on the table and did not cut it at once. They let the steam settle so the crumb would stay tender. Then Hugo showed them how to follow the stripe with careful hands, and they eased the loaf open along its soft middle. Two halves, still warm, still one bake.

Otto tasted the orange side first, because friendship can start with the other person's joy. His eyes widened. "It is sunny," he said. "It makes my honey idea feel bigger, not smaller."

Reina tasted the honey side. "It is round and kind," she said. "I want it beside my peels, not instead of them."

They built a plate that held both. A picnic basket under the table kept extra slices for the market stall, wrapped so the warmth would last. They did not keep the best stripe for themselves. The meeting place went on the center plate, where anyone could taste the handshake.

"Sit in the middle with me next Saturday," Reina said.

"Even if the ends are empty?" Otto asked.

"Especially then," she said. "Ends are fine. Middle is where the stripe is."

Hugo poured a little more honey into a dish and set peels beside it so the table looked like the loaf. They ate until they were content, and the basket still looked generous. Crumbs dotted the cloth like confetti from a tiny parade. Otto saved a peel curl. Reina saved a drop of honey on her spoon and let Otto finish it. The trade felt easy, the way a good recipe feels easy once you have made it with a friend.

Outside, Saturday voices braided with the smell of cinnamon. Inside, the ovens rested and the soup pots kept their quiet heat. The two ends of the bench were still there. They simply were not the only places to sit.

They saved the stripe pieces for last, the pale ribbon where honey and orange had almost met. Otto took a bite and then passed the rest to Reina without measuring who had more. The crumb there tasted round and bright together, a quiet conversation. Hugo poured water and listened to them plan the next Saturday seat, middle bench, lantern side, baskets underneath so the view stayed open. Flour still dusted Otto's chin. Reina brushed it away with a napkin and got a smear of cinnamon on her own wrist, which made them both laugh. The laugh felt like the loaf, two notes in one kitchen. Outside, the market bell rang. Inside, the soup pots kept their even heat. Friendship did not need the ends of the table to disappear. It only needed a place where the flavors could hear each other.

The lantern by the window glowed softly along the pale stripe of the remaining loaf. Otto, Reina, and Hugo leaned over the shared table, shoulders close, flavors mingling in the air. One bake had room for two joys, and friendship tasted like honey learning the language of orange.
