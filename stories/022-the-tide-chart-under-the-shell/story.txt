Ellis set his little wooden boat on the dry sand and tried not to look too hard at the water. The boat was light, with a green stripe and a tiny seat smooth from his hands. The cove shone like a spilled spoon of light. Grandpa had spread the paper tide chart on a flat board and pinned the top corner with a heavy white shell so the breeze would not steal it. Ellis knew the lines and numbers. He also knew what he wanted, which was to launch the boat this very minute.

"The chart says the kind tide is still on its way," Grandpa said, kneeling so his voice stayed low and easy. "If we put the boat in now, she will rub her belly on the sand and go nowhere much." Ellis nodded, but his feet did the small dance they did when waiting felt too big for his shoes. A gull stood on the board's edge, one orange foot carefully beside the paper, not on it. The gull looked at Ellis as if agreeing with Grandpa. "Even the gull says wait," Grandpa laughed.

Ellis traced the chart's blue curve with a careful finger. The curve was a promise, not a scolding. It showed when the water would rise high enough to lift the green-striped boat and carry it in a gentle circle near the shore. That time was not yet. He could hear the sea already, a soft repeating hush, and it sounded ready even if it was not. He picked up the boat, felt how easy it would be to run, and then set it down again. "I do not like the empty minutes," he said honestly.

"Then we will not leave them empty," Grandpa said. "We will fill them with looking." They walked to a tide pool cupped in the rocks, clear as a window. A starfish rested there, all five arms easy, colors soft as evening peach. Ellis kept his hands behind his back. He counted the tiny snails, the waving fringe of a green weed, the bubbles that popped like quiet secrets. The gull perched above them and dropped nothing, only watched the water with them. Ellis's hurry cooled a little, the way a mug cools when you set it in the shade.

Still, when they returned to the chart, the shell had not moved and the water had not climbed enough. Ellis sat on the picnic blanket with his knees up. He retied the boat's small string into a neater bow. He lined shells by size. He told Grandpa a story about a boat that learned to be a patient boat. His voice got faster near the end, because patience was still a wiggle in his ribs. "Is it time?" he asked. Grandpa checked the chart and the wet line on the sand. "Not yet. Soon is not the same as now."

Ellis pressed his lips together, then let them loosen into a breath. He lay back and watched a cloud shaped like a slow spoon. The lighthouse stood bright even in the day, a white friend at the edge of seeing. He listened to the wooden boats already afloat, their ropes creaking like porch chairs. He matched his breath to the waves. In, the water reached. Out, the water went to gather more. "I can be a little like that," he said. "I can go and come back to the waiting without breaking it." Grandpa's eyes crinkled. "That is the whole trick."

When the wet line finally touched the pebble Grandpa had set as a marker, Ellis did not sprint. He looked at the chart once more, then at Grandpa, and Grandpa nodded. Together they eased the green-striped boat into the shallows. For a heartbeat the hull whispered on sand. Ellis almost pushed harder. Then the water took the weight, a smooth living palm, and the boat rose free. It turned its bow toward the open shine inside the cove, exactly as the chart had promised. Ellis's laugh came out surprised and bright.

He held the string and walked beside his boat, ankles cool, heart unhurried. The gull flew a short escort and settled on a post, job done. Grandpa rolled the chart carefully so the pencil marks would not smudge and tucked the white shell into his pocket to use again tomorrow. "Waiting did not shrink the fun," Ellis said. "It kept the boat from getting a scratched tummy." He patted the green stripe, grateful and a little proud. The sea smelled sweet and metallic and alive, and the day felt wider than his hurry had been.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when they climbed the dune path with the boat dripping a trail of bright dots. Ellis looked back once. The place where he had wanted to rush was only a gentle dent in the sand, already smoothing. Grandpa set the chart on the cottage table where the lantern light could find it. Ellis put the shell on top, a small weight that meant try again tomorrow. He dried his feet and smiled at the round glow. Patience had felt slow in his knees, but it had given the boat a perfect floating start, and he could still feel that easy lift in his hands.
