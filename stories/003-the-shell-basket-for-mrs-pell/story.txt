The lane above the beach smelled of salt and warm rope. Nora walked with her empty string bag bumping her knee, on her way to buy a loaf, when she saw Mrs. Pell stopped beside a tuft of sea grass. A wide basket of shells rested against Mrs. Pell's boot. Cream spirals, peach fans, and smooth gray stones caught the light. The handle had slipped once already. A few small shells dotted the sand like spilled buttons. Mrs. Pell pressed a hand to her lower back and smiled anyway. "They were lighter when I picked them up," she said. "That is the trick of baskets. They learn new weight on the hill."

Nora set her string bag on a dry rock. "I can take one side," she said. "My loaf can wait a little." Mrs. Pell's eyes brightened, the same blue as her front door up the lane. "If you are sure, dear. I want these on the sill before my grandson's train. He is coming to stay, and a window should say welcome before a person does." Together they lifted the basket. It was heavy in a sandy way, not sharp, and it rustled softly as the shells settled. Nora matched her steps to Mrs. Pell's shorter ones. The gulls called overhead, bright and busy, and the sea kept its slow hush at the bottom of the path.

Halfway up, the basket tipped a finger's width. Three fan shells slid toward the rim. Nora stopped at once. "Let's rest on this wall," she said. They set the basket between them on the flat stones. Nora hopped down and gathered the spilled shells, brushing grit from each one with her thumb. They were cool, then warm where the sun had touched them. She did not sigh. She did not hurry. She placed them back in a neat layer so nothing stuck out to fall again. "You have a good eye," Mrs. Pell said. "That little gray one was hiding under the grass, and you found it." Nora felt the praise like a sip of warm milk.

At the blue door, the step was wide enough for sorting. Mrs. Pell brought out a shallow tray and a cloth. "We will make a picture," she said. "Not a crowded pile. A picture that lets each shell have a little air." Nora's hands itched to put the pink scallop in the center, because it was her favorite, round as a sunrise. She held it out instead. "You should choose first. It is your window, and your grandson." Mrs. Pell turned the scallop so its ridges caught the light. "Then this one is the heart of it," she said. "Thank you for not keeping the best choice in your own pocket." Nora laughed. "It would have made a lumpy pocket anyway."

They sorted by color. Creams with creams. Soft oranges in a curve. Grays like a path of rain. Nora noticed a shell with a chip along one edge. She almost hid it under the others. Then she paused. The chip made a tiny heart-shaped notch if you looked kindly. "This one has a mark," she said. "It can still belong." Mrs. Pell nodded. "Welcome includes the marked ones." They set it near the front, where a child might see it and know that perfect was not the rule. The cloth under the shells smelled freshly washed, like lavender soap. A breeze moved the curtain inside the open window and brought out the scent of lemon cake cooling on a rack.

Mrs. Pell's kettle sang. She poured two small cups of mint tea and one extra, covered, for later. Nora carried the tray of shells to the sill, moving an inch at a time so the picture would not slide. The window glass was cool against her wrist. Outside, the town roofs stepped down toward the water, and a white boat left a quiet line on the bay. "When he sees this," Mrs. Pell said, "he will know I thought of him while he was still on the train." Nora placed the pink scallop last, in the middle, just where Mrs. Pell pointed. Their shoulders touched, friendly and brief. The tea tasted green and sweet. Sugar crystals ticked in the cup.

Nora still had her loaf to buy, so she could not stay for cake. She did stay long enough to sweep the step with a hand brush, gathering sand into a little pile and tipping it back into the lane where sand belonged. Mrs. Pell wrapped two lemon slices of cake in paper. "For you and for whoever walks you home," she said. Nora tucked the packet into her string bag. It warmed the cloth. "If the basket needs to go back to the beach tomorrow, I can come," Nora said. "Empty baskets are good company too." Mrs. Pell squeezed her hand, a light squeeze, no more. "You already made the hill shorter."

On the way to the bakery, Nora passed the place where the shells had spilled. The sand was smooth again, with only the marks of their two sets of shoes. She bought a round loaf, crackling at the crust, and the baker slipped a sprig of rosemary on the paper because the day smelled like it. Nora told him, in one sentence, about the window. He nodded as if welcome were a kind of bread. The sea glittered at the end of every side street. Nora's arms were tired in a proud, useful way, the way arms feel after carrying something that mattered.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in Mrs. Pell's blue house when Nora looked back from her own gate that evening. The shells on the sill made a small pale constellation above the glow. Nora set the rosemary loaf on her table and ate a corner of lemon cake, tart and kind. She had not needed to be the fastest walker on the lane. She had only needed to take one side of a basket, to gather what fell, and to give the first choice away. The town sounded like water and distant wheels, and Nora felt as settled as a shell placed exactly where it could shine.
