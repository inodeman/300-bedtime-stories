The little school smelled of paper, orange peels, and the green soap by the sink. Sun lay across the tables in wide stripes. Mr. Adeyemi had pinned a long paper mural along the wall, a town the class was making together: houses, a bakery, a strip of field, and a round sun that still had no color. The crayon tin sat in the middle of Juniper's table. She was the tin keeper that week, which meant lids closed, broken bits saved in a jar, and no color left on the floor if she could help it. She liked the job. The crayons made a soft wooden sound when they rolled, and her fingers smelled faintly of wax.

Nico leaned over the mural with his tongue tucked at the corner of his mouth, thinking hard. "The sun has to be the real yellow," he said. "Not the pale one. The pale one looks like butter that forgot itself." Juniper nodded. She had planned to color the wheat field with that same real yellow, the one labeled SUN on a worn paper band. She lifted the tin. Blues were plenty. Greens were plenty. The real yellow was one crayon, still long, but only one. Two hands reached, stopped, and hovered. Juniper felt the small problem arrive like a pebble in a shoe. It was not a fight. It was a moment that needed a good idea.

"We could snap it," Nico said, then shook his head at once. "No. Snapping makes sad short crayons, and you asked us not to." Juniper was glad he remembered. She rolled the yellow under her finger. It was smooth and a little warm. "What if we share turns?" she said. "You color a ray. I color a stalk of wheat. Then you. Then me. The crayon stays one crayon, and the picture still gets yellow." Nico considered this with a serious face that soon unfolded into a grin. "Rays and stalks. Like a game." Mr. Adeyemi, passing with a stack of paper, heard them. "That is fair planning," he said. "I will not choose for you. You are already choosing well."

They carried the crayon to the mural together, Juniper holding the blunt end, Nico the point, so neither walk was a grab. Up close, the paper smelled new. Juniper said, "You go first. A sun without a start is only a circle." Nico colored one thick ray, tongue of color stretching out. The yellow was bright as a dandelion. He stopped where they had agreed, at the count of ten slow breaths. Juniper counted in a whisper. Then she colored one stalk in the field, a golden line with a little heavier top, like grain nodding. Wax built up in a soft shine. "Your turn," she said, and passed the crayon without wiping it on her sleeve first, so no color was wasted.

Other children worked nearby. Someone colored a blue door. Someone made a cat that was mostly whiskers. The room hummed with quiet effort. Juniper noticed Nico's second ray wobbling. "The paper dips there," she said. "If you put a finger on the edge, it stays still." She showed him, pressing only the margin, not his drawing. The next ray came out smoother. Nico said, "Now the wheat." He did not take her turn. He waited. Juniper liked the waiting. It made the yellow feel shared instead of borrowed. She added three stalks and a tiny seed head. Her wrist did not get tired, because the turns were short. The sun began to look warm enough to stand under.

When the crayon wore a flat side, Juniper peeled the paper band back a little so the point could be used along its edge for thin lines. "This way we get more," she said. Nico drew a thin ring around the sun, a halo of almost-touched paper. Juniper used the edge to make pale grass between the stalks, lighter because the color was thinner there. They had discovered two yellows inside one crayon. Nico laughed. "It had a secret." Juniper laughed too. "Plenty left," she said, reading the feeling if not the label. They signed nothing on the mural. Mr. Adeyemi had said the town belonged to the whole class. Still, Juniper felt their SUN and wheat were a kind of signature made of patience and wax.

At tidy-up time the yellow crayon went back into the tin, shorter by a honest amount, still long enough for another day. Juniper checked the floor. One crumb of wax sat near her shoe. She pressed it onto a scrap paper and put the scrap in the save jar. Nico brought the lid. "Tomorrow we could share the orange if the bakery in the picture wants a roof," he said. "Or we could let Lina use it. She keeps looking at the fruit stall." Juniper nodded. "We can ask Lina first." Asking first felt like the same idea as turns, only wider. Mr. Adeyemi clapped once, soft as a book closing. "Coats on. The town will dry on the wall and greet you tomorrow."

Juniper walked home with yellow still faint on her finger. She did not scrub it off right away. It was a reminder that one bright thing could stretch. Her street smelled like bread from a nearby kitchen and like the dust that sunshine seems to have. She told her mother about rays and stalks. Her mother said, "That is how porches get built too. One board, then the other person's board." Juniper liked thinking of the mural as a porch of color, big enough for more than one painter.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the classroom that evening, though Juniper only imagined it while she looked at her own kitchen lamp, and the sentence of the day still felt true in her mind as she pictured the mural waiting in the quiet school. She washed her finger at last, watched the yellow thin away, and did not mind. The color was on the paper where it belonged, sun and wheat together. Sharing a turn had not made her picture smaller. It had made the town on the wall feel finished by more than one heart, which was a larger kind of bright.
