Habiba stood on a stool beside Grandma Lotta while a pot of rice rested, fluffy and white. The recipe lived in Grandma Lotta’s memory, not on a card. “Warm rice, a spoon of honey, a small snow of cinnamon, and apple pieces if the bowl is willing,” Grandma Lotta said, tapping her temple. Habiba loved this kitchen kind of story. She loved the way steam fogged the window over the sink. She wanted to help so much that her elbows stuck out like little wings.

Grandma Lotta turned to fetch apples from the bowl. The cinnamon jar sat open. Habiba sniffed it. The smell was sweet and bold. She thought of winter mugs and cozy socks. “A small snow might not be enough,” she told herself. “Grandma’s memory might have forgotten the best part.” She shook the jar. Cinnamon fell in a brown hill, much more than a snow. A cough of spice rose up. Habiba’s eyes watered. Quick as a blink, she pushed the jar behind the flour tin. Her heart tapped fast. The feeling was nervous, mixed with a wish to hide.

“Ready for apples,” Grandma Lotta said, returning with two red ones. She stirred honey into the rice. The spoon left shiny trails. Then she tasted a grain and blinked. “Oh. That cinnamon walked in with boots on.” She did not sound angry. She sounded puzzled. Habiba stared at the honey spoon. The nervous feeling grew taller. She could stay quiet. The jar was hidden. Maybe they would think the jar had tipped itself. Habiba knew that was not true. Her stomach felt tight, the way a fist feels before it remembers how to open.

She slid off the stool and brought the jar out from behind the flour. Her voice came smaller than she wanted, but it came. “I added a big snow,” she said. “I thought your memory skipped the good part. Then I hid the jar because I felt scared you would be disappointed. I am sorry. I should have asked.” Grandma Lotta set the spoon down and knelt so their eyes met. “Thank you for telling me the truth,” she said. “Honest words let us fix a pot. Hidden words leave us guessing.” Habiba’s tight stomach loosened one knot.

Together they looked at the rice. “Too much cinnamon can still become friendly,” Grandma Lotta said. “We add more rice from the little leftover pot. We add milk, a splash. We add apple, chopped small, so the sweet fruit holds hands with the spice.” Habiba poured milk while Grandma Lotta stirred. The brown hill melted into a softer tan. Habiba chopped apple with a safe table knife, slow and proud. She tasted when she was invited. The rice was warm, gently spicy, and sweet at the edges. It was different from the usual bowl, and it was good.

Habiba named the rest of her feelings while they scooped servings into blue bowls. “I felt sneaky, and I did not like that feeling. I felt brave when I picked up the jar. I feel relieved now.” Grandma Lotta nodded. “Those are clear names. Next time your idea knocks, you can let it in through the door. You can say, may I try a little more? Then we decide together.” Habiba practiced the sentence aloud. It sounded strong and polite. She liked the way it left room for a no or a yes.

They carried the bowls to the table and ate while the window cleared. Habiba told the whole story again, not to stay in trouble, but to remember the shape of honesty. Grandma Lotta told a memory of once stirring too much salt into soup and laughing when Grandpa brought extra potatoes. “Kitchens are full of second tries,” she said. Habiba leaned against her arm. The honey rice tasted like a problem that had been welcomed instead of buried. She put the cinnamon jar back in the open, where hands could see it. After the bowls were empty, Habiba asked for a pencil and a card. “Memory is wonderful,” she said, “and a card can be memory’s friend.” Grandma Lotta nodded. Together they wrote the recipe in simple lines. Rice. A spoon of honey. A small snow of cinnamon, and ask before more. Apple pieces. A splash of milk if the spice walks in with boots. Habiba drew a little jar with a smile so the card looked friendly. She did not hide this paper. She set it in the recipe box where anyone could find it. Writing the truth down felt like putting the jar back in the light a second time. Her nervous feeling had somewhere safe to rest. She read the card aloud once, slowly, and Grandma Lotta clapped a quiet kitchen clap. “That is a recipe and a promise,” she said. Habiba put the pencil away in the cup where it belonged.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the two blue bowls. Habiba watched the light rest on the cinnamon jar and felt light herself. Telling the truth had not shrunk her. It had given the recipe a way forward, and given her a sentence she could use the next time an idea knocked.
