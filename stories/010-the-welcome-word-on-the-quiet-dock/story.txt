Morning on the dock smelled like wet wood and orange tea from Captain Idris's mug. The ferry Merry Minnow rubbed softly against the fenders, a sound like a big cat leaning on a door. Mateo coiled a light rope the way Uncle Sol had shown him last week, loops even, no knots hiding. His job was usually the rope and the chalkboard of times. Today a new job waited. Captain Idris tapped the rail. "Our greeter is at a wedding inland," he said. "The first run is only neighbors. I would like you to say the welcome. One sentence. Clear as a bell that knows it is a bell." Mateo felt his tongue become a visitor in his mouth. He nodded anyway. "I can try it until it fits," he said.

They practiced by the ticket box, where the wind was smaller. "Welcome aboard the Merry Minnow," Captain Idris said, easy, like offering bread. Mateo copied the shape of it. The first try came out thin and fast, all one squeak. He stopped and smiled at himself. "That was a mouse version." "Then give me the person version," said the captain, not unkind. Mateo set his feet. He looked at a knot in the wood, then at the captain's oatmeal-colored cap, because a face was better than a knot. "Welcome aboard the Merry Minnow." The words landed. A gull on a post turned its head as if considering a ticket. Mateo tried again, a little warmer. "Welcome aboard the Merry Minnow." "That one could open a door," Captain Idris said.

Passengers were not there yet. Mateo used the spare minutes well. He wiped the rail where hands would hold. He checked that the little step stool was steady for anyone who liked a boost. He said the sentence to the rope, then to the tea steam, then once with his eyes on the far pilot house window, imagining a person there. His heart beat in a busy way, like feet on a gangplank, and he let it beat. Courage, this morning, was not a climb. It was a sentence that he would not swallow. Uncle Sol arrived with a crate of letters and heard the last practice. "Good bones in that voice," he said. "Send it out to the person, not to the floorboards."

Mrs. Gale was first down the lane, a basket of library books on her arm, her scarf the color of raspberries. Mateo stepped to the painted mark where the greeter stood. His hands wanted a rope. He gave them the job of resting open at his sides. Mrs. Gale reached the gangplank. Mateo looked at her eyes, which were friendly and a bit curious. Out came the sentence, whole and unhurried. "Welcome aboard the Merry Minnow." Mrs. Gale's face opened like a window. "What a proper hello," she said. "I feel expected." She touched the rail and stepped on, books safe. Mateo's ears hummed with gladness. The sentence had gone where he sent it. It had not fallen on the boards.

Next came the twins from the chandlery, each with a small drum that they promised not to bang until the far shore. Mateo welcomed them too, the same words, newly meant. "Welcome aboard the Merry Minnow." One twin whispered, "He sounds like a captain's front door." Mateo kept his smile inside his manners and made sure their drumsticks pointed down. A fisher with a mended sleeve came third. Mateo's voice did not shrink for the taller person. He had learned it in the practice: the sentence was a gift of the same size for everyone. "Glad to be expected," the fisher said, echoing Mrs. Gale without having heard her, because welcome teaches its own reply.

When the little crowd was aboard, Captain Idris gave Mateo a nod that was better than a trumpet. "You may ride this run," he said, "and welcome them off as well, if you like. Hellos have cousins named glad-you-came." Mateo stood near the cabin, feeling the deck's gentle push. The harbor slid. Colors changed on the water. He did not need to paint them. He only needed to keep his feet and his voice. At the far wharf he said, "Glad you came on the Merry Minnow," and Mrs. Gale patted the air in thanks, both hands full of books. The words were not fancy. They were placed right, like coils of rope.

On the ride back, Uncle Sol let Mateo hold the whistle cord without pulling it. "A voice did the work today," Uncle Sol said. "Whistles can rest." Mateo watched the dock grow nearer, the ticket box, the post, the gull that had not bought a ticket and did not need one. He thought of his first squeaky try and did not feel embarrassed. It had been a step, the way a gangplank is a step. The last loop of the morning was quiet water and the smell of tea cooling in a mug someone had saved for him. He drank it. It was strong and sweet.

He helped set the fenders out again, each bump-bump against the dock a period at the end of a good line. His name was not painted on the ferry. It did not need to be. Mrs. Gale would remember a raspberry scarf morning when a boy looked at her and made the boat feel ready. Mateo coiled the light rope one more time, loops even, humming the welcome under his breath only to keep it polished, not because it was hiding.

Between arrivals Mateo refilled the captain's mug and his own smaller cup from the thermos. The steam smelled like orange peel. He said the welcome once more, not because it was lost, but because a useful sentence stays bright when it is handled kindly. A sparrow hopped along the rail and left when the gangplank creaked, no fuss, just a bird with plans. Mateo straightened the chalkboard number so it could be read from the lane. His hands had found calm jobs, and his voice had found its job too. When the next footstep sounded on the boards, he was already facing the lane.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the ticket shed when he hung the chalkboard inside for the day. Mateo read the sentence once more in his mind and let it rest. He had used his voice to make a person feel expected, and the harbor had carried that kindness out and back like a letter that did not even need a stamp.
