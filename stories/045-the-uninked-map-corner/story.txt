June spread her paper map across the mossy table inside the hollow tree library and smoothed the curled corners with both palms. Most of the oak wood was drawn in careful lines. She could see the rope footbridge, the picnic clearing, the mushroom cottages, and the fern hollows, even a dot for the lantern window of her own home. One corner, though, was only pale paper. No path. No tiny oak. June's eyes kept returning to that blank. "Someone stopped the ink right here," she said. A gentle fox who sorted leaf-pages lifted its head, as if the empty corner were a question it also liked.

"I want to know what the empty part is keeping," June said. She rolled the map, tucked it under her arm, and stepped into the bright day. She did not rush. Curiosity felt like a string of warm lights in her chest, not a shove. She followed the drawn path until the real woods matched the last inked oak, a big tree with a pale scar of bark like a smile. Beyond it, a slim opening in the ferns showed a way the map had left alone. June could still see the main path behind her. "I am visiting a blank," she told the fox, who had come along. "I can step back whenever I like."

The uninked way was short and soft under her shoes. Moss sank kindly. Ferns tapped her shoulders like quiet greetings. The air smelled greener, as if the leaves had just breathed out. June walked twenty slow steps and found a round pocket of sun, a little room of light. Acorns sat in their caps on the moss like tiny hats set out to dry. A fallen oak branch made a low bench. Fireflies resting from last night dotted the underside of a fern like seeds of light. June turned in a circle. "The blank was saving a surprise," she said, "not hiding a problem."

She sat on the branch bench and opened the map again. The fox hopped up beside her, careful to keep its paws off the paper. June held a berry-ink twig she had carried from the library. "May I draw what is really here?" she asked. The fox touched the blank corner with its nose, and June decided that was a yes. She drew a small curve for the fern pocket, a bench shape, and three tiny stars for the daytime fireflies. Her lines were a little wiggly. She did not mind. A map could be true and still look handmade. "If I had not looked, this corner would have stayed empty forever," she said.

They explored with their eyes more than their feet, so the pocket would stay peaceful. June noticed a strip of darker moss where dew collected, a good sip-place for small paws. She noticed oak leaves fallen in a spiral, as if the breeze liked patterns. She noticed the fox noticing her, and she laughed. "You are curious about curious people," she said. The fox's mouth opened in a friendly pant. June added a tiny paw print to the map to show that gentle friends were welcome. She did not draw a fence. The pocket did not need to be closed. It only needed to be known.

A breeze turned a corner of the map, and June caught it before the wet ink could smear. She blew softly until the berry lines dried to a dull shine. While she waited, she wondered aloud whether the first mapmaker had left the blank on purpose, as an invitation. Maybe they had simply run out of daylight. Both ideas felt kind. "Maps should leave a little room," she said. "Then someone else gets a turn to discover." The fox leaned into her arm. Its fur smelled like clean leaves and warm dust. June lettered the words fern pocket, then read them aloud so they felt solid.

They walked back the same short way, and the main path was right where they had left it, sunny and ordinary and easy to trust. June felt a fizz of pride, the good kind that does not need a prize. At the hollow tree library she set the map on the wide table and placed a smooth stone on the corner so it would stay flat. "Anyone may look," she said. "If you add something, add it kindly, and do not cover lines that are already true." The fox set an acorn cap beside the stone, a small helper weight. June smiled so hard her cheeks ached in a happy way.

Before leaving, she copied the new corner onto a spare scrap for her cottage wall. Copying helped her remember the bench, the spiral leaves, and the firefly dots. She thanked the library fox with a nod and a berry from her pocket. On the walk home she passed the rope footbridge and checked her map against the real ropes, pleased when they matched. Curiosity had taken her only twenty steps off the known line, yet those steps had given the wood a newly true place. She swung her arms, light and glad, and the ferns brushed her as if they agreed with every step.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over June's scrap map, which she fixed beside the round glass with a dab of sweet sap from an oak twig. Outside, warm dusk settled in the branches, and a few fireflies woke for real, brighter than the daytime seeds of light. June thought of the blank corner and how it had been an invitation instead of a mistake. She sipped berry tea from an acorn-cap cup. Asking what an empty space might become, she decided, was one of the finest walks a person and a fox could share.
