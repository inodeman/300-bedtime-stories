Rafi noticed the slick strip after Lorca's rain had done its good work and moved on. The yard was fresh, the herbs glittered, and the path the chickens liked, the one from the porch to their sunny dust bath, had become a smooth smear of mud. A hen tried one careful step, lifted her foot, and chose the long way through the grass instead. She was not bothered. She was simply wise. Rafi still wanted the short way to feel kind again.

"A pebble path," Rafi said. "Not a wall. Not a deep ditch. Just small smooth stones so their feet can find a dry idea." Maude, who had already used her brave voice once that week, used it for a plan. "Light loads only. If a stone needs two hands and a grunt, it is the wrong stone." Hawa agreed and offered the wheelbarrow, then looked at it and shook her head. "A barrow might get heavy before we notice. Cloth bundles are honester." Kira brought four small cloths. The team had rules before it had pebbles.

They gathered stones from the place behind the pump where round pebbles already lived in a loose pile, not from the pond edge where water creatures might be using them. Each friend chose stones no bigger than a potato and no smaller than a walnut. They carried three or four at a time. Button the dog wanted to help with his mouth. Gideon, passing by, reminded him of the greeting stone. Button sat and supervised with one wag, which was a real job because it kept the work calm.

Rafi laid the first line, pressing each pebble into the soft earth so it would not roll. Maude checked the gaps. Hawa hummed the chicken song slowly, not to call the hens into the work, but to keep the pace gentle. Kira brought a cup of water for people, not to make more mud. When Rafi's bundle started to feel like more than a bundle, Maude said, "Second trip, not bigger muscles." They laughed and split the stones again. Teamwork was a set of small refusals as much as a set of yeses: no to heavy, no to hurry, yes to coming back.

The path grew from the porch toward the dust bath like a dotted sentence. A chicken watched from the rosemary, interested and unhurried. Rafi did not shoo her onto the unfinished part. "She can test it when it is a real path," he said. They left a little room at the edges so grass could still grow and so the path would not pretend to be the only legal road in the yard. Animals could walk beside it if they preferred. The pebbles were an invitation.

When the last stone sat firm, they stepped back. The first hen came, set one foot on a pebble, then another, and walked the whole short way to her dust. She sank into the dry patch with a happy flutter. Two more hens followed. One went through the grass anyway, and Rafi did not take it personally. Moss was not asked to haul anything. The donkey's light-picnic fame could stay in the lane. This was a people-sized team with chicken-sized results.

They washed their hands and shook the cloths. Rafi's palms were dusty in a proud way. Maude said, "Nobody's bundle was a jar of peaches in disguise." Rafi grinned, remembering the morning he had needed a reminder, and thanked her again without making a long speech.

Rafi walked the pebble path in his socks of imagination, heel to toe, checking for a wiggle. One stone rocked. Maude pressed it in with the heel of her hand, no grunt required. Hawa sprinkled a little dry soil at the edges so the line would feel seated in the yard rather than dropped on top of it. Kira poured the last of the people's water onto the herb bed, not onto the path. Button remained a calm supervisor until the work was truly done, then received a slow ear rub for excellence in waiting.

The hens took one more stroll across the stones before their evening roost, unhurried, almost proud. Rafi did not call it his path when he told the grown-up. He called it ours. The word felt as sturdy as the pebbles. Many light trips had become one dependable kindness, and anyone could see it without reading a sign. Tomorrow if rain returned, the stones would still be there, small and ready, a team you could stand on.

Rafi washed the cloths and hung them where they could dry without dripping on the new stones. He thanked each helper by name, Maude, Hawa, and Kira, and he thanked Button for the sitting. Naming people, he decided, is part of teamwork too. It keeps the path from looking like it appeared by magic, and it reminds every builder that their light bundle mattered.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as the dust bath sent up its little cinnamon cloud and the pebble path held its shape. Rafi looked at the line of stones and saw four friends in it, plus a dog who had helped by sitting still. Teamwork, he thought, is not one hero with a heavy barrow. It is many light trips, a shared rule, and a path that friends may use when they are ready.
