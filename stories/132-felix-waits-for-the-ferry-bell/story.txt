The ferry boards were warm from the afternoon, and the ropes smelled like rain that had dried in the sun. Felix stepped on with Papa and felt the boat give a small polite shift, like a big animal settling its feet. Gulls argued softly over a crumb and then made peace. A brass bell hung by the cabin door, shiny as a spoon. The deck guide, a woman in a blue cap, rested her hand near it. "When the bell rings, we walk, we do not run," she told the line of families. "The front rail is wide enough if we arrive like neighbors."

Felix could see the front already. The view there would show the whole harbor mouth, a bright opening between two low hills. His toes wanted to go now, while people were still finding benches. A gap in the crowd looked exactly his size. He leaned. Papa's hand stayed loose around his, not a trap, just a reminder. "The bell is the start," Papa said. "If we jump the start, someone else's picnic gets bumped." Felix pictured a basket tipping. He did not want to be a bump. He wanted to be a boy at the rail.

He planted his shoes and made a game of waiting. He counted rivets on the bench, round little moons in a row. He listened to the water pat the hull, a rhythm slower than his hurry. The guide checked a rope and nodded to herself. A baby two benches away laughed at a gull, and the gull pretended not to notice. Felix breathed in orange peel from someone's snack and salt from the air. "I am waiting," he told Papa, so the waiting would be a choice and not only a rule. Papa squeezed once. "I see you waiting. That is strong work."

The engine hummed a note that Felix felt in his knees. Still no bell. He almost stood. Instead he put both hands on his knees and pressed the hurry down into his feet, where it could fidget without traveling. He watched a flag on the mast lift and fall, practicing patience of its own. The guide caught his eye and gave him a small salute, as if waiting were a job she respected. Felix saluted back with two fingers. The game grew easier. The harbor smells lined up: rope, orange, salt, and a hint of warm paint.

Then the bell rang, one clear note that did not startle. It simply opened the walk. People rose. Felix rose too, and he did not sprint. He walked beside Papa, letting a grandparent with a cane set the pace for a few steps before the deck widened. "After you," Felix said, and the words felt as shiny as the bell. At the front rail there was room. The harbor mouth shone. A green lamp on a post looked like a candy no one needed to eat. Felix wrapped his fingers around the warm rail and felt the boat's yes under his shoes.

"You got the view," Papa said, "and nobody's basket had to dodge you." Felix grinned into the wind. The wind took the grin and cooled his cheeks. He watched a cormorant stand on a post as if the post were a throne. The ferry's wake wrote a white sentence that faded. Felix did not ask to go elsewhere yet. The front was the prize, and patience had carried him there without a tangle of apologies. He thought of the rivets and the salute and felt a quiet pride, the kind that does not need to hop.

They shared a pear, cut with Papa's pocket knife in careful slices over a napkin. Juice dotted the napkin like rain. Felix offered the first slice to Papa and took the second. The bell stayed quiet now that its job was done. The guide walked the deck and thanked the line with her eyes. When she passed Felix she said, "Good wheels on those feet." He looked down and laughed. "They wanted to be rabbits. I asked them to be ferry feet." She nodded as if that were a sensible repair.

On the way back to the bench he still did not run, even though the exciting part was over. The boards had gaps where a hurrying shoe might find a surprise. Walking let him see a carved initial, very old, and a patch of paint like a cloud. He showed Papa. They made up a kind story for the initial, a traveler who liked birds. The ride home to the dock would be the same boat, the same bell, the same chance to practice. Felix decided he would wait again without making it a struggle.

At home he told Mama about the brass note and the green lamp and the rabbit feet that had agreed to walk. She hung his cap on a peg and welcomed the story of the day without hurrying him through it. The problem had been a gap in the crowd and a body that wanted to dart. The bell, and his choice to honor it, had turned the gap into a path. He set his shoes side by side, ferry feet at rest.

Felix traced a little map on the fog of his breath against the cabin glass, then wiped it clear so the next passenger could see. He noticed a coil of spare rope as neat as a snail and a bucket of fresh water for rinsing hands. The ferry kept its tools in plain sight, which made the whole deck feel cared for. He told Papa that waiting had let him see the tools, not only the view. Papa said people who rush mostly meet their own elbows. Felix laughed and kept his elbows tucked, a ferry guest with room to spare.

The lantern by the window glowed softly, round and steady as the bell's after-sound in his memory. Felix watched the light and heard water patting a hull that was now far off and calm. He was glad he had waited. The harbor mouth, he thought, would still be there whenever the bell said go. His eyes closed on a green lamp and a pear slice and a walk that had been wide enough for everyone.
