Cleo liked the polishing cloth more than she expected. It was soft, the color of oatmeal, and it made the cottage window sing a tiny squeak when she moved it in circles. Morning light poured through. Outside, dunes rolled down toward the wooden boats, and the friendly lighthouse wore the sun like a clean shirt. The keeper had trusted her with the inside of the glass, including the small lantern on the sill. "Circles, and light pressure," he had said, then stepped out to shake a rug. Cleo dipped the cloth in the vinegar water and began.

She was happy. Happiness made her bold. She climbed onto the sturdy bench to reach the top corner, one knee planted, one foot down, the way Auntie had taught her so chairs would not become adventures. The cloth whispered. The glass cleared. Cleo leaned in to admire a far tide pool, a speck of orange that might be a starfish, and her free thumb, dusty from the bench, met the lantern's side. It left a print. A perfect sandy oval, right where the evening light would have to look through it. Cleo's stomach dropped the way a skipped shell drops after its last hop.

She glanced at the door. The rug was still being shaken, a rhythm of thumps. She could turn the lantern so the print faced the curtain. She could smear it into a vague cloud and call it old sea fog. The cloth was in her hand, an easy helper for a fib. Cleo touched the print and felt grit. It was hers. A turned lantern would make the keeper hunt for a flaw he did not understand. Her ears warmed. She stayed on the bench, cloth lowered, and practiced the true sentence once, quietly. "My thumb visited the glass without an invitation."

The keeper came in with the rug over his arm and saw her face before he saw the lantern. "That is a thinking face," he said. "I smudged it," Cleo answered, and pointed, and did not add any decorations to the story. "Sandy thumb. I was going to hide the print against the curtain. I did not. I want to clean it properly, and I wanted you to know whose sand it was." The words were hot and then cool, like tea going from sip to comfort. The keeper set the rug down. He looked at the oval. He looked at her. "Thank you for not making me solve a mystery," he said. "Mysteries are fun in bottles, not on lanterns we need."

They cleaned it together. He showed her a dry corner of the cloth for the last pass so the vinegar would not leave a halo. Cleo worked in small circles and lifted her other hand wide, a seagull wing of caution. The print thinned, ghosted, and vanished. The glass gave back the dunes, sharp enough to see a gull walking the path with a bit of string in its beak, carrying it away from the boats like a helpful neighbor. "There," Cleo said. "The light can go through without eating sand." She hopped off the bench the safe way, foot then knee then both feet.

The keeper did not lecture her into a puddle. He told a short true thing instead. "I once wiped a drip off the chart and took a pencil number with it. I pretended the tide had changed. Then I had to un-pretend, which took longer." Cleo smiled, small and grateful. Honesty had company. It was not a lonely island for children who slipped. She hung the cloth so it could dry without souring. Her thumb still glittered. She washed it at the tap until the sand went back toward the sea where it belonged. "Clean thumbs for clear glass," she said. It sounded like a song they might remember.

She finished the lower panes, slower, proud of the squeak and of the way the room brightened. Auntie arrived with a basket of pears and noticed at once. "The window looks like it had a good morning," she said. Cleo glanced at the keeper, who let her tell it. "It had a thumb, and then it had the truth, and then it had circles," Cleo said. Auntie laughed and passed her a pear. The juice was cool. They stood in the light and watched a little wooden boat adjust its rope, a tiny far movement through glass that did not lie. Cleo felt as clear as the pane. Hiding would have made the whole room feel foggy, even after the sand was gone.

Before lunch she wrote a tiny note on the chore list, not as punishment, as a helper. Dry cloth last. Thumbs wide. The keeper pinned it beside the chart. "That note will save future noses too," he said. Cleo ate her pear and watched the lantern's clean side throw a small bright line on the sill. She had arrived wanting to be a perfect polisher. She was leaving as an honest one, which seemed sturdier. The shore outside did not need her to be flawless. It needed the light to pass, and people to say what their hands had done.

The lantern by the window glowed softly even in the daytime, a faint warm heart in clear glass, as Cleo folded the oatmeal cloth into a square. She could see the dunes through the spot where her thumb had been, and nothing in that view accused her. The print was gone because she had named it. She set the cloth in its basket and let her shoulders drop. Honesty had been a short sentence with a long shine. Cleo carried her plate to the table, hungry and light, while the window kept its promise to show the bright, true shore.
