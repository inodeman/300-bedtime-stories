Rowan mashed three dark berries in a bowl and watched the purple juice crawl up the sides in slow fingers. The berries smelled sweet and wild, like a sunny hedge after a warm day. Sana held a white cloth square and a wooden spoon. Grandpa had said the mashing and the waiting were their work, and the warm-water rinse, if needed, would be his. No stove this time. The color would come from fruit and patience. "If we stir once and yank the cloth out," Rowan said, "we will get a pale maybe. If we wait, we might get a real purple." Sana nodded, though her toes wiggled with the wish to be done already.

They pushed the cloth into the cool juice. It floated, then sank as they spooned berry pulp gently over it. Their fingers stained at the tips, purple moons under the nails. "We look like we painted with evening," Sana said. They set the bowl on the counter, covered it with a plate, and started to walk away. Sana lasted twelve steps. She came back and lifted the plate. The cloth was still mostly white, with a shy lavender touch. "It is not working," she said, and her voice wanted to become a complaint. Rowan put the plate back. "It is working too quietly to see yet. Let us do another job while it thinks."

They swept the floor. They lined up books. They counted the spoons in the drawer, which was a silly job and a useful one, because waiting needs a place to put its hands. The kitchen clock spoke in small ticks. Sana peeked again. "Still shy," she admitted. "I want to tell Grandpa it is already bright, so we can be finished. That would be a fake finish." She looked at Rowan. "I will not say that. It is still pale. That is the truth." Rowan bumped her shoulder, friendly. "Honesty makes the waiting cleaner. We will not pretend the color."

They went outside and picked five more berries from the low bush Grandpa had shown them, only the ripe ones that fell into the hand with a touch. Leaves tickled. A bird scolded far off and then lost interest. Back inside they mashed the new berries and added the juice, cold and vivid. The cloth, lifted for a moment, had deepened to a gentle lilac. "Better," Sana said. "Not done." They submerged it again. This time Sana did not lift the plate for a long while. She drew a picture of the bowl instead, a circle and a square of cloth, and shaded the cloth in light pencil. "I will darken the drawing when the real cloth darkens," she said. "The picture has to wait too."

When they finally rinsed the cloth in cool water, purple left the rinse in clouds and still stayed in the cotton. It was not the deep night-purple of the bowl. It was a clear daytime purple, even and pretty, with one darker freckle where a berry bit had rested. Sana touched the freckle. "I could say it is a fancy dot I planned." She smiled, small and true. "I did not plan it. A berry sat there. I like it anyway." Rowan nodded. "Then we tell it that way." They hung the cloth on the line. Drips tapped the step. The smell of berries stayed sweet and a little tangy.

Grandpa came to see. "Is it finished?" he asked, not as a trap, just as a question. Sana stood tall in a quiet way. "The color is finished enough to use. The freckle is an accident I am not hiding. The pale stage was real. We waited past it." Grandpa's eyes crinkled. "That is a good report. A cloth and a truth." He did not ask them to be faster next time. He asked them to wash the bowl, which they did, purple swirling toward pale pink and then clear. Their hands took longer, and a faint stain remained in the lines of their palms, a map of the afternoon.

They folded the dry cloth into a cover for the basket that held napkins. The freckle landed on a corner, like a signature the berries had written. Rowan smoothed it. "Patience is a little boring in the middle and very beautiful at the end," he said. Sana laughed. "And honesty is how we do not cheat the middle." They ate one last berry each, bright and seedy, and made faces at the tartness, then smiled. The bush outside was lighter by eight berries and still full of green. They had taken only what they needed.

Neither of them announced a talent for color. They had mashed, waited, peeked, told the truth, waited more, and finished. The basket cover would see bread and napkins and ordinary days. The purple would fade someday, and that was all right. Fading was a kind of time too. For now it glowed, soft and certain.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the basket, and the berry purple answered with a deeper shade in the folds. Rowan and Sana rested their stained fingertips on the table and felt content. The cloth was done. The waiting had been kept honest. Nothing extra had been pretended, and nothing true had been wasted.
