Salma knelt beside the science table until her eyes were level with the jars. One sprout stood taller, its stem a pale ladder toward the sun. The jar beside it held a shorter sprout with a seed coat still stuck on like a speckled hat. The glass smelled faintly of wet paper and soil crumbs. Kwame sat on a stool with a picture book about seeds balanced on his knees. The sunny classroom hummed with quiet choosing time. Paint cups clicked as someone lined them up far away. Salma did not touch the tall stem. Curiosity, she had decided, could look first and poke almost never.

"Why is this one in a hurry?" she asked. "I gave them the same number of spoonfuls yesterday. I counted." Kwame opened the book to a page of sprouts in a row, some high, some just waking. "Same drink does not always make the same day," he said. "The book says light matters, and so does the way the seed was facing." Salma tilted her head. The tall jar sat closer to the window. Its shadow on the table was short and dark. The shorter jar sat behind a stack of picture books that made a little shade. "Oh," she said. "I made a tiny hill of books, and the hill stole a piece of sun." She felt the click of understanding, a pleasant pop like a lid set down softly.

They tested the idea without disturbing the roots. Salma moved the book stack to the other side of the table, where it would shade nothing but the bare wood. Then she and Kwame turned both jars so each stem could see the window. The glass was cool. They used two hands. A drop of water on the outside smeared under Salma's finger, and she left the inside droplets alone. "If I am right, the shorter one will lean a little by tomorrow," she said. "If I am wrong, we will have a new question, and that is still a good harvest." Kwame liked that sentence so much he repeated it. "A new question is a good harvest."

Salma wanted to know about the speckled hat too. She did not pull it off. Pulling might tear the leaf that was folded underneath. She brought the jar to the window light and looked until she saw a split in the coat, a seam where the plant was doing its own unzipping. The picture book showed the same seam in three drawings. "It is not stuck because it failed," she said. "It is stuck because it is still on the page before the next page." Kwame traced the drawing, then stopped tracing so he would not smudge it. "We can read the next page and let the real seed catch up when it wants." They read about seed coats feeding the baby plant and then falling away like a suitcase set down after a trip. Salma smiled at the suitcase idea. She could see it.

They made a curious log on a card, not a scoreboard. Salma wrote the date as a small sun with a number beside it, and two circles for the two jars. She marked the tall one "near the window before" and the short one "behind books before." Kwame added, "Books moved. Jars turned. Hats left on." The pencil smelled like cedar. Salma read the card aloud to be sure it told the truth. Curiosity that fibbed would not be curiosity at all. It would be a story that could not help anyone. She taped the card to the table with a quiet rip of tape, the sound sharp and small.

At the library later that day they looked for one more fact, because one answer had opened a door to another. The nature shelf had a book with a flap that lifted to show roots. Salma lifted it slowly so the flap would not crease hard. Underneath, the roots were longer than the leaves. "The busy part is hidden," she told Kwame. "That is why patience and curiosity hold hands." They did not borrow the jars out of the school. They borrowed the book, checking the pocket card, and carried it back carefully against Salma's sweater. The sweater was warm. The book was cool. The walk between library and school smelled like sun on the chalk path.

Salma set the library book beside the jars, open to the roots, and stood with her hands behind her back so she would remember not to peel the speckled hat. Kwame checked the spoons of water with a marked stick they had made, the same mark for each jar. The water lines matched. "Tomorrow we look again," he said. "We do not drag the answer out by the stem." Salma laughed a little. "I will bring my eyes and leave my tugging fingers in my pockets." She put her hands in her pockets right then, to practice. The pockets were full of nothing but lint and a crayon cap, soft and ordinary.

She looked once more at the tall sprout and the short one and felt fond of both. The tall one was not a winner. The short one was not late in a way that needed sorry. They were two living answers to light and time. Salma whispered thank you to the window, which was a little silly and also exactly right. The glass did not answer in words. It answered in brightness.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the two jars and the curious log. Salma watched the speckled hat shine, still on, still useful, and she left it there, happy to wait for the plant to take its own next step.
