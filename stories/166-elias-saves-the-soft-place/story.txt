Elias arrived at the music corner of the festival with a folded blanket and a small drum that was not his. The drum belonged to Mr. Ade, who had taught Elias a simple palm pattern: soft, soft, softer. The corner was a ring of benches near the bakery, where players took turns making quiet music while people ate. Lanterns stood on the ground in jars, glowing through glass without flickering wildly. Elias looked for Mr. Ade and saw him standing, not sitting, with the drum case against his leg and a polite smile that was growing tired around the eyes.

The benches looked full of neighbors and coats and a basket of bread. Elias did not climb over anyone. He walked to the end of the nearest bench, where his own blanket had been saving a wide space. "Mr. Ade," he said, "this end is for you. I put the blanket down so the wood would not feel cold, and so the space would stay a space." Mr. Ade's shoulders eased. "I was going to listen from standing height," he said. "Sitting will let the drum sit too." They settled. The blanket smelled like soap and a little like the cedar chest. Elias took the outside edge, which was narrower, and found that narrower was still enough.

Mr. Ade warmed his hands around a cup of tea that the baker's sister brought. She nodded at Elias as if she had seen the saved seat and approved. "There is buttered bread when you want it," she said. "No rush." The tea smelled like lemon peel. Mr. Ade took a sip and then showed Elias the pattern again, tapping it on his own knee first: soft, soft, softer. "The festival does not need a big announcement," he said. "It needs a pulse that helps people chew slowly." Elias tried the pattern on his knee. His hands were smaller, so the softer beat was the one he liked best.

When it was their turn, they did not move to a stage. They stayed on the bench. Mr. Ade played the drum with his fingertips, and Elias kept the same pattern on a wooden box, very lightly, so the box would sound like a polite cousin of the drum. A few people turned their faces, not their whole bodies, which felt right. The tune from a nearby fiddle wrapped around the pattern without hurrying it. Elias watched a crumb travel on the breeze and land on the blanket. He left it until the song ended, then brushed it away. He did not want a scratch in the music from a sudden flick.

A woman with a fiddle case hovered near the bench, looking for a place to set the case that was not the ground. Elias slid his knees to the side. "The blanket can hold a case for a song," he said. "It is clean." She set it down as if it were a sleeping cat and thanked him with a nod. The case smelled faintly of resin, sharp and sweet. Elias kept his hands off the clasps. He only guarded the space with his calm sitting. When she lifted the case again, the blanket showed a warm rectangle, and Elias smoothed it flat for Mr. Ade's drum.

After the song, Elias noticed Mr. Ade's cup was empty. The water jug was on a table behind a cluster of chairs. Elias stood, waited for a gap, and filled the cup halfway so it would not spill on the walk back. "Lemon water," he said, offering it with the handle turned out. "Thank you," Mr. Ade said. "A seat and a sip. You are looking after the music by looking after the player." Elias felt a warm pride that did not need to be loud. He took a piece of buttered bread and ate it in small bites. The butter was sweet. The crust whispered when he chewed.

Between songs the baker's sister brought a basket of torn bread to the benches so no one had to stand if they were tired. Elias passed the basket to Mr. Ade first, then to the listener on his other side, and took his piece last. The crust was flaky and the crumb was soft. He chewed with his mouth closed while the fiddle tried a new quiet line. "You pass things the way you play," Mr. Ade said. "Soft, soft, then a little left for the next hand." Elias stored that sentence beside the drum pattern, sure he would want it later.

They played one more pattern while the lanterns were moved from the ground to the hooks along the bakery wall. The light rose, and faces looked gentler. Elias kept the softer beat even when his hands wanted to show off a faster one. Mr. Ade gave him a look that said yes, stay with the small weather. When they finished, a listener placed two apple slices on a napkin and slid them along the bench. "For the pulse," she said. Elias and Mr. Ade each took one. The apple was crisp and cold. Juice dotted the napkin like tiny glass.

When the lanterns rose to the hooks, Elias stood on the ground and did not climb. He pointed to an empty hook the baker's sister could reach and held the jar steady while she lifted. The glass was warm, not hot, and the light made his fingers glow at the edges. "Good noticing," she said. "Hooks are easier when someone spots the empty one." Elias felt useful in a way that did not need applause. He returned to the blanket and found Mr. Ade had kept his place with a palm on the wood, the same kindness turned around.

Elias walked Mr. Ade to the corner where his daughter was waiting with a shawl. "He saved me a soft place," Mr. Ade told her. Elias folded the blanket, shaking it once so the crumb went back to the path and not into the cloth. His arms remembered the wooden box and the quieter beat. At home he told his mother about the halfway cup and the apple. She listened while the kettle clicked as it cooled. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and Elias folded the blanket one more time, hoping the next tired player would find a space already kept warm.
