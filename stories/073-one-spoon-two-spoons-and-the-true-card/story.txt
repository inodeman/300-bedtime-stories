Nkechi's cinnamon buns had come out of the bread oven smelling like a gentle hug. She had used one small spoon of cinnamon, and the orange peel on top made the flavor bright. While the buns cooled, she wrote a recipe card so Tomas could bake them too. Her pencil hurried. In the cinnamon line she wrote two spoons instead of one. She did not notice. The card looked neat. The mistake looked like the truth.

Tomas read the card at the shared table, where honey jars and a bowl of peels waited. Hana, the kind grocer, had brought extra cinnamon from her stall so the bakery would not run low. The tins were full. The morning was full. Tomas liked following cards exactly. He measured two spoons. The dough turned a deeper brown. It smelled louder. He baked the buns with care, and they rose beautifully.

When Nkechi tasted one, her eyes opened wide. The bun was still friendly. It was also very cinnamon, like standing inside a spice jar. Tomas watched her face. "Did I miss a step?" he asked. His voice was calm. He was not afraid of a small puzzle. He was curious and honest already.

Nkechi looked at the card. She looked at her memory. Her stomach did a tiny flip, the kind that happens before a true sentence. It would be easy to say the oven was extra warm, or the cinnamon was a bold batch. Those would be foggy words. The clear word was waiting.

"I wrote two spoons," she said. "I used one. I hurried the pencil. The card told you something I did not do. I am sorry. The buns are good, and the card was not true yet."

Tomas nodded slowly. "Thank you for saying it. I thought I might have stirred wrong."

Hana tasted a corner and smiled. "This one is a drum. Yours, Nkechi, is a hum. Both are music. The card should tell the baker which song it is."

They did not throw the louder buns away. Food was a gift in that kitchen, and these buns were tasty if you knew what you were meeting. They set the gentle batch on one plate and the bold batch on another. A little sign, written in Hana's clear letters, said one spoon hum and two spoon drum. People could choose. Plenty sat on both plates.

Nkechi erased the hurried number. She wrote one small spoon, then added a second line. If you want a bigger cinnamon song, use two spoons and say so. She showed Tomas the change. "Honesty is part of the recipe," she said. "A hidden mistake makes the next baker feel lost."

Tomas copied the card in his own neat writing so there would be two true cards, one for the bakery and one for the market stall. They worked without rushing. Patience kept the pencil from inventing new numbers. Orange peel still went on top, because that step had been true all along. Honey waited for anyone who wanted a soft gold finish.

They carried both plates to the shared table. Picnic baskets underneath held extra rolls, so nobody had to worry about the last bun. Neighbors from the Saturday stalls drifted in, drawn by the two smells. Some chose the hum. Some chose the drum. A few brave tongues tried both and laughed at the surprise they had been told about. A surprise with a true sign is a delight. A surprise with a wrong card is a muddle.

Nkechi felt lighter after the truth, as if she had set down a heavy spoon. "I wanted my card to look perfect," she said.

"True is more useful than perfect," Hana said. "Perfect that fibs is only tidy."

Tomas took a hum bun and a small piece of drum bun and ate the hum last so the quiet flavor could finish the bite. "I am glad you told me," he said. "Now I can bake your hug on purpose."

They propped the corrected card where the light could read it. Cinnamon dust sparkled on the wood. The ovens rested. The soup pots kept a mild simmer for the lunch that would come later, abundant and unhurried.

Nkechi tapped a small blank corner of the card where she would draw the one-spoon bun after lunch, so the lesson could be seen again in the daylight. She had already leveled the cinnamon with a careful finger while Tomas watched, and she had shown the spoon to Hana before it touched the dough. Tomas traced the flat line on his copy, not a heaping hill. Hana nodded. "Honest pictures help honest numbers," she said. The two plates on the table still offered a choice, hum or drum, and the honey jar sat between them like a kind referee. Nobody had to pretend the card had been right the first time. The correction was visible, and the visible truth felt as warm as the crust.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the two plates and the honest card. Nkechi, Tomas, and Hana sat close enough to pass the honey without stretching. One spoon and two spoons had both found a place, and the truth between them tasted warm.
