Henrik shook his basket of borrowed library leaves and heard a sound that was not a leaf. A soft slide, then a brighter whisper. He looked inside and found a green ribbon bookmark coiled at the bottom, the kind used to keep a favorite leaf-page from getting lost in a stack. It was not his. His own marker was a plain grass loop still tied to his paper map. The ribbon must have slipped in when he packed up at the hollow tree, maybe from the table where a gentle fox had been reading about ferns. Henrik held it up in the sunlight. It was pretty. It would look fine on his map. That thought sat down in his mind and tried to get comfortable.

He was halfway along the oak path, closer to his mushroom cottage than to the library, and the walk back would take time he had planned to use for a quiet snack. He could return the ribbon tomorrow. Tomorrow was a kind word people used when they hoped a small wrong would shrink. Henrik stopped under a wide tree and sat on the moss with the ribbon across his knee. A firefly slept on a nearby fern, uninterested in bookmarks. "If I wait, the reader will look for this and not find it," he said. "The pretty part does not make it mine. The finding part means I know where it belongs."

He turned around. The decision made his steps lighter, which surprised him, because the basket was the same weight. Honesty had a way of taking stones out of pockets he had not noticed filling. He crossed the rope footbridge without hurrying, one hand on the soft guide rope, the ribbon tucked where it could not slip out again. Ferns brushed his ankles. He practiced his sentence. "I think this traveled home by mistake, and I am bringing it back before dusk." Saying it to the oaks first made it easier to say to a friend. He smiled at his own seriousness. A ribbon was small. Telling the truth about it still mattered.

At the library the reading fox was patting a stack of leaf-pages with a worried paw, lifting corners and peering between them. Henrik's chest pinched in a useful way. He walked straight over and held out the green ribbon. "This was in my basket," he said. "I did not see it go in. I noticed on the path, and I almost kept it because it is beautiful and because turning around was a bother. Then I came back. I am sorry for the worry." The fox went still. Then its ears lifted, and it took the ribbon as gently as if it were a fresh leaf.

Henrik watched the fox's face change from tight to soft. The fox laid the ribbon in the exact page it had been using, a drawing of ferns uncurling, and the place looked finished again. Henrik let out a breath. "I am glad it still knows its page," he said. The fox touched his wrist with a cool nose, a thank-you, and also a forgiveness that did not need to be heavy. No one scolded. The truth had arrived quickly enough to mend the afternoon. Henrik sat on the reading bench so his knees could stop being the bravest part of him.

They looked at the fern page together. The fox showed him how the ribbon's end could lie outside the stack so a paw could find it later. Henrik nodded. "A bookmark only works if it can be found, and if it stays with the right reader," he said. He told the fox about the moment under the oak when keeping it had tried to sound reasonable. The fox listened without turning away. Being honest included the almost, not only the pretty ending. Henrik felt cleaner for saying the almost out loud. He shared two berries from his pocket, one slightly squashed, and gave the squashed one to himself.

Before he left, he checked his basket with extra care, lifting every leaf. Nothing else hid at the bottom. He tied his grass loop tighter to his map so his own marker would not go visiting by accident. The fox walked him to the door and sat in the patch of sun as if to see him off with no hard feelings left over. Henrik waved. The wave felt easy. On the bridge he touched the guide rope and thought about how both rope and ribbon were thin things that kept larger things from slipping. He liked that picture. It made honesty feel woven into the wood, not like a test he might fail.

The picnic clearing offered a stump where he finally ate his snack, an acorn biscuit and the rest of the berries. A different gentle fox napped nearby and did not need anything from him, which was its own peace. Henrik unfolded the paper map and did not clip the green ribbon to it, because the ribbon was home now. His grass loop was enough. He lettered a tiny reminder near the library mark: turn around while the day is still kind. Then he blew on the ink until it dried. The reminder was for him, and it did not make him feel ashamed. It made him feel equipped.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in his mushroom cottage as he set the empty basket by the door, ready for the next honest errand. He could still picture the fern page with its green ribbon shining like a path through the stack. Henrik washed his hands and watched the warm dusk gather in the oaks. Honesty, he thought, was a pretty thing put back in the right paws, a sentence practiced under a tree, and the lighter steps that came from turning around before tomorrow had to carry the weight.
