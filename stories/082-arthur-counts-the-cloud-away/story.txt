Arthur knelt on the rug in his bedtime slippers and lifted the cardboard tube to his eye. The tube was their homemade telescope, light as a poster roll, with a paper ring keeping one end perfectly round. He wanted the moon at once. A thin cloud had parked itself in front of that bright face, pale as spilled milk. Arthur puffed his cheeks. "Move, please," he told the cloud, but the cloud did not listen. Crickets in the garden sang on, patient as little clocks.

Grandma set a cup of hot milk on the low table and tucked the quilt around Arthur's knees so the waiting would be cozy. "The sky is not a cupboard door," she said kindly. "It opens when it is ready. Would you like a job while you wait?" Arthur nodded. Waiting with nothing to do made his toes wiggle. Grandma pointed toward the balcony, where the night air smelled like damp leaves. "Count ten cricket songs. Then look again. If the cloud is still visiting, count ten more."

Arthur listened. One song was a bright creak-creak from the mint pot. He held up a finger. Two. Three. The cloud's edge grew thinner, like a curtain someone was slowly pulling. He did not peek early, even though he wanted to. Four, five, six. He sipped his milk between numbers so his mouth would have a turn at being patient too. The milk was hot at the first sip and gentler at the second. Seven, eight. A paper star taped to the window glass fluttered when the balcony door breathed. Nine. Ten.

He raised the cardboard tube. The moon was still partly covered, but a silver rim showed, curved and clear. "A little more," Grandma said. "The best pictures often arrive after the last wiggle." Arthur smiled at that. He started another ten, this time tapping his knee instead of bouncing. On the shelf, glow-in-the-dark stickers waited on a sheet, but he left them there. Tonight the job was looking, not decorating. When he reached ten, the cloud had torn into soft scraps. The moon sat in the opening like a clean white plate.

"There," Arthur whispered. Through the tube the moon looked near enough to greet. He could see faint gray patches, gentle as smudges of flour. Grandma explained in a calm voice that those marks were wide plains and that the moon was round even when clouds hid part of it. "Like a biscuit behind a napkin," Arthur said. "Yes," Grandma said, "a very far biscuit that does not mind being watched." They laughed softly so they would not startle the quiet.

Arthur wanted to find a constellation next, and he wanted it immediately. He swung the tube across the sky and saw only a blur of sparkles. "Slow," Grandma reminded him. "Let the tube rest on the balcony rail. I will name them, and you can visit one at a time." They stepped onto the safe balcony together, slippers soft on the cool boards. Grandma showed him the Big Dipper, seven stars set like a spoon. Arthur found the bowl first, then the handle. He said the parts out loud so his hurry would have words to follow.

Back inside, Arthur drew the spoon shape on a scrap of paper and laid it under the window lamp. His lines were a bit wiggly, and that was fine. He did not crumple the page. He added a small cloud at the corner to remember what he had waited through. Grandma warmed the last of the milk and poured a little more into his cup. "You gave the sky time," she said. "That is how wonder stays friendly." Arthur pressed a glow sticker onto the corner of his drawing, not to rush the night, but to mark the place where patience had worked.

He set the cardboard tube on the quilt where it could not roll away. The crickets were still singing, and now their song sounded like praise instead of a delay. Arthur yawned a true yawn, the kind that comes when a body is ready and not when it is bored. He had seen the moon's flour smudges and the sky spoon. He had also felt the good stretch of waiting, which was a kind of work his hands could not do alone.

Arthur carried his drawing to the balcony door and held it up beside the real spoon of stars. The handle sat lower in the sky than his pencil had guessed. He did not crumple the page. A first map could be friendly and still need a second look. Grandma hummed a made-up spoon song, only four notes, and Arthur hummed it back. "Patience lets your eyes visit twice," she said. He noticed then that one star in the bowl shone a little brighter, so he made that dot darker and clearer on the paper. The cricket near the mint sang without being counted, and Arthur let the extra music be a gift. He set the cardboard tube by the window lamp so the paper ring would stay dry and round.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside Arthur's paper spoon and the empty cup. The cloud was gone, and the moon had the whole balcony to shine on. Inside, the cardboard telescope rested as patiently as the boy who had learned to count cricket songs. Arthur slid under the quilt, proud of the slow looking, and the night felt wide and kind.
