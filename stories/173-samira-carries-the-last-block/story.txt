Samira had walked beside the family lantern all evening, close enough to feel its warmth and not yet holding it. Papa carried the handle. The town lantern walk was a calm river of paper lights, peach and cream and blue, moving from the square toward the lanes. A flute kept a slow tune. People shared sesame crackers from a basket at the corner, and the crackers were thin, salty, and crisp. Samira took one and brushed the seeds from her palm so they would not stick to anyone's sleeve.

At the square's edge, Auntie Noor paused to retie her scarf. "The last block is the short one," she said. "Three gardens, one puddle that is only a shine, and then our gate." Samira looked at Papa's hand on the handle. The handle was smooth wood. She wanted to know its weight. The wanting was not a storm. It was a clear small wish. "May I carry it from here?" she asked. "You can walk on my other side." Papa did not tease. "Yes," he said. "We will go at your pace. If your arm grows tired, we trade, and trading is not a failure."

He showed her the grip: fingers over, thumb along, lantern a little away from her coat so the paper would not brush. The candle cup inside was steady. The light rose into a shade painted with a simple leaf. Samira lifted. The lantern was lighter than the soup pot and heavier than a cup. It tugged gently when she stepped. "Oh," she said. "It has a rhythm." Papa smiled. "Then you and the rhythm can agree." Auntie Noor took the outside of the path, a calm border, not a fence.

The first garden smelled like damp mint. Samira walked straight. The leaf on the shade seemed to float. A neighbor waved, and she nodded instead of waving back, because her hand had a job. "Your light looks settled," the neighbor said. The sentence warmed her more than the candle did. She passed the second garden, where a rose had closed for the night. She did not reach for it. The lantern asked for level hands. She gave it that. Her shoulder spoke once, a small news of effort, and then settled too.

The shine that Auntie Noor had called a puddle was only a flat mirror on one stone. Samira stepped around it so her shoe would not slip and so the lantern would not lurch. "Good choosing," Papa said. He did not grab the handle. His hands stayed near, empty and ready, which helped her more than taking over. She heard her own breath and the flute, now farther behind. The tune still matched a walking pace. Samira matched it. Courage, she thought, was not a shout. It was a steady handle and a person who believed you could learn it in one short block.

At the third garden their gate came into view, green paint soft in the lantern glow. Samira felt a flicker of hurry, the wish to arrive triumphantly. She let the flicker pass and kept the same steps. "Almost," Auntie Noor said, "and almost is still a time for care." Samira smiled. The handle had grown familiar. Her fingers knew the smooth place where Papa's hand had warmed the wood earlier. She carried that warmth the rest of the way. At the gate she stopped fully before lowering anything.

"Would you like to hand it to me, or set it on the step yourself?" Papa asked. Samira liked being asked. "I will set it on the step," she said. She bent her knees, kept the shade upright, and placed the lantern where the stone was flat. The light did not wobble into a panic of shadows. It simply sat, leaf glowing, candle calm. Auntie Noor clapped once, very softly, almost a pat of air. "A new job, neatly done," she said. Samira's cheeks were warm. She shook her arm out and laughed a little at the pleasant tiredness.

They shared the last sesame crackers on the step while the lantern finished its outdoor time. Salt sparkled faintly on Samira's lip. She told Papa the rhythm she had felt, how the light tugged and then agreed. He said every carrier learned that conversation. Auntie Noor pinched the candle out with a proper snuffer, a tiny sigh of smoke that smelled like warm wax. The leaf shade went dim, but the evening did not feel smaller.

Halfway along the block, a crack in the pavement tried to catch her toe. Samira saw it because she was looking, and she shortened one step. The lantern stayed level. "I saw you choose," Papa said. "Choosing is the heart of carrying." Samira's heart felt steady, like the candle. She passed a window where someone was setting out cups, and the cups shone back her leaf-light for a moment. She did not stop to admire herself in the glass. Admiration could wait on the step. The job was still in her hand, warm wood and a small living flame.

Auntie Noor began a very quiet hum of the flute's tune so Samira would have company in her ear. The hum did not pull her faster. It laid a soft rail. Samira followed it past the rose garden and felt the handle's rhythm match the notes. "I can hear the walk," she said. "Then the walk can trust you," Auntie Noor answered. A beetle crossed near the gate and Samira stepped aside without a lurch. Even a small courtesy fit inside a brave new task. She was still carrying the lantern when the green gate grew large and close.

Inside, Samira washed cracker salt from her hands and watched the window. She had carried light past three gardens, and the gardens were still there, mint and rose and gate. Papa set a house lamp on the sill. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and Samira rested her newly wise hand on the table, glad she had asked for the last block and kept the flame level all the way home.
