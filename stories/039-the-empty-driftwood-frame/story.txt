Rufus set the driftwood frame in the middle of the table and saw that it was empty. The wood was silver-gray, salty, and smoothed by real weather. The keeper had found it above the tide line and said, "If anyone can give this a middle, it is you." The middle was a blank of pale board. Around it, in jars, waited sea glass in greens and ambers, and empty shells Rufus had checked twice so he would not borrow anyone's house. "Empty is an invitation," he said.

He tried placing glass at random. It looked like a pocket turned inside out. He sat back and looked out the window instead, which was the real picture he loved, the friendly lighthouse, the curve of cove, the little wooden boats, the dark commas of weed, the pale sand. "I will not copy every stone," he told the frame. "I will suggest the cove so the wall can remember it at night." Suggesting felt like a kinder job than copying. He sketched a light curve in pencil on the board, a bay like a smile, and a small rectangle for the lighthouse that did not need to be tall to be true.

The sea glass lighthouse became four stacked greens, the darkest at the bottom. Rufus stuck them with the tiny dabs of paste Uncle allowed on crafts, not enough to ooze. An amber piece made a door. He left the glass lamp area as a gap so the real lantern light, later, could sit in that gap and seem to shine from the picture itself. "A trick that is also true," he said, pleased. Shells became boats if he chose the long narrow ones and aimed them the same direction, as if a gentle wind had organized the fleet. One shell was chipped. He used it anyway. Chips were honest. The cove had chips too.

A problem arrived in the sky of the picture. The top felt bare, and bare sky might be all right, but Rufus wanted one gull, only one, helpful and not crowded. He did not have a gull-shaped shell. He had a white chip and a smaller gray chip. Together, overlapping, they made a wing-and-body if you knew the secret. He glued, held his breath, and released. The gull seemed to fly toward the lighthouse, which was the right errand for a gull in this cottage. Rufus laughed. "You were two scraps. Now you have a job." Creativity, he thought, was introducing scraps to each other until they recognized a new name.

He worked until his shoulders hummed, then stopped to walk outside and check the real colors. The water was more silver than blue. He came back and added three flat silver shells as a path of light. He did not add a starfish made of orange glass, even though he had a piece that would do it, because he wanted the living starfish to remain only in the pools, not collected even as an idea stuck down. Instead he pressed a ring of tiny plain shells in one corner, a pool seen from above, and left the center open. Anyone who knew could imagine the animal safe inside. Rufus liked leaving that kindness in the design.

Uncle brought pear slices and did not touch the frame with juicy fingers. "May I look?" he asked. Rufus nodded, suddenly shy, which was silly and normal. Uncle was quiet for a long breath. "I can tell which way the wind is going," he said. "And I can tell you walked down to look again. The silver is right for today." Rufus felt the shy feeling turn into warmth. He fixed one boat that pointed the wrong way, a small change, the way a story gets better with one true sentence. Then he stepped away before he could add too many dots. Stopping was part of making. The empty spaces let the filled ones breathe.

They carried the frame to the wall together, Rufus holding the bottom, Uncle the wire. It hung beside the window, not blocking the view, keeping it company. From the table the glass lighthouse caught a little daylight. The chipped boat still belonged. Rufus cleaned paste from his thumb and set the leftover pieces back in jars, sorted, ready for another day. Leftovers were not failures. They were the next invitation. He told Uncle about the gull made of two scraps. The cottage felt full in a light way, like a pocket that held exactly the right shells.

Rufus stood outside and then inside, comparing. The real cove moved. The picture stayed. Both were good. He did not want the picture to replace the walk. He wanted it to remind tired evenings that the walk existed. A gull cried far off, bright and unharsh, and the scrap gull seemed almost to answer. Rufus grinned. "Stay a picture," he told it. "The live ones have the sky." He washed his hands, ate a pear slice, and let the paste finish drying without being poked. Patience at the end was the frame around creativity. He had learned that from the shore, which never rushed a shell into smoothness.

The lantern by the window glowed softly and found the gap Rufus had left in the glass lighthouse, so the little picture suddenly had a working lamp of its own. He stood with Uncle and watched the honey light gather there, then spill toward the shell boats. The driftwood edge looked like a piece of the beach that had agreed to come inside. Rufus touched the wall beside the frame, not the glass, proud and quiet. An empty middle had worried him for a moment. Now it held a cove made of looking, choosing, and leaving the living starfish in the real water. Creativity glowed in that small gap, steady as the lantern, ready for night to arrive.
