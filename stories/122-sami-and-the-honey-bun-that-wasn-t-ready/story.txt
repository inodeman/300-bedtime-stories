The night market woke up as the sky turned the color of peach jam. Paper lanterns bobbed on strings, and their light made the lane look buttered. Sami held Papa's hand and listened to spoons, bells, and the soft slap of dough on a board. Cinnamon walked through the air and sat on his sleeve. "We will buy one thing and then walk the long way home," Papa said. Sami already knew which thing. At the end of the lane, the bun stall glowed like a small oven with a roof.

The baker, a woman with flour on her cheek, slid a tray toward the heat. The buns were pale and round, each one shining with a thin coat of honey that had not yet bubbled. Sami stood on his toes. "Are those ready?" he asked. The baker shook her head kindly. "They need a slow while. Honey likes time, or it stays shy on the top." Sami looked at the counter. A basket held yesterday's plain rolls, cool and perfectly fine. His stomach made a small hopeful sound. He could choose a roll now. The honey buns only smelled like a promise.

Papa crouched so they were the same height. "We can take a roll," he said, "or we can wait and watch the market while the honey learns to shine." Sami pressed his lips together. Waiting felt long when a smell was that close. He touched the edge of the counter, which was warm like a sunlit step. "I will wait," he said. The words came out smaller than he wanted, but they were steady. The baker smiled. "Good. Waiting is a job too. You may hand out the little napkins if you like. That makes the time useful."

Sami stood at the side of the stall where he would not be underfoot. He folded napkins into neat squares. The paper was soft and smelled faintly of oranges. People passed with cups of tea and bags of greens. A man hummed without words. Somewhere a pan hissed, then settled. Sami counted lanterns to keep his patience company. One, two, three, all the way to twelve, and then he started again because a breeze turned them and he lost his place. Papa bought two small pears and set them in the string bag. "Still waiting?" Papa asked. "Still waiting," Sami said, and he meant it.

The baker opened the oven door. Heat breathed out, sweet and gold. The buns had risen, but the honey was only starting to freckle. "Not yet," she said. "If I pull them now, the middle will be a sigh instead of a song." Sami nodded, though his toes tapped once. He made them stop. He watched a child across the lane choose ribbons with great seriousness, holding each color up to the lantern light. He listened to a ladle knock a pot three gentle times. The market did not rush him. It kept offering small things to notice, like a pocket full of quiet treasures.

At last the baker drew the tray out and set it on a rack. The honey had turned a deep amber and caught every lantern. Steam rose in curls. "Now," she said. "You waited for the real ones." She tucked a bun into a paper sleeve and then, seeing Sami's careful napkin squares, added a second bun. "One for the waiter, and one to share on the walk. Patience made room for both." Sami held the sleeve with both hands. The warmth traveled into his palms. He did not bite yet. He wanted the first taste to happen when they were walking, the way he had pictured.

They moved slowly down the lane. The bun smelled like flowers and toast. Sami broke it so the honey stretched in one shining thread, then tore clean. He gave Papa the larger half without measuring too hard. The first bite was soft, then sticky, then warm all the way to his ears. "It was worth the slow while," he said, a little sticky at the corner of his smile. Papa nodded. "The roll would have been kind. The bun is what you hoped, because you let it finish." Sami licked a thumb and laughed at the shine.

Near the tea stall he saw the baker's younger helper looking for napkins. Sami still had three squares in his pocket. He ran them back, careful of the bun sleeve. "These are ready," he said. The helper grinned. "So are you." On the way home the second bun waited, and Sami decided it would be for breakfast, when the waiting would feel like a story instead of a minute. The market's sounds grew softer behind them. Pears bumped gently in the bag. His hands smelled like honey and orange paper.

At home he set the breakfast bun on a plate and covered it with a clean bowl so the night would not dry it out. He washed the stickiness away and told Mama about the pale buns, the freckles of honey, and the napkins he had folded while the oven worked. Mama listened and touched his hair. "You let a good thing take its time," she said. Sami looked at the covered plate and felt full in the way that is not only about food. The problem had been a hungry hurry, and patience had turned it into a warm walk.

They paused by a stall of wooden spoons so Sami could rest his warm sleeve against his chest. A vendor sanded a handle with slow strokes that sounded like a cat's purr. "Good work takes the time it takes," the vendor said, mostly to the spoon. Sami thought of the buns and felt proud that he had not tugged the hurry string. He watched a curl of wood fall, pale and perfect, and he did not ask for it. Some finishes were meant to stay where they were made.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the covered plate. Sami watched the light sit on the bowl like a quiet lid. Outside, far down the hill, a few market lanterns still swayed, small as seeds. He thought of honey learning to shine and of napkins stacked in squares. His breath slowed. Tomorrow's bun would taste like the waiting he had done well. He closed his eyes, and the lane of peach-colored sky folded itself into sleep.
