Leandro sat on the butter-yellow bench of the slow ferry, a grown-up on one side and a round tin on his lap. Inside the tin, lemon slices lay in a bright spiral, smelling sharp and sunny. A small jar of honey and a pot of mint tea waited on the guide's tray, thick cups that would not tip because the boat was calm. "The lemons are for the whole bench," his grown-up said. "One slice each, and the rest stays for the ride back." Leandro put his palm on the lid. He loved lemon more than almost any taste. "What if I am the most lemon person here?" he asked.

The ferry guide smiled without teasing. "Then you are the best person to offer it, because you know how good it is." Leandro was not sure. Offering meant watching other people take the thing he wanted. He looked at the passengers: a neighbor with a sun hat, a quiet child with a counting stone, a grown-up who had been gazing at the floorboards. There were five slices and five people, if he counted himself and not the guide, who had already had tea on the shore. The math was kind. The feeling was tighter than the math.

The guide poured mint tea. Steam rose in soft ribbons. "Lemon?" the guide asked, looking at Leandro, not grabbing the tin. Leandro took a breath. He opened the lid. The smell jumped out, wonderful and shared already, because smells do not stay in laps. "One slice each," he said, and his voice came out almost steady. He held the tin toward the quiet child first. The child took a slice with two fingers and said, "Thank you," as if the words were also a gift. Leandro's chest loosened one notch.

He offered the neighbor, then the floorboards grown-up, who looked up and chose a slice and smiled at the red scarf on the bell as if color and lemon belonged together. Leandro's grown-up took one and squeezed it gently into the tea. One slice remained, the largest, which felt like a test. Leandro could say the tin had run out. Everyone would believe him. He looked at the slice. It was his. It was also the fair one. He put it in his own cup. "That is mine, and that is enough," he said. The honey jar went around after, a gold thread in each tea.

They sipped as the ferry passed under the bridge. Shade, then sun. The lemon made the mint brighter. Leandro watched the quiet child swing their feet, happier, not because of a toy, but because someone had included them. "The ride tastes bigger," Leandro said, surprised. His grown-up nodded. "Sharing a favorite thing can do that. You did not give away the whole tin. You gave the right amount." Leandro licked a drop from his lip. It was still lemon. He had not lost lemon by letting it travel.

The guide said, "On the way back, the second tin is yours to offer too, if you want the job." Leandro thought. The job was not a burden if the spiral of slices was a way of saying you are on this bench with me. "I want it," he said. "I might still feel the tight feeling. I can open the lid anyway." The guide tapped the kind bell once, a low note, and the scarf fluttered. Leandro laughed. "Even the bell shares its sound." The river carried the note away for anyone on the path to hear.

He closed the empty tin carefully so it would not dent, and set it beside the honey jar. His hands smelled like citrus. He did not wipe them off at once. The smell was a reminder that generosity could stay on your skin. The quiet child showed him the counting stone, smooth and dotted, and Leandro showed the child the tin's neat hinge. They traded looking, not things they needed to keep. The ferry's slowness, which sometimes made him fidget, felt like time enough for tea.

At the landing nobody rushed. Leandro stood when his grown-up stood. He looked back at the bench, at the cups nested in their rack, at the place where the tin had been a question. He had answered it. The plaza fountain flashed ahead, and the clock tower chimed as if it liked punctual boats. Leandro's cup of sweetness was inside him now, and so were four other people's sips, in a way that did not take his away. He liked that kind of math better than hoarding.

On the ramp he touched the tin's hinge once, a promise for the ride back. "One slice is still a whole slice," he told his grown-up. "I do not have to take the spiral to taste the sun." His grown-up said that was the heart of sharing. Leandro watched the red scarf on the bell as the ferry prepared to rest, and he thought the scarf and the lemon were alike. Both were bright, and both were better when people could see them and share the moment.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the ferry cabin window as they climbed the ramp. Leandro glanced at the round tin, ready for the return ride. Sharing the lemon slices, even though he loved them, had made the slow bench feel generous. He walked toward the plaza with mint and lemon still bright on his tongue, and with room in his hands for someone else's smile.
