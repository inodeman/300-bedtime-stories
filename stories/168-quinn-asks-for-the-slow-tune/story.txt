Quinn held a small unlit lantern and stood where the town green met the lane. The evening festival walk was about to begin. Families waited in a loose line, paper lights pale against the dimming sky. Nana had her shawl pinned and her comfortable shoes on. She could walk a long time if the pace stayed friendly. Near the oak, Miss Pell lifted a flute, and a drummer tried a bright pattern that made feet want to skip. It was pretty. It was also quicker than the way Nana liked to go.

Quinn watched Nana's hand on her own lantern handle. The hand was steady, and the steps she practiced in place were short. The bright pattern did not match those steps. Quinn felt a flutter in the middle, the kind that comes before a necessary sentence. No one looked unkind. The musicians were smiling, waiting for a nod from the walk guide. Quinn could stay silent and let Nana fall a little behind. The thought did not fit. Quinn stepped closer to Miss Pell, close enough to be heard and not so close as to bump the flute.

"Excuse me," Quinn said. The voice came out smaller than the flutter, so Quinn tried again, still gentle. "Excuse me. My nana walks best with the slow tune, the one that sounds like rocking. Could we begin with that? Then the line can stay together." Miss Pell lowered the flute and really looked. "Thank you for telling me," she said. "A walk is a shared road, not a race. The rocking tune is a good start." The drummer nodded and softened his hands. "I can play like a porch swing," he said. Quinn's flutter eased into warmth.

Nana had heard. She touched Quinn's shoulder. "That was a clear kindness," she said. "I would have managed, and it is better that you asked. Asking let the music take care of many feet, not only mine." Quinn had not thought of the other walkers. Now Quinn saw Mr. Ade with his careful cane, and a parent carrying a covered pie with both hands. The slow tune would serve them too. Quinn stood a little taller without trying to look tall. Courage, it seemed, could be a sentence offered before the song began.

Miss Pell played the rocking tune. It rose and fell like a chair on a porch, unhurried. The drummer answered with a sway. The guide lifted a hand, and the lanterns were lit from a single sheltered candle, passed along without rush. Quinn's paper shade glowed apricot. Nana's glowed cream. They stepped onto the lane together. The music walked beside them instead of ahead. Quinn listened for the places where the tune took a breath, and those breaths matched Nana's. "Better," Nana said. "My shoes agree."

A little way along, the lane widened and the drummer's hands grew cheerful. The tune lifted, not much, but Nana's steps shortened to keep up. Quinn felt the flutter return, smaller now. "Miss Pell," Quinn said, walking up beside her without crossing the flute, "the porch swing is becoming a fast swing." Miss Pell's eyes thanked him over the mouthpiece. She eased the phrase back down, and the drummer followed as if he had been waiting for permission to relax. Nana's shoulders dropped. "Good ear," she murmured. Quinn's ears felt warm in the evening air, and the apricot lantern stopped bobbing quite so quickly.

Along the way, people shared small foods from baskets so no one had to stop the whole line. Quinn passed a napkin of walnut pieces to Nana and took one. The walnut was toasty and a little sweet. A neighbor offered dried apple rings that smelled like the orchard. Quinn thanked them in the same clear voice that had spoken to the flute, and noticed the voice was easier now. Windows shone. A cat watched from a step and did not follow. The town felt held together by the slow song, like beads on a soft string.

Halfway, Miss Pell glanced back. Quinn gave a small nod that meant the pace was still kind. Miss Pell smiled around the flute and kept the tune from speeding when the lane sloped down. Downhill wanted hurry. The music declined to hurry. Quinn felt proud in a quiet pocket of the chest. "You watched after your words," Nana said. "That is the second half of speaking up." Quinn remembered the nod. Words began a thing. Attention kept it good.

When the walk reached the green again, there was warm cider in small cups and a last humming of the rocking tune without instruments. Quinn sat with Nana on a bench and let the cider steam touch both their faces. It tasted like apples and a hint of clove. "Was it hard to ask?" Nana said. "A little, at the first word," Quinn answered. "Then it felt like making room." Nana tapped her cup to Quinn's. The tap was soft. People around them looked comfortable, pies unspilled, canes unhurried, lanterns steady.

Miss Pell found them on the bench after the cups were filled. "Your sentence tuned us," she said. "I was thinking of sparkle. You reminded me of company." Quinn did not know what to do with praise, so Quinn offered her a cinnamon stick from the cider tray, still dry and fragrant. She tucked it into her flute case like a small souvenir. The drummer tapped the rocking rhythm on his knee once more, just for Nana, and she tapped it back on the bench. The wood answered with a soft tick. Quinn stored the sound beside the memory of speaking twice, the first time to begin and the second time to keep the promise.

At home Quinn set the apricot lantern on the table and told the story of the porch-swing drum to Papa, who had stayed to mind the soup. Papa said a town could be tuned, just like a flute, if someone named the right speed. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and Quinn looked out toward the green, glad a small brave sentence had given the whole walk room to breathe.
