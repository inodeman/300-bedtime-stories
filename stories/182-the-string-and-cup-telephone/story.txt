The string between the two open windows sagged like a sleepy jump rope. Supper smells still floated in the lane, warm rice and a little cinnamon. Dina held a paper cup to her mouth and felt her heart tap tap tap. Across the narrow gap, Ekon's window glowed. His elbow rested on the sill. "Can you hear me?" he called in an ordinary voice. Dina heard him through the air just fine. She wanted the cup phone to work, but she also wanted her own voice to feel brave.

"I have a joke," she said, not very loudly. The words seemed to fall into the flowers below. Ekon leaned farther, careful, with both feet on his floor. "I cannot hear the joke," he said kindly. "Only the sparrows." A sparrow did cheep from the gutter, sharp and cheerful. Dina looked at the two cups and the long string Papa had helped them tape inside. The string drooped in the middle and brushed a leaf. "Maybe it is broken," she whispered. Her cheeks felt warm. Trying again felt bigger than the gap.

Ekon lifted his cup. "Papa said the string must be tight, like a clothesline, not loose like a noodle," he said. "And he said we should not lean out. We talk, and a grown-up checks the knots." Dina nodded. She liked plans. Plans gave her courage a place to stand. She tied a scrap of blue cloth to her end of the string so they would know which way was hers. The cloth fluttered, soft as a sigh, and smelled a little like laundry soap.

Papa came to Dina's window with careful hands. He did not laugh at the sagging string. "You noticed the important part," he said. "A loose line swallows words. May I pull it tighter while you both hold your cups?" "Yes, please," Dina said. Her voice was still small, but it was clear. Papa drew the string until it hummed when he plucked it with one finger. The sound was a tiny boing that made Ekon smile. "Fingers away from the tape while I press it down," Papa said. They waited. The tape squished flat, sticky and sure.

"Your turn," Ekon said into his cup. Dina lifted hers. It smelled like dry paper and a hint of the apple juice they had used the cup for earlier, now washed and clean. She took a breath that filled her chest. The joke was about a snail who carried a backpack made of a leaf. It was not a loud joke. It was a gentle one. She spoke right into the cup, closer than a secret. "Why did the snail bring a leaf?" she asked. Then she put the cup to her ear, wondering if silence would answer.

Ekon's laugh arrived as a warm buzz in the paper. "Why?" he asked, and the word tickled her ear. Dina smiled so hard her nose wrinkled. She spoke again, a little stronger. "Because even a slow walker likes a snack for the road." Ekon laughed properly this time, the sound round inside the cup. The string stayed tight. A moth brushed the blue cloth and wandered off toward the streetlamp, pale and harmless. Dina felt her shoulders drop. Her voice had crossed the gap. It had not needed to be big. It had needed a try.

They took turns. Ekon told her that his soup had a noodle shaped like a ring. Dina told him the crickets were starting, a soft creak creak in the bushes. When the string went a little slack again, they did not give up. Dina called Papa, and he helped them tighten it once more. "You were right to ask," Papa said. Ekon added, "And you were brave to send the joke first." Dina touched the blue cloth. It felt like a small flag for courage. The paper cup was light in her hand, but the friendship inside it felt full.

They said good night three times, because good night through a cup was too nice to do only once. The string made one last tiny hum when the breeze tested it. Dina set her cup on the sill where she could find it in the morning. She could still taste cinnamon in the air, and the sparrows had gone quiet. The blue cloth stirred, then rested. Ekon waved, both feet safe on his floor, and Dina waved back. Her heart was not tap-tapping now. It felt steady, like the tight string. She touched her throat and smiled, surprised that brave could feel so gentle.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in Dina's room, a small gold circle on the wall. She looked at the cup and the blue cloth and whispered the snail joke once more, just to hear her own brave voice in the quiet. The paper smelled faintly of clean apple, and the string's shadow lay straight across the sill. Across the gap, Ekon's light blinked a friendly good night. The string held, neat and firm, ready for tomorrow's words, and Dina let her shoulders rest.
