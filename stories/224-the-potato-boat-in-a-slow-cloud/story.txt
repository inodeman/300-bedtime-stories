Davin lay on a blue blanket in the meadow with his chin on his folded arms. The sky was a wide calm page. Clouds moved so slowly that they seemed to be thinking. Paloma sat beside him with a stick of pale chalk and a small board for notes. "I only see plain white puffs," Davin said. "They look like mashed clouds with no pictures in them." Paloma tilted her head. "Plain is a place pictures like to hide. Let's give them time to show a shape."

They watched one cloud peel away from a larger puff. Paloma pointed with the chalk, not touching the sky, only aiming her idea. "That one is a boat," she said. "See the round cabin and the long deck?" Davin squinted. At first he saw a potato. He did not laugh at her boat, and he did not give up on his potato. "Maybe it is a boat carrying a potato," he said. Paloma clapped her knees. "Yes. A picture can hold two ideas if we build it kindly."

Davin sat up, interested now. Creativity, he was learning, was not a test with one right cloud. It was a workshop in the air. He studied a long streak. "That is a loaf of bread," he said, "with a crack along the crust." Paloma added raisins made from tiny darker puffs. They both laughed. Davin drew a quick loaf on the board, then a boat, then left space for Paloma's next idea. The chalk squeaked in a friendly way. Above them, the clouds did not mind being borrowed for art.

A bigger cloud arrived, soft at the edges. Davin thought it looked like a whale. Paloma thought it looked like a mitten. They paused. Davin felt the old wish to win the guessing. Then he remembered the potato boat. "We can turn the board over and keep both," he said. On one side he sketched a whale with a kind eye. On the other, Paloma drew a mitten big enough for a hill. They held the board up so the real cloud could float between their two drawings, as if it approved of the game.

They tried sound as well as lines. Davin hummed a low note for the whale and a warm bakery hum for the bread. Paloma tapped the board like gentle rain on a boat deck. The meadow grass answered with a hush. Davin noticed that making a picture changed how he felt the day. The sky was not empty. It was a slow parade. He asked Paloma if clouds got tired of being animals. She said they probably liked the rest between shapes, the way artists step back from a page.

When a cloud thinned into wisps, Davin did not call it ruined. "It is becoming steam from a cup," he said. Paloma nodded. "Or threads for a kite tail." They added those words to the board. Davin liked that a disappearing line could still be a new idea. Creativity did not have to grip a picture too tightly. It could wave as the wind changed the paint. He rolled onto his back again and let the next cloud arrive without forcing it. The waiting made the seeing sharper.

A small child shape of cloud, really just a puff with a bump, passed over the blanket. Davin almost said it was nothing. Instead he asked, "What does it need?" Paloma answered, "A story job." So they gave it a job. It carried the whale's mail to the bread loaf boat. The idea was silly and gentle, and it made them both grin. Davin wrote one sentence on the board. The sky delivers letters made of light. He read it aloud in a clear voice, proud but not loud, so the meadow could keep its peace.

Before they left, they copied their favorite shapes onto the path with chalk, small and low, where feet could walk around them. A boat. A loaf. A whale. A mitten. Davin stepped back. "I came here seeing only white," he said. "Now the white feels full." Paloma bumped his shoulder in a friendly way. "You made room for more than one answer. That is a real kind of making." They shook the blanket, watched one last cloud drift toward the harbor, and started home with chalk dust on their fingers like a pale reminder.

They played one more round before folding the blanket. Davin closed his eyes and Paloma guided his face toward a cloud with words only. "Soft on the left, taller on the right, a notch like a bite." Davin guessed a cup, then a nest, then laughed when Paloma said it was a shoe for a giant who tiptoed. He opened his eyes and saw the shoe at once. Making pictures from sound and from sight felt like two brushes. He thanked her for not giving the answer too soon. The wait had let his own creativity wake up and join hers under the wide, unhurried sky.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Davin propped the little board beside it. In the warm light the drawings looked ready for another look. He whispered the mail-cloud story once more, just for the joy of it. Outside, the evening sky kept a few slow puffs, still changing, still kind. Davin felt creative and calm, glad that pictures could be shared and that the sky never scolded anyone for seeing a potato and a boat at the same time.
