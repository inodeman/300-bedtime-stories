Amina’s basil looked tired, and the tiredness worried her more than she wanted to say. The leaves drooped like little green flags at the end of a long parade. She had watered it. She had said good morning to it. Still, the plant seemed to lean away from her, toward the balcony rail, as if it wanted a different neighbor. When her friend Boris came over with a paper bag of bread, he found her sitting on the little balcony stool with her chin in her hands.

“I think I am a bad plant friend,” Amina said. “I feel guilty, and I also feel confused. I did the things I know.” Boris set the bread inside and came back out. He did not laugh at the droop. He looked for a long quiet minute, which was already a kind of friendship. The sun lay hard on the pot. The railing’s shadow stopped just short of the leaves. Boris touched the soil and found it warm, almost hot. “I do not think you forgot the plant,” he said. “I think the sun is giving it too big a hug.”

Amina blinked. She had been staring at herself as the problem. Boris was staring at the place. “Friendship is noticing with someone,” he said, a little shy of his own wise sentence. “My grandpa’s mint likes morning sun and afternoon shade. Maybe your basil is the same.” Amina felt relief rush in so fast she laughed. “We can move it,” she said. “Will you help? The pot is heavier than it looks when it is full of water.” Boris nodded. “That is why friends have two sets of hands.”

They gripped the pot together, fingers away from the tender stems. “On three,” Amina said. “One, two, three.” They shuffled, not lifted high, across the balcony to the corner where the wall made a cool triangle of shade. A leaf brushed Amina’s cheek and smelled bright. They set the pot down and turned it so the droopiest side could rest. Then they waited to see if they had blocked the door. They had, a little. They nudged the pot a hand’s width and checked again. The path was clear. The plant sat like a guest who had been offered a better chair.

Boris filled a cup with water from the kitchen and let Amina pour, because it was her plant and he was the helper, not the boss. The water sank in slowly. No muddy river. Amina’s guilty feeling loosened its belt. “I still feel a little sad that it drooped,” she said. “Sad can stay for a minute,” Boris answered. “It does not mean you failed. It means you care.” They sat on the stool side by side, which meant one of them was half off the stool, and that made them both snort. Friendship felt like shared shade and a silly seat.

They made a small sign from scrap paper. MORNING SUN, AFTERNOON SHADE. Amina drew a sun with a sleepy face and a corner with a smile. Boris held the tape. They did not stick the sign on a leaf. They stuck it on the pot’s rim, low, where it would not shade the plant more than the wall already did. Amina said, “If you ever have a droopy something, I will come look at the place, not only at you.” Boris looked pleased in his ears, which turned pink. “Deal,” he said. “We can be each other’s noticing friends.”

Inside, they sliced the bread and ate it with a thin shine of honey. Amina told Boris about the first day she planted the basil, how the seeds looked like specks of pepper. Boris told her about a bean vine at his home that only climbed after he moved its string closer. Their stories swapped across the table like extra slices. Neither story was a contest. Each story made the other person nod. Amina realized friendship was also trading what you have learned so nobody has to feel guilty alone.

Before Boris left, they checked the basil one more time. The leaves had not sprung up like magic, and they did not pretend otherwise. One leaf, though, had uncurled enough to catch the softer light. Amina whispered, “Thank you for staying.” Boris whispered, “Thank you for letting me help.” They bumped shoulders, gentle as pots being set down. The balcony corner looked friendlier, and so did the afternoon. Amina waved from the door until Boris reached the stair, then she waved at the plant too, just to be fair. She wrote a tiny note for herself and stuck it beside the sink. Check the shade, not only the water. Tell Boris what the leaf does tomorrow. The note made the friendship feel like it had a next page, not only a good afternoon. She washed the honey knife and thought about how quickly guilt had turned into a plan once someone looked with her. The basil in the cool corner was already part of a story she wanted to keep telling kindly.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the shady corner beyond the glass. Amina could see the little sign and the two sets of fingerprints she imagined still on the pot. Friendship had moved something heavy into a kinder light, and the kitchen felt leafy, grateful, and not alone.
