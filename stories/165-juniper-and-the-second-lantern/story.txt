Juniper had walked the town bridge many times in daylight, when the canal looked green and the rail felt ordinary under her hand. On the night of the lantern walk, the same bridge seemed like a new instrument. People moved slowly, each carrying a paper light. The water held every lantern in a second, wobbly copy. Juniper walked between Mama and their neighbor, Mr. Cho, who had a lantern painted with a heron. No one was loud. Shoes made soft ticks on the wood. Somewhere ahead, a flute played a tune that sounded longer whenever the walk passed under the low arch.

"Look down, then look up," Mama said. "The water and the sky are having a conversation." Juniper looked down. Her own lantern, the color of apricot, floated beneath her as if the canal had borrowed it. When she lifted the real one a little, the copy lifted too, late by a breath. She laughed without breaking the quiet. "It is following, but politely," she said. Mr. Cho's heron rippled when a leaf spun past. The leaf was brown and small, and it did not mind becoming part of the picture. Juniper watched it slide under the bridge and vanish into a darker shine, not gone, only around the bend.

Under the arch the air cooled. The flute note swelled, round as a bowl, then thinned again when they came out on the far side. Juniper stopped for a moment with Mama's hand still loosely in hers. "The bridge sings back," she whispered. Mr. Cho nodded. "Stone and water like to keep a sound for a little while. They are not in a hurry." Juniper tried a tiny hum of her own, just three notes. The arch gave them back softer, as if it were shy and kind. She did not hum louder. She wanted to hear the place, not cover it. A ribbon of light from many lanterns trembled on the underside of the arch like quiet fish.

Juniper asked Mama if they could stand still for three breaths in the middle of the bridge. People passed them kindly, lanterns nodding. In those breaths Juniper heard the flute, the water tapping the posts, and her own sleeve whispering against her coat. The apricot copy below steadied when she steadied. "It listens when I listen," she said. Mr. Cho held his heron lantern a little lower so its reflection could stand beside hers. The two copies almost touched, then a ring from a falling leaf opened between them like a soft door. Nobody needed to speak loudly. The night was already explaining itself.

On the far bank the walk curved past the closed bookshop. The window showed a stack of stories, and the lanterns painted moving gold on the glass. Juniper saw her apricot light cross a blue one and make a brief purple. "Colors can visit each other," she said. Mama squeezed her hand. "Only for a moment, and then they are themselves again." That felt like a wonder Juniper could keep. She watched a blue lantern and a yellow one pass, and the meeting place on the window glowed green. She did not need to own the green. It was enough to notice it and let it go on down the street.

Near the bookshop, Juniper noticed the stacked stories in the window seemed to lean toward the light, though of course the books were only resting. She told Mama that wonder could be a trick of gold on glass and still be real. Mama said, "Real can be gentle." They shared a small roll from Mama's pocket, torn in half, the crumb warm and salty. Juniper brushed seeds from her palm into a flower box, not onto the walk. A cricket began in the box, then thought better of it and went quiet, as if saving its note for later. Juniper saved her next hum for the arch.

At the square, the walk paused for shared food that did not interrupt the hush. There were warm rolls, pear slices, and cups of mild broth that smelled of leeks. Juniper sat on a low wall and held her lantern steady between her feet, the handle hooked on her shoe so it would not tip. The broth warmed her palms through the cup. She told Mr. Cho about the purple and the green. He told her that his heron, in the water, had seemed to blink. "Maybe the canal was winking," Juniper said. They both smiled into their cups. The flute began again, farther off, and the bridge answered even from here, a faint round echo.

Juniper asked if they could cross once more on the way home, slowly. Mama said yes. The second crossing was quieter, because many people had already turned toward their lanes. The water copies were fewer and clearer. Juniper lifted and lowered her lantern just to see the apricot friend below do the same. A single flute phrase wandered into the arch and stayed there like a guest who knew how to sit still. "I think the night is wider than the street," Juniper said. "It has a downstairs in the water and a song inside the stone." Mama did not correct the idea. "That is a good way to measure," she said.

When the square's broth was finished, Juniper rinsed her cup at the washing tub and stacked it where the rims made a tiny chime. The chime was one note, clear and short. She compared it to the bridge's answer and decided both were music, one small and one wide. Mr. Cho said the town had many instruments that did not know they were instruments: water, stone, glass, even a careful footstep. Juniper tried one careful footstep and heard it answer the boards. She grinned and then made the next steps ordinary again so the listening could spread out.

They left Mr. Cho at his gate, where his heron lantern nodded good night. Juniper's arms were pleasantly tired. At home she blew out her candle with Mama's snuffer and watched the apricot paper go dim, while the memory of its water twin stayed bright. She set the lantern on the table and touched the rail-smell still on her fingers, a little like wet wood and cool iron. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and Juniper stood beside it, wondering how many gentle copies of the evening the canal was still carrying under the bridge.
