Cosmo shook the big quilt over the rug until it settled in soft hills. One corner was thicker than the rest, stuffed with extra cotton, and it was the best place to sit. Cosmo always chose it first. Tonight Uncle came in from the balcony with cool hands and a smile, carrying a plate of plain star-shaped oat biscuits they had baked before sunset. The window lamp was already on. Hot milk steamed in a small pitcher. Crickets answered one another in the garden, and the moon looked round enough to share with anybody.

Cosmo sat down in the thick corner and felt the quilt rise around him like a nest. Uncle rubbed his fingers and pretended to shiver in a silly way. "The balcony is a good place to greet the moon," Uncle said, "and then it is a good place to leave." He reached for a biscuit. Cosmo looked at the soft corner, then at Uncle's cool fingers, and something in his chest made a kind decision. He scooted over. "This part is the warmest," he said. "You can have it. I will take the middle."

Uncle's eyebrows went up in a happy surprise. "Are you sure? You love that corner." Cosmo nodded. "I can love the middle too." He smoothed the thick corner flat so Uncle could settle in. Then he looked at the biscuits. There were five. He counted with a whisper. "Two for you, two for me, and one on the plate for the person who wants a later bite." Uncle laughed. "That is fair sharing. The later bite can be for Grandma when she finishes hanging the paper stars."

They poured the hot milk into two cups, Cosmo holding the pitcher with Uncle's hand nearby so the stream stayed steady. The cups did not match. Cosmo's had a chip on the handle, and he liked it anyway because it still held milk perfectly. He gave Uncle the cup without the chip. "You have been working with paper," Cosmo said. "Your cup should be easy to hold." Uncle traded a look of thanks that felt as warm as the milk. They tapped cups gently, a tiny clink, and listened to the crickets between sips.

Cosmo still wanted a long turn with the cardboard telescope. The tube lay on the quilt, smelling faintly of paper and paste. He lifted it, saw the moon in a quick glance, and then held it out. "Your turn first, since your hands were cold. Count one cricket song, then I will look." Uncle did not argue. He watched the moon, then passed the tube back right away. Sharing went faster when nobody clutched. Cosmo's turn felt even sweeter because it had been offered, not grabbed. He found the Big Dipper's spoon shape and showed Uncle the handle.

Grandma came in with bits of paste on her thumb and a new paper star dangling from a string. Cosmo pointed at the biscuit waiting on the plate. "That one has your name, only we did not write it," he said. Grandma sat on the quilt's far edge, and Cosmo shifted so her knees could have some of the thick corner too. Three people made the nest smaller and the evening bigger. They took turns naming one gentle thing in the sky. Grandma chose the moon. Uncle chose a bright star. Cosmo chose the way the paper stars in the window echoed the real ones.

Before bedtime Cosmo pressed a glow-in-the-dark sticker onto a spare biscuit napkin, a little circle like a shared moon, and set it by Grandma's cup so she would see a friendly glow later. He folded the quilt only after everyone had stood, so no one lost their warm spot early. The pitcher was empty. The plate held crumbs and a great deal of goodwill. Cosmo put his slippers side by side and felt proud that the best corner had not stayed a secret.

He looked out once more. The balcony was quiet, the garden safe, the moon unbothered by how people divided biscuits. Sharing had not made Cosmo's portion tiny. It had made the room feel like a place where everyone could uncurl their fingers. He hummed with the crickets for a moment and decided the middle of the quilt was a fine country of its own.

Cosmo noticed the cardboard tube had rolled toward his own knee again, as if it wanted to choose him. He laughed and rolled it back to the middle of the quilt. "The middle is for taking turns," he said. Uncle looked through while Cosmo counted one slow cricket song out loud, no faster at the end. Then Grandma had a turn, and she found a bright star that was not in the spoon at all. Cosmo clapped softly. Sharing the telescope made the sky feel less like a prize and more like a window they could stand at together. He broke the last biscuit into three little points and passed them around. Crumbs fell on the quilt, and nobody minded, because the evening was already sweet enough to sweep later.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the folded quilt and the plate with its last crumbs. Paper stars rested against the glass. The cardboard tube lay where the next person could reach it without asking twice. Cosmo climbed into bed with warm milk still sweet on his tongue, glad that the best corner had been large enough for more than one.
