Gul creased a sheet of pale blue paper down the middle and ran a thumb along the fold until it was sharp. The paper smelled dry and clean, like the inside of a new notebook. Hester sat across the wide wash basin, which Papa had set on a low bench and filled with a hand's depth of cool water. The basin was for floating, not for any rushing river. It reflected the window and a slice of sky. "One boat for you and one for me," Gul said. "But there is only one pond, so we share it." Hester nodded. "Sharing a pond means our waves belong to both of us."

They folded in the corners, then the sides, the way a neighbor had shown them with slow hands. Gul's boat came out tall and a bit tippy. Hester's was wider, with a sturdy bottom. "Yours looks like it can carry a button," Gul said, offering a spare blue button as a pretend passenger. Hester grinned. "Yours looks fast. Maybe it carries a whisper." They set the boats on the water at the same moment. Ripples ran out, met in the middle, and made a wobble that tickled both hulls. Gul's tall boat leaned. "Oh," Gul said, reaching, then stopping. "If I grab, I will make a storm. May I have the middle for a second to steady it?"

"Yes," Hester said at once, pulling her wide boat toward her own edge to give room. "The middle can be yours for three breaths. Then we trade." She counted softly. Gul blew a tiny breath, not a blast, and the tall boat straightened. "Thank you for the space." When the three breaths ended, Gul guided the boat back. "Your turn for the wide part." They made a fair pattern. Each sailor had a turn to cross the bright middle, where the window's reflection looked like a path of coins. The water smelled like the clean tap and a drop of lemon soap from the basin's last bath.

Then a drip from Gul's wrist hit the tall boat's paper edge. The blue paper darkened and softened. The boat sagged and took on a little water, the button passenger sliding. "It is getting tired," Gul said, voice small. Hester did not splash or tease. She lifted the soggy boat out with two careful fingers and set it on a towel. "We can let it rest and fold a new one. I have paper. We can share my sheet." She tore nothing in a hurry. She creased a fresh square and slid it across. "You fold the pointed parts. I will hold the corners so they do not escape."

Together they made a second boat, lower and stronger, with a wider belly. Hester showed how to press the bottom flat on the table before it ever met the water. The paper crackled softly. Gul added a tiny paper seat so the button would have a place. "This one learned from the first," Gul said. They launched it. It sat high and calm. Hester's boat came alongside, not bumping, just neighborly. They took turns blowing, cheeks round, breaths warm and gentle. When both boats drifted into the same corner, they laughed and used a finger to share the corner, nudging one left and one right.

They played until their fingers were wrinkled and cool. The first boat, almost dry on the towel, was too wavy to sail again, so they did not force it. Gul said, "It can be a little dish for paper clips." Hester liked that. A thing that could not float could still be useful. They poured the basin water into the garden pot outside the door, slowly, so the dirt could drink. The mint leaves trembled and then shone. Back inside, they set both good boats on the windowsill to dry completely, the button still aboard the new one like a captain with a quiet job.

Gul looked at the shared sheet of paper, now only a small leftover strip. "You gave me half of your paper," Gul said. "I want to give you half of my button tin." Hester shook her head kindly. "You already gave me turns in the middle, and you let my boat have the corner. That was the share I wanted." They bumped shoulders, a friendly tap, and ate crackers that tasted salty and toasted. Crumbs stayed on the plates. The basin, empty now, rang softly when Gul tapped it with a fingernail, a bright ping that made them both grin.

No one said their boats were the grandest on any sea. They were paper, and they were theirs, and they had sailed a pond the size of a basin without a single unkind splash. Gul traced the fold lines and felt how practice lived in the creases. Hester set the button-boat where the breeze could rock it just a little, a whisper of air, not a wind.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the two dry boats and the wavy one that had become a clip dish. Gul and Hester watched the light shake in a last drop inside the empty basin. They had shared water, paper, and turns, and the sharing had made the play bigger, not smaller. The boats rested, finished and friendly, as the evening grew quiet around them.
