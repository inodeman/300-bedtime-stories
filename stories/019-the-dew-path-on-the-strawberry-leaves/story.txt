Ivy stepped into the garden with a bowl and a plan to be quick. Breakfast was near, and the strawberries along the sunny edge were red enough to be called ready. Dew had settled on everything, a cool jewelry the sun had not collected yet. Her bare ankles brushed rosemary, and the smell rose sharp and piney. A bird tried three notes and saved the rest. Ivy knelt, bowl tucked, fingers ready to pinch stems. Then a line on a leaf caught her. It was silver, not water exactly, a narrow road that gleamed and wandered from the brick border to the biggest berry leaf. She forgot to pinch.

"A snail walked here," she whispered. The snail was still in the story, under a ruffled leaf, small and brown, horns out, in no hurry at all. Its path dried behind it into a faint shine. Ivy's bowl stayed empty. She watched the horns touch the air, thoughtful as fingers. The snail did not owe her speed. Grandpa's mug steamed at the door. He did not call her in. He had seen the look of a person being captured by a quiet thing. Ivy shifted so her shadow would not fall on the snail. The dew on the strawberry leaves made windows, round ones, and inside each window the garden appeared upside down, tiny and true.

She leaned until her nose nearly met a drop. The upside-down rosemary was a forest. Her own eye became a planet. When she blinked, the planet blinked. Wonder rose in her like the steam from Grandpa's mug. "I almost picked right through it," she said. "I almost treated the morning as a cupboard." The snail turned, a slow comma, and the silver road gained one more curve. Ivy did not move it. She did not put it in the bowl. Bowls were for berries that were ready to be eaten, not for neighbors who were busy being snails. She sat back on her heels and let the wet soak a circle on her knees. The cold was a fair price.

Grandpa came as far as the path. "The patch will still be a patch in five minutes," he said. "What did it show you?" Ivy pointed. "A road. And windows. And I think the red berries are wearing hats of light." He looked, really looked, and nodded. "Then pick after the show's first scene. The second scene is jam, and jam can wait its turn." Ivy smiled. They watched together until the sun found the leaves and the dew windows trembled. One drop fell and became ordinary wet. Another stayed, brave and round. The snail slipped under the ruffle completely, show over, life continuing in the cool. Ivy felt she had been a good audience.

Only then did she pick. She chose berries that came off with a gentle lift, red all the way to the shoulders, and she left the pale ones to finish their own wonder tomorrow. Each berry clicked the bowl. Dew wet her fingers and made the red look varnished. She tasted one. It was cold, then sweet, with a little sparkle of tart at the end. "It tastes like it still knows the morning," she said. Grandpa chuckled. "That is because you did not rush the knowing out of it." They left a few ripe ones on the plants for the birds who also kept a breakfast list. Sharing with birds felt like part of watching, not a loss.

Inside, the bowl sat in a stripe of sun. Ivy rinsed the berries and the silver road stayed outdoors where roads belong. She told the upside-down rosemary story while they ate. The berries stained the milk pink, a small magic that did not need a wand. Grandpa put a leaf from the path, one empty leaf, in a glass of water on the sill so they could see its veins like a map. "We do not need the snail," he said. "We need the habit of stopping." Ivy traced a vein with her eyes. She decided her quick plan could start later every time the garden was wearing dew. Later was a kind of door, not a delay to be sorry about.

She washed the bowl and set it mouth-down, ready for another day. Through the window the strawberry leaves had gone from silver to plain green, which was not plain if you had seen the change. The change was the wonder. Ivy sketched a circle on a scrap, a drop, a road, a comma-snail, and pinned it above the sink with a wooden clip. It was not a fine drawing. It was a reminder. Grandpa added a tiny sun in the corner, with permission. Their pencils smelled like cedar. The kitchen smelled like berries. The day, which had nearly been only breakfast, was now a story with a slow middle.

Ivy carried the pink milk cup to the step and finished it where she could see the patch. A bird took one of the berries they had left, neatly, no mess, a guest who knew the rules. Ivy lifted her cup a little in greeting. The snail did not reappear, and she did not go hunting. Some shows closed their curtains. She had arrived in time for a scene, which was plenty. Her knees had dried. Her hurry had dried too.

The lantern by the window glowed softly though the sun was up, a small flame Grandpa liked to leave for a minute at dawn, and it matched the feeling in Ivy's chest. She had stopped long enough to see a dew path, a silver road, and a berry that still tasted like morning. Wonder had been waiting in the patch, patient as a snail, glad for a girl who set her bowl down and looked.
