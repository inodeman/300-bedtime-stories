Elodie noticed the stool before she noticed the view. It stood on the cottage step, three wooden legs, one of them shy of the ground by a pebble's height, the seat worn smooth as soap. The stool rocked under her hand. From the path above, she could see the lighthouse keeper standing at the lookout rail with nothing under him but his own legs, watching the little wooden boats come home in the bright wind.

She knew he liked that rail. He liked the count of boats and the color of the water and the gulls that sometimes led a lost line back to a cleat by simply sitting on the right post. She also knew his knees complained when the watching went long. The stool was down here. The knees were up there. The distance was a kind problem, small enough for a girl with a plan and big enough to matter. Elodie tested the wobble. A flat shell under the short leg helped on the step. On the uneven lookout stones it might not. She would need to carry the stool and a thin wedge of driftwood she had seen by the tank.

"I am borrowing you for a good reason," she said, and tipped the stool onto her back the way Auntie had shown her to carry light chairs, seat against her shoulders, legs in the air like a calm insect. It was awkward and not heavy. The path smelled of warm dune grass. A gull walked ahead, then flew a short hop, as if checking the route for loose sand. Elodie took small steps. When the stool's leg bumped her ear she laughed and shifted. "Sorry," she said, to the stool or herself, both. Below, tide pools flashed. A starfish held its rock. She did not stop to poke. She had a delivery.

Halfway up, the keeper saw her and started down. "That seat has my name worn into it," he called, "though nobody carved it." Elodie shook her head, smiling, and kept climbing so he would not have to. "Stay with the boats. I have this." Her breath grew bigger. Kindness, she discovered, could be a little sweaty and still count. At the top she set the stool down away from the edge, on the flattest stones. It rocked. She slid the driftwood wedge under the shy leg and turned it until the rock stopped. Then she pressed the seat with both hands. Solid. "Try," she said, a bit proud and trying not to show off.

The keeper sat. His face changed the way a sail changes when the right wind arrives, from tight to easy. "Oh," he said. "That is a better watch already." He could see the cove without leaning his knees into the rail. Elodie stood beside him, not needing thanks to make the moment real, but liking the thanks when it came. "You saw a gap and walked it shut," he said. "I saw a stool being a table," she answered. "Tables are fine. Your legs were doing chair work." A boat with a green fender slid past the bell post. They counted it together. The number matched the morning's chalk mark. The keeper's shoulders dropped.

They stayed long enough for Elodie to learn two gulls apart, one with a freckle on its neck, one with a darker tail. Neither bird begged. They rode the air and showed, by their easy circles, where the breeze was smooth. Elodie fetched the keeper's forgotten hat from a hook when the sun grew direct, another small carry, down and up. "You are building a path with your feet," he said. She drank from the shared water bottle and wiped her mouth. "I like useful walking." The stool held. The wedge held. A problem that had been a wobble and a distance was now a place to sit.

When the last fishing-free pleasure boat, a plain wooden one with a red seat, tied up far below, the keeper stood and stretched. "I can take the stool down," he offered. Elodie considered. Kindness did not have to grab every job. "We can share the legs," she said. She took the front. He took the back. The wedge rode in her pocket for next time. The path felt shorter with talk on it. They spoke of nothing grand, only of a cloud like a spoon and a pool that had turned from blue to tea-colored as the light changed. Sharing the carry felt like a second kindness layered on the first.

At the step Elodie set the stool where it would not block the door and tried the wobble again. On flat stone it was almost honest. She still tucked the wedge nearby, visible, so nobody would have to guess. "Tomorrow if your knees want the lookout, the seat can come," she said. The keeper touched the smooth wood. "And if your arms want a rest, I will do the climbing part. Kindness can take turns." Elodie liked that. She had not emptied herself out. She had moved a helpful thing to a helpful place, and the shore looked more comfortable because of it.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the three-legged stool as evening painted the step gold. Elodie looked at it from the doorway and felt the good ache in her shoulders, the kind that tells a true story. Up at the rail the wind could blow and the boats could nod and the keeper could sit when he needed. She had not changed the whole ocean. She had noticed a gap between a person and a rest, and she had carried a bridge made of wood. The lantern made even the shy leg look steady. Elodie smiled, washed her hands of path dust, and left the wedge where the next kind thought could find it.
