The bread smelled like honey and a warm crust, but the pan was still sending up little waves of heat. Fatima held her palms near it, not on it, and felt the warning softness of hot air. Kostas had the cloth wrap ready, a square that would keep the loaf cozy once cozy was safe. Papa was grating lemon at the counter. The zest made the air bright. "We can take it to our auntie when I say the pan is cool enough for your hands," he said. "You may invent while you wait. You may not rush the heat."

Kostas looked at the craft basket. Paper, tape, a smooth stick from the garden that had been sanded. "A breeze you can hold," he said. "If the air moves, the bread rests sooner, and our hands stay sensible." Fatima's eyes lit. She folded a small paper square into a fan, the kind that fits in a pocket. It went flap and moved a curl of hair at her temple. Near the pan, it was a polite cough of air, not a breeze. The crust kept shining. The little fan was cute and not enough, which is useful information.

"Bigger folds," Fatima said. "Like a map of wind." They used a wider sheet. Kostas creased it back and forth until it looked like a paper staircase. The creases popped under his thumbnail in a soft rhythm. Fatima taped the bottom closed around the stick so the fan would not fly apart. Papa glanced over. "Tape is fine. The stick stays in your fist, not near each other's faces. And the fan does not touch the bread. Crumbs and paper are better as friends from a distance." They nodded. The big fan smelled like paper and a little like the lemon on Papa's fingers.

They tried it. Fatima held the stick. Kostas stood where he could feel the result. The fan made a whoosh that lifted a napkin corner and cooled the skin on his arm. Heat over the pan shivered and thinned. It was not magic. It was air going for a walk. They took turns so nobody's wrist got tired. The first small fan sat on the table like a little sister watching a big sister work. Fatima laughed. "Creativity started pocket-sized," she said. "Then it grew to match the job."

Still, when Kostas reached a hopeful hand toward the pan, Papa shook his head with a smile. "The fan is clever. The pan is still warm at the rim. We wait for the middle, too." They waited. Waiting was easier with a job. They fanned, paused, fanned. The honey smell grew rounder, less sharp. A drip of sweetness had hardened on the edge into a glossy button. Fatima did not peel it. It would ride along as a jewel. The kitchen clock clicked. Somewhere outside, a wagon rolled and faded. Their invention kept the waiting from feeling empty.

Papa touched the pan with his own careful finger, then nodded. "Cool enough. Use the cloth. Two hands." The loaf left the pan with a gentle scrape and settled into the wrap, golden on top, soft at the sides. Kostas felt only warmth, the safe kind, through the cloth. Fatima's fan rested against the chair. It had not replaced patience. It had partnered with it. They tied the wrap with a loose bow that would not crush the crust. A little honey smell came through anyway, advertising the errand to the hallway.

Their auntie met them at her gate with flour still on her apron from her own baking day. "You brought the weather with you," she said, seeing the fan in Fatima's other hand. They told her about the small fan that tried and the big fan that helped and the wait that finished the job. She tore pieces of bread. The inside was steamy and pale and sweet. They ate on the step, crumbs on their knees, the fan making one lazy whoosh between bites so the bees would stay interested in flowers instead of honey. Nobody waved it at a bee. The flowers were doing that work.

On the way home Kostas carried the empty cloth, spotted with a gold crumb that still smelled like honey. Fatima carried the fan like a finished idea, the paper ridges ticking softly against her sleeve. The small fan was in her pocket again, a reminder that first tries can be honest and little. Papa washed the pan until it sang under the water. The kitchen lost its heat and kept its good smell. Creativity sat in the chair as a paper staircase on a stick, ready for the next warm loaf and the next careful wait.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the paper fan, which cast a ridged shadow on the wall like a little staircase of light. Fatima folded her hands around a last crumb of sweetness. Kostas watched the shadow and smiled, the stick cool in his fingers. The pan was cool, the neighbor had been fed, and the breeze they had made still seemed to linger, gentle and proud, in the quiet kitchen.
