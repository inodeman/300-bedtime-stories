Fabio lined up three stamps he and Gul had made, each one a small block with a shape on its face. Papa had cut the potato leaf and the potato dot the day before. The children had drawn the wavy line into a foam block with a blunt stylus, pressing until the wave sat deep enough to print. The table wore brown paper. Green and blue paint waited in trays, shiny as wet stones. "A picnic cloth," Fabio said. "Not a plain one. A cloth that could only come from our hands." Gul bounced once on her toes. "Then we need a pattern nobody has in a shop, because we invent it here."

They tested stamps on scrap paper first. The leaf was crisp. The dot was a cheerful pebble. The wave looked like a smile that had been pulled gently at both ends. Gul tilted her head. "What if dots are rain, waves are hills, and leaves are the trees on the hills?" Fabio's eyes brightened. "And we repeat it, but not like a copy machine. Like a song that changes a word each time." Creativity fizzed between them. They taped a clean cotton square to the table so it would not slide, the tape at the very edges where picnic food would not sit.

Fabio rolled blue over the wave and pressed the first hill near the bottom. The cloth took the paint with a soft thump. Gul followed with green leaves above the hill, medium hugs, not smooshes. Then came blue dots for rain, but she put some dots under the hill too. "Underground rain?" Fabio asked. Gul thought. "Roots drink. The dots under the hill are the drink. That is our change in the song." He loved it. They made the next hill a little taller. The next rain fell sideways, because Gul turned the dot stamp and dragged it a breath. A mistake almost, and then a new idea. "Wind," she said. "The picnic has a breeze."

Paint dotted their knuckles. The trays smelled sharp and clean. They talked while they worked, deciding, changing, keeping what surprised them. A leaf printed half off the edge. Fabio groaned, then leaned closer. "It looks like a tree walking into the picture. Let us put one walking tree in each corner, on purpose, so the half leaf becomes a rule." Gul clapped once, quietly, so the cloth would not jump. They built the rule into the other corners. The pattern was theirs: hills, drinking roots, windy rain, and trees that were halfway to somewhere. No two hills matched exactly. The cloth still felt planned, because the plans had room to breathe.

A smear appeared when a sleeve drifted. The blue wave blurred into the green. Fabio's first thought was to hide the cloth and start over, to protect the pride he already felt. He stopped. "I want this to be good," he said. "If I hide the smear, I am making it secretly worse. What if the smear is a pond the hills were missing?" Gul dipped a rag-wrapped thumb in pale blue and rounded the smear into a pond shape, then printed leaf reflections as dots beside it. The repair did not pretend the smear had never happened. It gave the smear a job. "Now the picnic has a place to rinse berries," Gul said. Fabio breathed out. "That is creative and kind."

They lifted the tape and hung the cloth over two chair backs to dry. The paint shone, then settled to a soft color. While they waited, they designed a second, smaller napkin with only dots and one walking tree, a little cousin of the big cloth. The napkin was quick and confident, because the big cloth had taught them. Crumbs of potato from the stamps stayed on the tray. They cleaned the blocks with a damp rag until the shapes were clear again, ready for another day. Water in the rinse cup turned the color of a river after rain.

At snack time they spread the dry picnic cloth on the rug, not outside, because the paint was new and the sky looked undecided. Apples, cheese, and a jar of water sat on the hills. The walking trees pointed toward the pond. Gul said, "I like that we changed the song." Fabio said, "I like that the smear became water." They did not say the cloth was the most beautiful in the world. They said it was finished, useful, and full of choices they could explain. Mama sat on a hill-print and smiled when a dot of windy rain showed by her toe. "Tell me the story of the pattern," she said, and they did, taking turns, neither one stealing the tale.

The apples were crisp. The cheese was salty. The cloth stayed mostly clean because they were careful, and one crumb landed in the printed pond, which made them laugh and move it to a real napkin. Creativity, they decided, could be eaten beside. It did not have to stay on a wall to matter. It could catch a picnic and remember a breeze.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the hills and the little pond when evening tucked the room in. Fabio and Gul folded the cloth with the paint on the inside, gentle so the pattern would not crack. Their made-up landscape was done, a picnic place that had never existed until their stamps and their ideas met.
