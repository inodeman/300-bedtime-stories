Camila liked the little post office because it smelled like paper and orange peel. Mama held her hand at the door. The bell gave a soft ping. Mr. Pell looked up from a stack of letters and smiled over his glasses. "Good morning, Camila. Are you here to buy a stamp or to say hello?" Camila held up a small envelope for Grandma, who lived two streets away, still close enough for a walk. "A stamp, please," she said. "The blue one with the tiny bird, if it is still there." Mr. Pell slid the stamp across the counter. Camila pressed it flat with careful fingers. The bird sat right in the corner, as if it had found a perch.

"Would you like to help me for one quiet minute?" Mr. Pell asked. "I am sorting the lane mail. You may look, but letters stay closed." Camila nodded and stood on the wooden step so she could see the cubbies. Each cubby had a name written on a card. Her own house was there. So was the corner with the flower tubs, and Mrs. Adeyemi's house at the end of the lane. One letter for Mrs. Adeyemi was thick and cream-colored. A blue stamp, almost like Camila's, waited in the corner. "She has not come in today," Mr. Pell said gently. "The step up to our door is a bother for her knees."

Camila looked at Mama. "Could we carry it?" she asked. "It is only a little way. We would not even need the green bus." Mama thought for a moment. "If Mr. Pell says the letter may go by hand, then yes. Kindness should be careful as well as willing." Mr. Pell wrote a tiny note on a card. "Delivered by Camila, with care," he read aloud. He tucked the card under the string around the envelope. "Walk on the path. Do not run. Letters like steady feet." Camila held the envelope against her sweater so the corners would not bend.

They stepped back into the sunny lane. A bicycle bell chimed somewhere behind them, and a gull called from the canal, far enough to sound like a squeaky toy. Camila did not turn toward those sounds. She kept her eyes on the pavement cracks she already knew. "What if she is resting?" she asked. "Then we will set the letter in the dry box by her door," Mama said. "And we will knock only once, like a polite sparrow." Camila practiced a soft knock in the air. Tap. Not a drum. Not a surprise. She smiled at her own quiet hand.

Mrs. Adeyemi's gate had a green latch that Camila lifted slowly. Pots of mint lined the step and made the air smell cool. The door was open a hand's width, and a radio played a song with no words, only a soft hum of horns. Camila knocked once. "Mrs. Adeyemi?" she called. "It is Camila from the post office lane. I have a letter that was waiting in your cubby." A chair creaked. Mrs. Adeyemi came in a yellow cardigan, her smile wide and a little sleepy. "A letter, on a bright Tuesday? What a fine visitor."

Camila held the envelope out with the bird stamp facing up. "Mr. Pell said the step was a bother, so my feet did the walking," she said. Mrs. Adeyemi's eyes shone. She touched the stamp with one finger. "This bird has been on a sorting table, and now it is home," she said. "Would you like one mint leaf to smell? The pots are for sharing, not for stripping." Camila breathed in the cool green smell and took only the leaf that was offered. "Thank you," she said. "I hope the inside says something happy."

"It is from my brother near the hill lookout," Mrs. Adeyemi said, reading the corner without rushing the seal. "He sends news of his tomato pots and a funny drawing of a kite. You have made the news come sooner, and you have made me feel remembered." She waved the card Mr. Pell had written. "Your name is on the journey. That is a kindness with a small ribbon on it." Camila felt warm in her cheeks. She had not thought of herself as part of the letter's trip. She had only seen a full cubby and wondered who was waiting.

On the way home, Camila told the route as if it were a tiny map drawn in the air. "Counter, lane, green latch, mint, smile," she said. Mama laughed. "That is a good map. It has people on it, not just streets." They passed their own front window. The glass was clean. A small brass lantern sat on the sill, unlit while the sun still worked, waiting for evening the way a story waits for its last quiet line. Camila patted the spare stamp in her pocket and felt glad it was still there for another day.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when evening came, and Camila set her stamp book beside it. She drew a small square on a scrap of paper and wrote, in careful letters, "A letter can ride in kind hands." Mama read it and nodded. "Tomorrow we can mail your note to Grandma," she said. "The blue bird can fly two streets and still be near." Camila leaned her cheek toward the warm glass. Outside, the lane was quiet. Somewhere down the hill, Mrs. Adeyemi was reading by her own lamp. Camila whispered, "Good night, letter," and the glow stayed steady, like a promise kept close to home.
