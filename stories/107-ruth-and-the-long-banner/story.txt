Cloth for the school fair banner lay across three tables pushed into a line in the sunny courtyard. The fabric smelled like clean cotton and a little like the grass beside the steps. Ruth held one end, and the letters they had planned, WELCOME FRIENDS, waited in chalk marks that still dusted her fingers. The banner was longer than her arms. When she tried to keep it flat and paint at the same time, the middle rose like a small hill and the chalk W went crooked. Pietro caught the far corner before it slipped toward a paint cup. Faye stood in the middle with a shaker, not sure yet where she fit.

"This wants more than one pair of hands," Ruth said. She smoothed the hill, and the cloth gave a soft whump. "If I paint while I hold, the letters lean. If I only hold, the paint waits too long and the chalk blows away." A light breeze lifted a corner and showed the pale underside, cool against Ruth's wrist when she caught it. Pietro nodded. "I can anchor the end with both palms and move when you say the word slide." Faye shook her shaker once. It sounded like dry beans in a friendly cup. "I can keep time so we do not rush the wet parts. Shake, then rest, then the next letter."

They tried a plan. Pietro held. Ruth painted the W with sunny yellow, following the chalk without hurrying. Faye gave two soft shakes and then a quiet space. In the space, Ruth's brush made a steady line. The paint shone. "Slide," Ruth said, and Pietro walked his hands a knee's length down the cloth. The banner stayed level. Faye smiled. "The rests are doing as much as the shakes." Ruth felt her shoulders drop. Teamwork was not everyone doing the same job. It was jobs that fit together like the lids on the paint cups, each one snug on its own color.

The E came next, then the L. A bee hummed past and did not land, curious and polite. Someone from the music room waved from the doorway and did not interrupt. The courtyard stones were warm under their shoes. When Ruth's brush began to drip, Faye said, "Rest," even though it was not a shake moment, and Ruth wiped the extra paint on the cup rim. "Good catch," Ruth said. "A drip would have made a river through the welcome." Pietro laughed, a small sound that did not jiggle the cloth. "I like being the hands that keep the river from starting."

Halfway along, the word FRIENDS needed more room than the first plan. The chalk marks crowded near the edge. Ruth did not sigh in a heavy way. She looked at her partners. "We can add a second strip of cloth," she said. "There is a spare piece in the basket, the blue one that feels like a pond." Faye brought it. The spare cloth was cool and smooth. Pietro held the seam while Ruth and Faye pressed a line of safe paste along the overlap, fingers wiping clean on a rag that smelled of lemons. They waited, and Faye shook a very slow pattern so the waiting had music. The seam held when they tested a gentle tug.

Now the letters had space. Pietro painted the letter I while Ruth held, because his arms needed a turn at the fun part. The brush felt springy in his hand. Faye kept the beat. Then Faye wanted a turn too, and she painted the N while Ruth shook the shaker. The shaker was lighter than Faye expected, and Ruth made the rhythm even softer so the brush would not bounce. "We can pass the jobs without dropping the welcome," Faye said. The word grew, yellow and clear, each letter with a friend inside the work of it.

They lifted the banner together and hung it between two posts with clips that snapped softly. The cloth rippled once and then settled. WELCOME FRIENDS could be read from the steps. The paint smell drifted and thinned in the open air. Ruth stepped back until the letters looked straight. They were straight because three people had cared about the middle as much as the ends. Pietro brought a damp cloth for chalky fingers. Faye set the shaker in its basket, where it gave one last tiny tick.

Children would come later with pictures and puppet tickets and seed jars to show, and the banner would greet them without anyone having to shout. Ruth traced the air under the word friends, not touching the damp paint. "I could not have held the whole hello alone," she said. Pietro and Faye stood on either side of her, their shadows making a linked shape on the stones. "None of us had to," Faye said.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the classroom just behind the courtyard, and its light reached the edge of the banner like a quiet extra hand. Ruth, Pietro, and Faye watched the letters dry in the sun, shoulder to shoulder, already ready to welcome the next friend who might need a corner to hold.
