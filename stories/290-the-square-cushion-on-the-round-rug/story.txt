Hanan spread the moss-green reading rug in a round pool on the plaza tiles, close enough to the fountain to hear water and far enough to keep the pages dry. She had borrowed a picture book from the book barge, a book about painted wooden fish that all faced the same way. Two friends were coming. Hanan set her favorite cushion in the middle because it was the best seat. The cushion was sunflower yellow, very square, and so plump it looked like a small cheerful crate. She sat on it and opened the book. The rug's round edges looked far away.

The first friend arrived and knelt at the rim. "I can see your knees," the friend said, "but not the fish." The second friend tried the other side and peeked around the yellow square. "The page is a secret from here," that friend said, not unkindly. Hanan felt a pinch. She loved the middle. The middle was where the story felt close. "This cushion is the most comfortable," she said. "It is," said the first friend. "It is also the size of a door." They all laughed a little, which helped. Still, the book stayed hidden behind yellow cloth.

Hanan looked at the rug as if it were a puzzle. Round rug, square cushion, three readers, one book that needed eyes. She stood up. "What if the square goes to the edge, like a slice, and we sit on the round part together?" she asked. "Then my cushion is not the throne. It is a backrest." The friends nodded, hopeful. Hanan dragged the cushion, not roughly, to the rim, and turned it so one flat side faced out and one corner made a soft support. The middle of the rug became empty and inviting, a pond of green with room for knees.

They sat cross-legged, shoulders not crowding, the book on a low flat basket so everyone could see the painted fish. Hanan was not in the exact middle, and for a moment that felt like less. Then the first friend said, "I can see the silver one's eye," and the second friend pointed at a plum fish and read a short line. The story worked because the circle worked. Hanan leaned on the yellow corner and found it still comfortable, only different. "I can feel the cushion and see the page," she said. "That is two good things."

A grown-up from the barge paused to check that the book was happy outdoors. "You made room," the grown-up said. "The rug was always round. The cushion needed a new job." Hanan smoothed a curl of the rug. "I thought the best seat was the one I wanted most. The best seat today was the one that let my friends stay." They read another page. The fish on the page faced one way, and Hanan thought of the real bridge, and of how facing the same way was a kind of sharing a direction. On the rug, they faced the book together.

When a fourth child hovered, shy, Hanan did not put the cushion back in the middle to protect her spot. She scooted a hand's width and patted the green cloth. "There is room if we keep knees tidy," she said. The child sat. The circle grew without breaking. The yellow cushion stayed at the edge, doing its new job as a backrest for whoever needed a lean. Hanan felt friendship as a shape, not a speech: a round space that did not let one square block the view.

They finished the short book and closed it gently. The fountain kept time with little sounds. The clock tower offered one kind chime, and nobody had to shush anyone, because they had been using indoor voices already, plaza edition. Hanan rolled her shoulders. Making room had not made her smaller. It had made the story bigger. She could still like her cushion. Liking it did not mean it must sit in everyone's way.

She helped her friends stack the book on the basket and shake grass fluff off the rug, though there was hardly any. The square cushion looked bright against the moss green, useful and not bossy. Hanan patted it. "You are a good edge," she told it, and her friends smiled at the silly kindness. They agreed to read again tomorrow and to put the plump square at the rim first, before anyone had to peek around it.

Hanan rolled the rug only halfway, then unrolled it again to show her friend where the middle had been empty and kind. "See," she said, "the round part was the real seat. The yellow square is a helper at the edge." Her friend patted the cushion. "It can still be your favorite and not be the boss." Hanan liked that sentence so much she repeated it once, softly, so she would remember it when she wanted the middle again. The book went back toward the barge in careful arms.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the barge window across the plaza, a gold square that reminded Hanan of her cushion and of making a square fit a round kindness. She folded nothing in a rush. She had moved what she loved so her friends could see. The rug held them all, and the painted fish, on the closed cover, faced the same friendly way.
