Habiba had a letter to write, and the words would not line up. Her cousin Lotta lived in another town, far enough that a visit was a someday, not a today. Habiba missed her. The missing sat like an empty stool at the kitchen table. She gripped a pencil and wrote Dear, then stopped. The next word felt heavy. She did not want to spell feelings wrong. She felt frustrated, hot in her cheeks, and also full of pictures. “My words are shy,” she told Grandpa Kamal. “My drawings are not.”

Grandpa Kamal pushed a bigger paper toward her, the kind that did not mind color. “A letter can travel as pictures,” he said. “Creativity is a bridge when sentences feel steep. Lotta knows your drawing hand. She will hear you.” Habiba’s frustrated feeling made room. She drew the kitchen table first, seen from above, like a map. She put an empty stool and labeled it with Lotta’s initial, L, so the seat was saved. She drew a blue bowl of mango cubes with two spoons, a memory of sharing. She drew a pancake shaped like a cloud. The pencil moved faster than the shy words had. Her shoulders dropped.

She added the balcony, a pot of mint with most of its leaves still on, because she had learned that enough was kinder than all. She drew a wooden spoon and a small pot with music marks, a family band frozen in ink. She drew the lantern, not yet glowing, but waiting by the window like a promise of evening. Around the edges she made a path of tiny footprints from her town to a cluster of buildings she imagined as Lotta’s. Between the towns she drew envelopes with wings, not because envelopes really flew, but because that was how her hope felt. The page grew crowded in a cheerful way. It looked like a day Lotta could visit without packing.

Habiba still wanted one word, so the letter would begin like a letter. She asked for help with missed you, and she wrote those two words at the top in careful letters. They were enough words. The pictures did the rest. Under the cloud pancake she wrote yum, which she could spell, and under the stool she wrote yours. Grandpa Kamal did not fix the drawing to make it adult-neat. He said, “It looks like your kitchen told the truth.” Habiba felt proud. Creativity had not been a trick to avoid the missing. It had been a way to carry the missing kindly, with colors, to someone who would understand.

They folded the page so the stool was not creased across its seat. Habiba slid it into an envelope that felt plump and important. She wrote Lotta’s name and checked the town twice. On the back flap she drew a tiny spoon, a signature that was also a joke. Her frustrated heat had cooled into excitement. “I feel like I am walking to her door with this,” she said. Grandpa Kamal nodded. “In a way, you are. A picture letter is a visit that fits in a pocket.” Habiba held the envelope to her chest for one second, a hug sent ahead of the mail.

At the corner box she paused, the way people do when a kindness is about to leave their hands. “What if she wanted more words?” she asked. “Then she can write words back, or pictures back,” Grandpa Kamal said. “You gave her a true piece of your week. That is a complete gift.” Habiba slid the envelope in. The box made its soft metal hush. She imagined Lotta opening the cloud, the stool, the mint, the winged envelopes. The empty stool at home would still be empty, and now it had been drawn on purpose, which made the missing feel seen instead of stuck.

Back in the kitchen Habiba started a second page, not to send yet, but to practice keeping a picture diary for the next letter. She drew the lantern again, this time with lines of light. She drew herself waving. The frustrated feeling did not own the table anymore. Creativity sat there, sleeves rolled up, ready for another town’s eyes. Habiba hummed a spoon-tap rhythm while she worked, so the paper would carry a little sound in its silence. She added one more drawing to the diary page, a corner box with a stamp shaped like a leaf, because the real letter was already traveling and she wanted to remember the feeling of letting it go. “I do not need a long speech,” she told Grandpa Kamal. “I need a true picture and a seat with her initial.” He kissed the top of her head in a grandfather way and said the next letter could be even smaller if the truth was still in it. Habiba liked that. Small and true could cross a town line. Her frustrated feeling waved goodbye from the empty stool, which did not look so empty when it had a name.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the new page and the place where the envelope had rested. Habiba watched the glow and hoped it matched a window far away. Her pictures were on their way to Lotta, a handmade bridge, bright and gentle, between two kitchens that missed each other and knew how to say so.
