Idris laid two cardboard tubes side by side on the quilt. Each tube was a simple telescope with a paper ring at the end. Alone, they were fine. Together, they could become a way for two friends to look at the same moment. He bound them with a clean striped sock, snug but not crushed, so the tubes sat like a pair of eyes. "A friendship telescope," he said. Grandma, who was his friend as well as his grandma, clapped her soft clap. The window lamp lit the stripes. Hot milk waited in two matching cups. Crickets tuned up in the garden. The moon was already at the balcony, as if it had saved them seats.

"Friends can look in the same direction," Grandma said. Idris nodded. He wanted the night to be a shared picture, not two separate peeks with a long wait between. They carried the twin tubes outside, each person holding one end of the sock so the bundle would not twist. Slippers padded the cool boards. Paper stars tapped the window behind them. Idris felt the good weight of an idea that needed another person to finish its job. He could have looked alone. He did not want to.

They rested the tubes on the rail. "Left tube for me, right for you," Idris said. "On three, we look at the moon." They counted, then looked. Both of them made the same small oh sound, and then they laughed because the oh had arrived together. "Flour patches," Idris said. "Yes," Grandma said, "in the same places." Friendship, he thought, was this echo, the joy of not being the only one who saw. They shifted a thumb's width and found the Big Dipper. Idris named the bowl. Grandma named the handle. The spoon belonged to both sentences.

Back near the door they drank hot milk with the twin tubes across their knees. Idris stuck a glow-in-the-dark sticker on each tube, matching dots, so neither friend would have a plainer tool. He smoothed them with equal care. "No favorite tube," he said. Grandma agreed. They taped a tiny paper star to the sock, a badge for the bundle. Idris told her that when his pal from the yellow house visited another night, the second tube would be ready, and tonight Grandma was the pal. She looked delighted. "I accept the post."

They played a looking game. One person chose a gentle target, the moon, a bright star, or the paper star reflected faintly in the glass, and both looked without saying what it was. Then each described one clue. If the clues matched, they had shared a view. Idris chose the moon. Grandma said, "Round and calm." Idris said, "Bright enough to make the rail shine." Match. Grandma chose a cricket sound instead of a sight, which bent the rules in a friendly way. They both pointed toward the mint pot. Match again. Idris liked that friendship could stretch a game without snapping it.

The air grew cooler. They moved the picnic of cups and tubes indoors onto the quilt. Idris wiped a drip from the sock so the stripes would not stay sticky. Grandma rinsed nothing yet. They still had a sip left, and ending together mattered. Idris said, "Thank you for looking when I looked." Grandma said, "Thank you for building a way for us to be side by side." The crickets kept a rhythm under their words. The room felt knitted, like the sock had shared its job with the whole evening.

Idris set the friendship telescope in the basket by the door, sock bow up, so tomorrow no one would have to search. He did not hide a tube under his bed for only himself. A shared tool should live in a shared place. He put his slippers next to Grandma's house shoes, a little pair and a bigger pair, which made him smile. Paper stars rested on the glass. The moon kept its seat above the balcony. Idris felt rich in company, which was a wealth made of minutes, not things.

He yawned, and Grandma yawned, and they called it the last matched look of the night. The cups went to the sink together, one wash and one dry. Idris touched the striped sock once more, proud of a simple binding that had turned two tubes into a friendship. Sleep could come now. The sky would still be there to share.

Idris untied the striped sock, checked that neither tube had been scratched, and tied the bow again with equal loops. "A fair bow for a fair share," he said. Grandma held the knot while he pulled gently. They each took one more look at the same bright star and said one word. Idris said, "Steady." Grandma said, "Steady," too, by happy accident. The match made them grin. He pressed a tiny paper star onto the sock so their binding would look like it belonged with the window's decorations. Friendship, he decided, was a knot that held without squeezing. The crickets finished a phrase together, or so it seemed, and Idris let that be the night's last lesson before the cups were dried.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the twin cardboard tubes and their striped sock. Idris climbed under the quilt with Grandma's goodnight close by. The crickets carried a duet through the garden, and the moon shone for more than one pair of eyes.
