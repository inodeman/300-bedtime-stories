Goldie peeled one round glow-in-the-dark sticker from the sheet and held it near the ceiling. "Not yet," she told herself. "A picture needs a plan." The window lamp lit the room in warm gold. Her bedtime slippers were tucked under the chair. Hot milk sent up a sleepy curl of steam. On the balcony, crickets played their tiny fiddles, and the moon looked like a blank page that did not mind other art. Goldie spread the quilt on the rug so her stickers would not stick to the wrong thing.

Grandpa sat with a cardboard tube telescope across his knees. "Will you copy the Big Dipper?" he asked. Goldie shook her head, smiling. "I want to invent one. The sky can keep its spoon. Our ceiling can have a teacup." She pressed five small stickers in a curve for the cup and one more for a handle. Then she added three tiny dots above it. "Those are steam," she said. "The teacup is full of hot milk for anyone who looks up." Grandpa clapped once, very softly, the way a person claps for a new idea.

Goldie stepped back. In the lamp brightness the stickers looked pale. "They are saving their glow," Grandpa reminded her. "Creativity can start before the shining part." Goldie liked that. She was not finished anyway. She cut paper stars from yellow scraps, not to copy the sticker cup, but to give it friends. One paper star became a spoon. Another became a little plate. She taped them in a cluster on the window, low enough for her hands and high enough to meet the moon's gaze. The tape roll hummed as it spun.

She told the story while she worked, because a picture felt lonely without words. "Tonight the teacup is new," she said. "The crickets come to the rim to sing thank you, and the moon lends a round saucer of light for free." Grandpa nodded. "That telling is yours, and it fits this room." Goldie felt the idea settle into place like a sticker pressed flat. She added a tiny paper cricket to the window, no bigger than her thumbnail, so the story would have its singer in view.

They dimmed the lamp a little, not all the way, just enough for the stickers to begin their gentle green shine. The teacup appeared on the ceiling as if it had been waiting in secret. Goldie gasped a happy gasp. "It works." She lay on the quilt and pointed so Grandpa could see the handle. He looked through the cardboard tube at the real moon, then back at the glowing cup. "Two kinds of wonder," he said. "One we visit. One we make." Goldie decided both could live in the same bedtime.

She still wanted a name that sounded official and kind. "The Teacup," she announced, "and the little stars are the Steam Sisters, even if they are stickers and not sisters at all." Grandpa wrote the name on a card and propped it on the sill. Goldie added a drawing of slippers beside the cup, because cozy things belonged in her sky. She sipped the real hot milk and laughed. "Now I am drinking what the ceiling is pretending to hold." A cricket answered from the balcony, right on time, as if it liked the joke.

Goldie checked the paper stars at the window and moved the spoon star a thumb's width so it would not cover the moon. Making art, she saw, included knowing when to leave space. She gave Grandpa the last sticker and invited him to add a crumb, a single dot, to the saucer. He placed it carefully. "A shared constellation is still your idea," he said. "You opened the sky on the ceiling." Goldie tucked the empty sticker sheet into a notebook so she could plan another shape another night, maybe a mitten, maybe a mint leaf.

She put the telescope back in its corner and folded the quilt only halfway, because she wanted to lie under the Teacup a minute more. The room smelled like milk and paper. Her heart felt fizzed with making, the good kind of fizz that settles into sleep. Creativity had turned leftover stickers and scraps into a place her family could point to. She whispered the name once, proudly, and let the green glow be the night-light of her own design.

Goldie carried the cardboard tube onto the balcony and looked at the real stars for a polite minute, then came back to her glowing cup. "I am not replacing you," she told the sky. "I am keeping you company from indoors." The night air cooled her cheeks. A cricket answered from the garden, and she decided the Steam Sisters had heard. She pressed the tube into Grandpa's hands so he could have a long look while she finished her milk. Making something new had not used up the old wonder. There was enough quiet beauty for a handmade sky and a true one, side by side, with the quilt waiting when her toes grew cool.

The lantern by the window glowed softly under the new paper spoon and plate. Above Goldie, the sticker teacup held its pretend steam. The moon kept its own bright job outside, and the crickets played on. Goldie closed her eyes beneath a sky she had helped to build, and the house felt full of fresh ideas.
