Poppy sat on the front step with her knees under her chin and her lantern unlit beside her. The small town was beginning its evening festival of lights, but not all at once. Down the street, a single paper lantern woke in a window, peach and steady. Then the next house answered. Then the next. The lights arrived the way a soft song arrives, note after note, with rests between. Poppy's mouth opened a little. She did not want to miss the order.

"It is a parade that never hurries," Grandad said. He had a matchbox in his pocket and no wish to use it early. The air smelled like cool stone and somebody's cinnamon buns. A fiddle far away played long notes that seemed to wait for the lights to catch up. Poppy counted houses. "We are number six if they start at the corner," she said. Grandad nodded. "Then our match has a when, not only a how." Poppy liked a when. It made the waiting feel musical. She tapped the rhythm very softly on her knee, one tap for each house that had not yet glowed, and the taps grew fewer as the light approached.

The second lantern bloomed above a blue gate. A face smiled and withdrew. The third was lower, on a step, and its shade had a painted fish. Poppy whispered the colors: peach, gold, fish-silver. She did not shout them. Wonder, she thought, could be quiet and still be large. A beetle crossed the step. She let it. Her fingers rested on her own shade, cream paper with a pressed sprig of thyme inside. The thyme smelled green when she leaned close. Grandad pointed out how each new light changed the color of the cobbles, first a hint, then a small path, then a warm ribbon that stopped just before their step.

Miss Rowan passed with a basket of plain biscuits for the neighbors who would walk later. "Are you the counter?" she asked, seeing Poppy's eyes on the sequence. "I am the listener of lights," Poppy said, which made Miss Rowan smile. She left two biscuits on a napkin. They were crumbly and mild. Poppy ate one and saved one for the moment after their own lighting, a small celebration that would not interrupt the count. Crumbs stayed on the napkin, not on the matchbox.

The fourth and fifth lights came close together, cousins in brightness. Poppy's chest felt like a drum played with a fingertip. "Now," Grandad said, and he did not rush the word. He struck the match. It flared, settled, and met the candle. Poppy lowered the shade only when the flame was calm. Thyme glowed, a secret garden in paper. Their light joined the line. She looked back. The first lantern was still faithful. She looked ahead. The seventh house saw them and answered a moment later. The song continued down the lane, calm and bright, all the way toward the far bright bakery window.

They did not leave the step right away. Poppy wanted to see the lights reach the bakery and turn the corner. Each new glow was a soft yes. A walker went by with a lantern of her own, and for a breath two lights stood side by side and made a warmer color on the stones. "They visit," Poppy said. "Then they go home along the street." Grandad hummed the fiddle's long note. It fit. Poppy hummed a shorter one, the way a single match fit inside a long evening.

When the line of lights had passed beyond sight, Poppy ate the second biscuit. It tasted like a period at the end of a gentle sentence. She and Grandad walked the street slowly, not to start anything, only to greet what was already glowing. They did not blow anyone's candle. They only nodded. At the bakery window, buns shone under a lantern, sugar sparkling. Poppy pressed nothing against the glass. She let the sparkle be. A spoon clinked once inside the bakery, and even that small sound seemed to wait its turn, the way the lights had waited theirs.

Back on the step, their own lantern still held its thyme. Poppy traced the sprig's shadow with her eyes. "The street learned a song," she said. Grandad said the street had known it for years and was glad of a new listener. They pinched the outdoor candle out with care when the walkers had all gone by. Smoke made one sweet curl.

Between the fifth house and theirs, Poppy closed her eyes for one breath to hear the fiddle without seeing the lights. When she opened them, nothing had skipped her. The street had kept her place. "It waits for eyes," she said. Grandad said good songs wait for ears too. A moth tested their unlit shade and left when it found no moon there yet. Poppy thanked it anyway, in her mind. The biscuit crumb on her lip was sweet. She brushed it away so the match would not meet sugar. Readiness, she decided, was a kind of wonder.

Walking back, she noticed the lights did not match perfectly, and the mismatch was pretty. One candle sat higher. One shade was wrinkled at the corner and still glowed honestly. "Perfect is not the pattern," Poppy said. "Coming next is the pattern." Grandad squeezed her shoulder through her sweater. The wool was warm from sitting. A window showed cups being dried, each cup catching a little peach from outside. Poppy waved at the cups, then felt shy and waved at the person instead. The person waved back with a towel. It was a small duet at the end of the long line.

Inside, Poppy told the order of colors to the kettle while Grandad smiled. She left the thyme shade on the table where morning could admire the pressed green. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and Poppy watched it as the latest note in a song the whole street had sung together.
