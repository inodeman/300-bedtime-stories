Mateo held his paper lantern up to the porch light and watched the moon he had painted. The moon was yellow, and the river under it was blue, with one small silver fish that he had added at the end. The paint still shone, wet as a raindrop on a leaf. Uncle Rafa set a cup of water nearby and a cloth that was only for wiping brushes, not sleeves. Down the street, neighbors were beginning to gather for the town lantern walk. Their voices stayed low. A soft drum tried a heartbeat rhythm and then grew even softer, as if it remembered the evening.

"I can see the fish," Uncle Rafa said. "The walk will pass our gate when the bakery clock reaches the gentle hour. That is not this minute." Mateo turned the lantern. A bead of blue wanted to slide toward the moon. He tipped the shade back just in time. His chest felt hurry, the good kind that comes when music starts. He also felt the bead of paint, which was a different kind of message. "If I go now, the river will smear," he said. Uncle Rafa nodded. "And your sleeve will wear a little ocean. The path will still be there when the paper is ready."

They sat on the top step. The wood was warm from the afternoon and cool at the edges. Mateo rested the lantern on an upturned crate so nothing touched the painted side. He counted his breaths the way Miss Rowan, the art teacher, had taught the class: in like smelling soup, out like blowing a dandelion without scattering every seed. The yellow moon lost its sharp shine and became soft. The blue stayed in its banks. Mateo wanted to touch it, to be sure. He put his hands under his knees instead. "Not yet," he told his fingers. Uncle Rafa peeled an orange. The scent jumped into the air, bright and friendly.

"Half?" Uncle Rafa asked. Mateo nodded. The slice was juicy and a little tart. Pulp threads clung to his thumb. He wiped them on his napkin and checked the lantern again with his eyes only. A neighbor passed with a basket of rolls, and the rolls smelled like butter. "Are you coming, Mateo?" the neighbor called, kindly, not rushing. "When the paint agrees," Mateo called back. The neighbor laughed a small laugh. "That is a good captain for a lantern." The drum down the street kept its slow heart. Mateo tapped the same rhythm on his knee, quietly, so the crate would not wobble.

Mateo watched a beetle cross the step and did not poke it. The beetle had its own slow errand, feelers tasting the air. "Even a beetle can teach a festival," Uncle Rafa said. Mateo smiled and checked the moon again. The yellow had settled into the paper as if it planned to stay. He folded his hands and let the orange scent fade to a quieter sweetness on his fingers.

Miss Rowan came along the sidewalk with her own lantern, plain cream paper with a pressed fern inside. She paused at the gate. "I know that moon," she said. "It was a puddle of shine last time I looked." Mateo pointed at the crate. "It is learning to be dry. I am waiting with it." Miss Rowan did not tell him to hurry. She looked closely, the way artists look, and smiled. "The fish has a clean edge. You are giving it time to become itself." She left a sprig of rosemary on the step. "For your pocket. It smells like patience to me, green and steady." Mateo tucked it carefully, away from the wet paper.

The bakery clock finally spoke with one mellow chime. Mateo looked at the moon. He hovered a clean finger near the yellow, not quite touching. The air felt dry against his skin. He touched the very corner of the paper, where there was no paint, and the corner was firm. Then, with Uncle Rafa watching, he touched the blue river with the pad of one finger. It came away clean. "Ready," Mateo whispered, as if a loud word might still smudge the evening. Uncle Rafa lit the candle cup with a long match, the flame small and respectful. Warm light rose into the moon. The fish seemed to flicker, awake but calm.

They joined the walk near the corner, not at the front and not in a rush. People moved like a slow river themselves. Lanterns bobbed at the height of smiles. Mateo held his handle with two hands when the sidewalk dipped, then with one when the path was level. A child-sized breeze, no stronger than a sigh, tested the paper and moved on. Under the footbridge, the flute's notes gathered and sounded rounder. Mateo listened and watched his painted river at the same time. The colors stayed where he had put them. "I can see your fish from here," Miss Rowan said beside him. "It did not wash away."

At the square, tables held apple slices, cheese, and little cups of warm barley tea. Mateo did not set his lantern down near the cups. He hung it on a designated hook where the pictures could be seen and the flames stayed apart. Uncle Rafa brought him tea that was more warm than hot. The cup toasted his palms. "Was the waiting long?" Uncle Rafa asked. Mateo thought about the orange, the rosemary, and the bead of blue that never fell. "It was long enough," he said. "The moon is still a moon." He sipped. The tea tasted toasted and gentle. Someone played the drum so softly that the apples did not tremble.

They walked home past gardens that had gone sleepy. Mateo's shoulder was a little tired in a proud way, the way a shoulder feels after carrying something carefully. On the porch he blew the candle out with Uncle Rafa's help, a pinch of the snuffer, not a gust that might bend the paper. The moon went dark, but the paint remained, yellow and blue and silver, ready for another evening. Inside, he set the lantern on the shelf away from the curtains. The lantern by the window glowed softly, a different lantern, the house one, and Mateo watched both lights and felt glad that he had let the paint finish its quiet work.
