Theo stayed on the bench when the last planned song ended. The small town festival had been a soft one: lanterns, shared bread, a flute, a drum that never tried to lead the heart too fast. Now Miss Pell lowered her flute and let the final note thin into the square. People did not rush. They smiled, gathered cups, and spoke in kitchen voices. Theo's hands rested on his knees. He felt the strange, pleasant wideness that comes when music steps aside and the world is still there.

"Can we listen to the after?" he asked Nana. She nodded at once. "The after is part of the evening. It just does not wear a title." They sat. Theo closed his eyes halfway, enough to help his ears, not enough to miss the lanterns. He heard a spoon touch a bowl, a polite clink. He heard shoes on stone, unhurried. He heard a cricket start, stop, and start again as if deciding the square was safe for its thin bright string. The bread he had eaten earlier left a faint salt on his lip. He tasted it and let the taste be part of the listening too.

Miss Pell did not pack up at once. She sat on her stool and breathed, and the breath across the flute's mouthpiece made one accidental note, softer than a whisper. She laughed quietly. "The flute wanted a last word," she said. Theo opened his eyes. "I think the square answered." He pointed with his chin at the paper lantern nearest them. Its shade ticked, a tiny tick, as the warm air inside met the cooler air outside. Tick. Then the cricket. Then a cup set down. "They are taking turns," he said. Nana's face showed the kind of pride that does not interrupt.

A neighbor carried a tray of leftover apple slices to the bench. "For listeners," she said. Theo took one. It was crisp and cold and smelled like the orchard bin. The crunch was loud only to him, so he chewed more slowly and heard the cricket slip back in between bites. Miss Pell accepted a slice and ate it in small moons. "When I was playing, I could not hear the paper," she said. "Thank you for staying. You found the second concert." Theo had not known he was finding a concert. The idea made his chest feel wide and calm.

He named the instruments under his breath: spoon, shoe, cricket, lantern paper, flute breath, apple crunch. Nana added the fountain at the far end, which was only a trickle tonight, a silver thread of sound. Theo listened until he could separate the trickle from the cricket. They were cousins, not twins. "The fountain is the long note," he said. "The cricket is the bright one." Miss Pell tapped her flute once, not a song, just agreement. The tap went into the list too. Theo laughed, then put the laugh on the list, because a laugh could be gentle enough to belong.

Walkers passed with lanterns, and their lights changed the look of the bench without changing the sounds much. One lantern creaked at its handle. Creak, pause, creak. Theo wondered how many small noises a town made when it was content. He did not need a number. He needed the feeling of noticing. The apple slice finished in a sweet bite. His fingers were sticky. Nana had a handkerchief that smelled like clean cotton. He wiped his hands and was careful not to cover his ears while he did it. Even wiping had a soft rasp worth hearing once.

They stayed until the cups were all in the washing tub and the tub's water gave a low slosh. Miss Pell slid her flute into its case, the clasps clicking like tiny polite doors. "I will remember the paper tick," she said. "Tomorrow's song might leave a window for it." Theo liked the thought of a window in a song. He and Nana stood. Their knees felt the cool of having been still. The square was not empty. It was simply finished with being a stage. The fountain kept its long note as they turned toward the lane.

Before they left the bench, Theo checked his list by holding up fingers. Spoon, shoe, cricket, paper, breath, crunch, fountain, tap. Eight, and the laugh made nine. Nana said some wonders are countable and still not used up. He wiggled the ninth finger by borrowing one of hers, and they both smiled. The fountain's thread of sound went on without needing their count. A moth crossed the lantern light and did not land. Theo let the moth be uncounted on purpose, a guest who did not have to sign anything.

Miss Pell walked with them as far as the corner. Her case bumped her knee softly, a muffled beat. "Do you hear that?" Theo asked. "I do now," she said. "Cases are instruments when the evening is this attentive." They paused to let a neighbor's lantern go by. The passing light warmed their faces and then gave them back to the dimmer lane. Theo smelled wax and apples and the iron of the corner post. He touched the post. It was cool. The coolness felt like another kind of note, one his skin could hear.

Near home, he stopped once just to be sure the cricket by the square was too far to follow, and a nearer cricket had taken up the job. The new cricket was slower. "They hand the song along," he said. Nana said towns are good at that. Theo nodded, satisfied, and matched his last steps to the slower chirp without trying to catch it. Wonder, he thought, was not a chase. It was a seat you kept saving, even after the flute had found its case.

On the way home Theo tried not to fill the air. He let their steps be the rhythm. A gate latch said one word. A leaf skated on stone and stopped. Nana hummed nothing, which Theo appreciated. "The after came with us," he said. She answered, "It always does, if you save it a seat." Their house window was already glowing, a square of quiet gold. He carried the handkerchief, sticky side folded in, like a small flag of the apple concert. Inside, Nana set the handkerchief to soak in cool water. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and Theo stood beside it, still saving a seat for the after, which had walked all the way home.
