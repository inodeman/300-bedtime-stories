A small brass bell sat in Oscar's palm, cool and quiet. Neri held a short loop of soft cord. They stood at their auntie's garden gate, and the wood was warm from the sun. Bees worked in the lavender. A hose clicked and sighed somewhere behind the tall rosemary. They had knocked twice, gently, and nobody had come. Not because anyone was ignoring them. Auntie was kneeling in the back bed, where leaf sounds covered little knocks.

"She told Papa she misses visitors when she is elbow-deep in soil," Neri said. "A bell could be a kind knock that travels." Oscar lifted the bell and it almost chimed, then did not, because a scrap of cotton was still tucked around the clapper from the walk. They had wrapped it so it would not ring in the lane and surprise the napping cat next door. The cotton smelled like the drawer at home. Oscar's idea was good. The bell's job was waiting inside the wrap, patient and muffled.

Their auntie's shed had a hook too high for them, so they chose the gate itself, at a height Oscar could reach without stretching onto his toes. Papa had said he would tie the final knot. He came down the lane with a paper bag that crackled. "Lemons," he said. "And I see a plan." Neri showed him the cord. "We want a hello, not a surprise," she said. Papa smiled. "Then the bell should ring when you mean it, and rest when you do not. I will knot it so it stays put. You keep your fingers on the gate, not in the loop."

They held the gate steady. It gave a tiny wooden creak. Papa tied the cord, tested it with a tug, and stepped back. "Your turn," he said. Oscar pulled the cord the way he had practiced in his mind. The bell stayed almost silent. Only a dull tuk came out. Neri frowned, not angry, just curious. She leaned close and saw the cotton still sleeping around the little clapper. "We forgot its coat," she whispered. The bees did not mind. A lavender stem brushed her wrist and left a sweet green smell.

Oscar eased the cotton out while Papa watched, so no finger slipped into a tight space. The cloth came free, soft and square. Neri put it in her pocket to take home. "Now," Oscar said. He rang the bell with a small flick. The sound was round and bright, one clear note that flew over the rosemary and into the back bed. It was not loud enough to startle. It was loud enough to be a hello. They waited. Soil-dusted footsteps came along the path, and their auntie appeared with a smear of earth on her cheek and a smile already started.

"That note found me," she said. "What a kind invention." She opened the gate. The garden air was cooler and full of leaves. She poured lemonade from a pitcher beaded with water. The cups were cold against their fingers. The drink was sharp and sweet, with a seed that Neri fished out and set on the saucer like a tiny boat. Oscar told about the cotton coat, and how a hello can hide inside a good idea. Their auntie laughed, a warm sound among the leaves. "Even bells need their scarves off when it is time to speak," she said. She let them ring it once more, just to hear the note sit among the bees without bothering them.

They helped carry a light basket of cut mint to the gate, nothing heavy, stems cool and fragrant. Neri held the gate so it would not swing into anyone's knees. Oscar coiled the extra cord into a neat circle and hung it on a low nail Papa said was safe. Before they left, their auntie touched the bell, not ringing it, just thanking it. "Come again," she said. "I will listen for you." The lane outside felt ordinary and golden, but the gate had a new voice, small and welcoming. A lavender stem nodded as the gate closed, and the note seemed to stay in the leaves.

On the way home Neri kept the cotton square folded, a reminder that a good idea can wear the wrong coat until someone looks closely. Oscar swung his arms and hummed the bell's note under his breath. Papa walked between them, the empty lemon bag folded flat. The rosemary smell followed them for three houses and then let them go. Kindness, Neri thought, could be a sound you give to someone else.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when they set the cotton square on the table at home. Oscar tapped a spoon against a cup, trying to remember the bell, and the spoon was too sharp, so he stopped and just smiled at the quieter idea. Neri looked toward the lane, where a single bright note still seemed to hang in the warm air, ready for the next hello. The cotton square lay folded, clean and done with its job.
