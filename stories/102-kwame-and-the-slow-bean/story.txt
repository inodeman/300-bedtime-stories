Kwame rested his chin on his hands and stared at the glass jar on the science table. Inside, a bean lay on a wet paper towel like a little boat that had not decided to sail. The sunny classroom smelled like damp cotton and pencil shavings. Next to his jar, Ruth's bean had already pushed out a pale hook, curved and steady. Kwame's jar only showed a brown seed with a shiny spot where he had rubbed it that morning, hoping the rub would hurry it. The window made the water gleam. He wanted a sprout before story time, and the seed did not seem to care about clocks.

"Yours is still sleeping," Ruth said in a warm voice. She set her jar down so the two glasses clinked a tiny song. "Mine cracked in the night. I heard nothing. It just decided." Kwame sighed through his nose, a soft sound. "I checked before snack and after snack," he said. "I even whispered grow, please. Maybe it did not hear me through the glass." Ruth tapped the side of his jar with one finger. The glass felt cool. "Seeds do not use ears," she said. "They use time. The picture book says the root starts in the dark, where we cannot clap for it."

Kwame pulled the library book closer. The page showed a bean cut open, with a map of a tiny plant folded inside like a secret coat. He liked the drawing of soil crumbs, each one round as a bit of corn bread. "So something might be happening, and my eyes are too early?" he asked. Ruth nodded. "That is the quiet part of patience. The quiet part still counts." She showed him how the paper towel in his jar was still evenly wet, not drippy and not dry. "You already did the helping," she said. "Now the seed does its own work."

Kwame wanted to shake the jar, just a little, to wake the bean. His hands hovered. Then he remembered a music class day when shaking too soon turned a song into noise. He put his palms flat on the table instead. The wood was smooth from many elbows. "I will wait until after the rug story," he said. "If I shake it, I might break a root I cannot see." Ruth smiled. "We can check the towel at lunch and leave the bean alone. Waiting can be a kind of careful."

They read the page together, voices low. The book rustled. A line said roots drink before leaves wave. Kwame traced the arrow with his finger, not pressing hard enough to dent the paper. Outside the window, a tree held still bright leaves, and the light moved slowly across the science table as the sun crossed the sky. Paint cups on the far shelf made a row of quiet colors. Nothing in the room rushed, except Kwame's thoughts, and even those began to walk instead of run.

At lunch he did not grab the jar. He ate apple slices that tasted sharp and sweet, and he told his feet to stay under the bench. When he came back, he and Ruth looked without lifting the seed. The brown coat had a paler line, thin as a thread of chalk. "Oh," Kwame whispered. "It heard the time." Ruth whispered back, "It heard the water." They grinned at the almost-sprout and did not poke it. The line was small, but it was real, and real was enough for one day.

Later, during quiet choosing, Kwame drew the jar in his notebook, including the tiny line, so he would remember the hour it appeared. He added a small sun in the corner instead of a hurry mark. Ruth drew her longer hook beside it. They did not ask who was faster. They labeled both pictures growing. The pencil smelled faintly of cedar. Kwame's shoulders, which had been up near his ears all morning, dropped down where shoulders belong.

The next morning he walked in and went straight to the science table, but he made himself stand a breath away before looking. The pale line had become a small root, creamy and curved, nosing into the wet towel. A bump hinted that a stem might follow. Kwame felt a laugh bubble up and kept it gentle so he would not fog the glass. "You took the slow road," he told the bean, "and the slow road worked." Ruth bumped his shoulder with hers, friendly and light. "We can turn the jar when the leaves want the window," she said. "Not before."

Kwame nodded. He placed the picture book open to the root page, like a friend sitting beside the jars. He noticed other seeds on the table, some speckled, some round, all on their own clocks. He did not line them up from fastest to slowest. He only made sure each towel was damp and each jar sat out of the bumping path near the paint cups. Patience, he thought, was a kind of watering you did with your waiting. He wrote a little sign that said, "Roots are busy even when we see only seed."

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the science table, and its light rested on Kwame's jar like a quiet hand. The new root shone pale against the paper towel. Kwame sat on the edge of his chair, hands in his lap, and listened to the room settle. He could wait for the first leaf. The seed had shown him how.
