The rolls on the bakery rack looked like a row of little moons. They were soft, warm, and almost the same. Benji held one and turned it, searching for a mark that would tell it from the next. Ayo held another and sniffed the cinnamon in the air. Bea set a bowl of orange peels on the shared table, each peel cut into slim curls that shone with oil.

"If they all look alike," Benji said, "how will the market stall show which roll is which story?"

Ayo tilted a head. "Rolls do not have to be stories."

"They could be," Bea said. "We have peels and cinnamon. Those are like pencils you can smell." Her eyes were bright with the idea. Creativity fizzed in the room the way yeast fizzes in dough, quiet and busy.

They did not cut into the rolls or hide them. They asked the baker's rack if a few moons could wear pictures, and the rack, being a rack, said nothing, which they took as a friendly yes because there were dozens more plain rolls behind these. Plenty stayed simple for anyone who liked simple. The picture rolls would be a cheerful extra.

Benji pressed a peel curl into a spiral that looked like a sun. The oil left a bright dot. Ayo dusted cinnamon in a soft leaf shape, tapping the spoon so the powder would not storm. Bea made a tiny wave along one side, three peels in a row like water. They worked slowly. A rush would smear the sun into a blob.

"The sun looks like a snail," Benji said, laughing at his own first try.

"Then let it be a snail," Ayo said. "Or wait, lift the peel, and give the sun another chance."

Benji waited. He warmed the peel between his fingers until it bent without cracking. The second sun sat round and proud. He grinned. "Patience helped my picture."

They made more. A little door. A honey drop drawn with a real drop so small it shone. A cinnamon stick that was a cinnamon stick, laid like a log beside a peel campfire that did not burn anything, because it was only a picture of coziness. The bread ovens hummed behind them. Soup pots bubbled a calm beat. Saturday light from the market side turned the peels into stained glass.

When six rolls wore pictures, they carried them in a wide basket to the stall just outside. The stall cloth was their own, set with honey jars and a stack of recipe cards. Neighbors drifted close, drawn by the smell before the shapes.

"What is the wave for?" asked a child voice from behind a basket, and Bea answered without needing a new name in the story of her friends. She said, "The wave is orange. The leaf is cinnamon. The sun is the Saturday we are having."

People chose with their eyes and then with their hands. Some chose plain moons. Some chose pictures. Nobody had to guess and go without. The basket stayed generous. Benji watched a sun roll leave in a picnic cloth and felt proud in a sharing way, not a keeping way.

Back at the shared table they tasted one leaf roll together, breaking it into three. The cinnamon leaf had melted into a sweet shadow. The crumb was airy. Ayo licked a sticky finger. "The picture became flavor," Ayo said. "That is a good trick."

Bea wiped the table and saved the leftover curls in a jar so they could scent the next dough. "We can draw again next week," she said. "New pictures. Same rolls. Same plenty."

Benji sketched the sun on a recipe card so they would remember the press-and-wait method. He wrote, Warm the peel. Press gently. If it smears, laugh and try once more. Share the pretty ones and the plain ones.

The market noise swelled and softened. Inside, the ovens ticked. The three friends stacked the empty baking tray and did not mind the crumbs. Crumbs meant the pictures had been delicious, not only looked at.

On the shared table one pictured roll still waited, the one where all three pictures touched. The sun leaned on the leaf. The wave curled under both. Benji had worried the shapes would smear, and Ayo had said, "Let them be neighbors." Bea had pressed the last peel with the pad of her finger and waited a breath before she let go. The picture held. Cinnamon dust made a soft path between the shapes, like a lane in the market. Window light gilded the orange. Every plain roll still on the rack looked content to be plain. The pictured one looked content to be fancy in a homemade way. Nobody had to pick a side forever. A bite could hold a sun and a crumb at the same time.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the recipe card and the last peel curl. Benji, Ayo, and Bea sat shoulder to shoulder, cinnamon on their wrists and orange on their thumbs. The rolls had started as matching moons. Now the morning held suns, leaves, and waves, and the shared table smelled like a story you could eat.
