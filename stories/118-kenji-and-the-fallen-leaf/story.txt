A fallen leaf waited on the library step, brown at the edges and still green near the stem, like a note the tree had finished writing. Kenji picked it up with two fingers and held it to the light. The veins made a shining map. Faye stood beside him with a sheet of thin paper and a fat piece of green chalk. The sunny air smelled like warm bark and the stone step. They had come outside between picture books to learn the tree without taking anything it still needed. Kenji's rule, said out loud so he would keep it, was simple. "Fallen leaves are gifts. Leaves still on twigs stay with the tree."

Faye nodded and touched the bark with her palm. It was rough and cool in the grooves, warmer on the ridges. "We can make a rubbing," she said. "The paper feels the tree, and the tree keeps its skin." Kenji liked that. He held the paper steady against the trunk while Faye rubbed the chalk sideways, not point-first, so it would not poke. A pattern rose on the page, dark and pale, the same grooves he felt under his other hand. The chalk smell was dry and friendly. "It looks like a quiet river," he said. Faye tilted her head. "It looks like the lines on the seed jars, those pale roots." They both smiled at the rhyme the world was making between tree and jar.

Kenji set the fallen leaf on the paper and rubbed it too, gently, until its shape appeared beside the bark. A tiny piece of the leaf crumbled, which was what fallen leaves are for, becoming soil. He brushed the crumbs onto the ground at the tree's feet. "Back you go," he told them. "You can feed the roots." Faye did not laugh at him for talking to crumbs. She added, "Thank you for the map." Talking kindly to the world was a way of remembering it was not only scenery. They looked up. The leaves still on the branches clicked softly against one another, a sound like the patient shakers, only the tree had invented it first. Kenji closed his eyes for three breaths and just listened. When he opened them, the light looked greener.

They carried the rubbings inside and laid them next to the science jars on the library window shelf. A sprout in one jar had two small leaves now, smoother than the tree's leaf, newer. Kenji held the rubbing beside the jar, not touching the glass to the chalk. "Same idea, different day," he said. "Veins carry what the plant needs." Faye read a sentence from a picture book that said leaves breathe in their own slow way. They practiced a slow breath together, in and out, not to copy a leaf exactly, but to feel calm enough to notice one. The rug fuzz tickled Kenji's ankle. He did not mind. Noticing had room for tickles.

A question rose. Could they see the veins better with a drop of water on the fallen leaf? The leaf was still outside. They went back, dipped a finger in the water cup they used for paint rinsing, and let one drop fall on the leaf's center. The green darkened and the veins turned brighter, a trick of wet and light. "Just one drop," Faye said. "We are visiting, not soaking." Kenji agreed. They watched until the drop shrank. Then he placed the leaf under the bush where the soil was soft, not in a pocket, not in a book where it would stain a page. His hands smelled like green and chalk. He wiped them on the grass, then thought better of wiping on the grass, and used his own smock.

Inside again, they labeled the rubbing so the learning would stay kind and clear. "Bark from the library tree. Leaf that had already fallen. Both put back in their ways." Kenji wrote slowly. Faye drew a tiny jar next to a tiny tree with an arrow that said "cousins." They taped the paper to the science board in the school hallway later, high enough to see and low enough for friends. Kenji told Ada and anyone near, but he did not need a crowd. He told Faye, who already knew, and that retelling helped him keep the rule in his mouth. "We did not peel the tree. We listened and we rubbed."

He watered the sprouts with a spoon, one spoon each, because nature care was not only about the big tree. The water darkened the paper towel. The stems did not need a speech, but he gave them a quiet one anyway. "Grow on your clock." Faye capped the chalk and set it in the cup so it would not roll away and snap. The day felt round and complete, like a leaf shape. Kenji's chest was quiet in the good way, the way a garden is quiet when no one is grabbing.

They stood at the window and matched one real leaf outside to the rubbing inside. The match was close, not perfect, and close was how living things are. Kenji pressed his palm to the cool glass and then to his own chest, feeling the same hand that had been gentle. Faye hummed a leaf-rubbing song with no words, just a soft tone. The tree answered by doing nothing sudden. That was its kindness, and theirs was leaving it whole.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the rubbing and the jars, picking out the chalk rivers and the pale new leaves. Kenji and Faye watched both kinds of green, the drawn and the living, and went to wash their hands, already careful of how they would touch the world next.
