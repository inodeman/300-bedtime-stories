The school fair tables made a bright path across the sunny courtyard, with posters drying, seed jars shining, and the welcome banner lifting in a slow breath of air. Abena stood by the puppet stage they had set near the steps. Her job was to tell friends that the little show would begin when the shaker rang three soft times. She knew the sentence. She had practiced it to a paint cup, and the paint cup had not blinked. Real friends were different. Their faces were kind, and still her voice felt like it had gone to sit inside a pocket. Tariq stood beside her with the shaker, patient as a fence post that had learned to smile.

"I can say it," Abena whispered. "I just need my voice to come back from its pocket." Tariq nodded. "We can give it a path. First you tell me. Then you tell the banner, which is not a person and will not surprise you. Then you tell the courtyard." The idea was so practical that Abena's shoulders dropped a finger's width. She faced Tariq. "The puppet garden will begin after three soft shakes," she said. Her words were clear at this small distance. Tariq's eyes were encouraging without being loud. "That was the whole job," he said. "The courtyard only needs that same sentence. It does not need a bigger you. It needs a steady you."

Abena turned toward the banner. WELCOME FRIENDS rippled. She told the cloth the sentence. A breeze lifted her hair and cooled her forehead, which felt nice, like the sentence had made room for air. She held the edge of the stage. The wood was warm and a little dusty. Courage, she decided, was not a drum. It was a hand on warm wood and a sentence that stayed till its period. "Will you stand where I can see the shaker?" she asked. Tariq moved to the side, not in front of her. "I will be the starting sound, and you will be the meaning," he said. "People need both."

Friends began to gather because gatherings happen when tables smell like oranges and fresh paint. Abena felt the hop in her stomach again. She looked at Tariq's shaker, blue and quiet. He lifted his eyebrows, a question. She nodded. He shook once, twice, three times, each shake with a rest, the patient pattern from music class. The sound was a polite bell made of beans. Faces turned, friendly, not staring in a heavy way. Abena put her hand on the wood and let the sentence out. "The puppet garden will begin after three soft shakes." She heard herself. The sentence had arrived whole. Someone near the seed table smiled and stepped closer. The hop in her stomach sat down and became a glow.

She had one more line, the one about where to sit. For a blink she wanted to hide inside the first success and not risk a second sentence. Then she remembered the paint cup, how the second practice had been easier, and she remembered Tariq saying steady, not bigger. "There is room on the rug and room on the steps," she added. "If you sit, the small puppets can see you too." A soft laugh moved through the courtyard, the good kind, the kind that makes space. Children settled. Skirts and knees and crossed ankles made a colorful border. Abena stayed standing until everyone who wanted a seat had one. She did not rush them. Courage had time inside it.

The show began, other friends moving the puppets, and Abena sat at the end of the rug where she could still see Tariq. Her heart was fast and happy, like a shaker used correctly. She watched the mouse puppet bow and thought, I used my voice in the sun, and the sun did not swallow it. When the show paused between scenes, a small child looked unsure about where to put a cup of water. Abena pointed to a safe spot on the low wall and said, "It can rest there," and the words came without a pocket detour. Tariq caught her eye and tapped the shaker once, a private applause.

After the bows, people thanked the puppets and also thanked Abena, not in a noisy pile, but in passing smiles. "I heard you," said Salma, carrying her poster. "I knew where to sit." That thank-you felt better than a trumpet. Abena drank water that tasted like the cool inside of the school. Her throat was fine. She had not needed to become someone else. Tariq offered her the shaker. "Do you want to make the ending sound?" he asked. She did. Three soft shakes, rests included, and then she said, "Thank you for being a gentle audience." The courtyard seemed to glow a shade warmer.

She helped stack the extra mats. The work of her hands calmed the last fizz in her arms. "Next time I will start with the banner if I need to," she told Tariq. "And then I will look at a friend's face, and then at the whole sun." He nodded. "That path will be there whenever you want it." They tied the shaker's lid tight so the beans would sleep. The fair smelled like cut oranges and dry paint and warm stone. Abena breathed it in until her chest felt wide enough for the next hello.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the classroom that opened onto the courtyard. Abena saw it and lifted her chin. Her voice had a home in that light and in the open air, and she had used it, steady and kind, exactly when the fair needed a beginning.
