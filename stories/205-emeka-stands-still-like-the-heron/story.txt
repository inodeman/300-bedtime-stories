Emeka arrived at the pond with bounce still in his shoes. He had a smooth stone in his pocket and a head full of ideas. The heron was already there, gray and tall, standing on one leg in the thin water near the reeds. The heron did not splash. The heron did not perform. The heron watched the light move, patient as a clock that knows it does not need to tick out loud.

"Hello, heron!" Emeka called, friendly and a bit too big. "Do a step. Do a twirl. Show me something." The heron did not twirl. One eye regarded Emeka from the side, calm and unbothered. Emeka's shoulders sank. He tossed the idea of the stone away, back into his pocket, because the pond was not a place for throwing. He wanted something to happen right now. The water only made rings from a drifting seed.

The grown-up rested a hand on the railing of the little viewing spot. "The heron is not slow to annoy you," the grown-up said. "The heron is practicing a kind of attention. You can borrow it. Feet quiet. Voice small. Eyes busy." Emeka folded his arms, then unfolded them. He planted his feet. He counted three breaths. The first breath felt like waiting for a turn. The second felt like listening. The third felt like the day getting wider.

A yellow leaf arrived from somewhere upstream in the meadow ditch. It turned once, became a tiny boat, and passed the heron's reflection without touching a single feather. Emeka had not known he wanted to see that. A ring of ripples opened where a bug touched the surface and went on its way. The heron did not lunge. The heron simply continued being a heron, which was the whole show if you were patient enough to call it a show.

Emeka's nose itched. He did not shout about it. He wrinkled and stayed. A dragonfly hovered, blue as a piece of sky that had learned to steer, then landed on a reed far from his fingers. He did not reach. He watched the wings finish their last shiver. "I see you," he whispered, to the dragonfly, the leaf, and the bird. His legs had a message about sitting down. He listened to the message and sat on the flat rock, which was another kind of stillness.

Time passed in pond minutes, which are softer than kitchen minutes. The heron shifted at last, a careful change from one leg to the other, like someone turning a page. Then the long neck moved, and the head turned toward Emeka. It was not a bow. It was a greeting from a neighbor who appreciates quiet guests. Emeka felt the greeting land. He lifted one hand halfway and let it rest. "I can wait," he said, and this time the words were true.

He stayed until the leaf boat disappeared around the bend. He could tell the story of it without inventing a splash. On the walk home he noticed more because his hurry had been set down: a web with dew still in it, a chicken feather caught kindly on a thistle, the way the pony's ears made a V when the pony dozed. Patience had followed him off the rock.

Emeka tried the heron way on smaller things during the walk home. He waited while a snail crossed one warm stone, even though he could have stepped over and forgotten it. He waited while Button the dog decided whether to stretch or stay curled. He did not say hurry to either of them. The snail arrived. Button chose to stretch, long and pleased. Emeka felt a smile that was quieter than his usual smile. Patience, he realized, was a kindness you could carry in your knees and in your mouth, not only a trick for ponds.

He also noticed what he did not do. He did not poke the water to make it perform. He did not clap to make the heron hop. The heron had offered a small greeting, and Emeka had not spent it like a coin that had to buy a show. At home he showed the grown-up how long he could stand on one foot. It was not very long. They both laughed. "The heron has had more practice," the grown-up said. Emeka nodded. "I will borrow a little more tomorrow, and I will not borrow the bird's whole afternoon."

He placed the smooth stone back in the garden path where other stones lived, because he had not needed it to make a splash. "The pond already had ripples," he told Ursula when she asked why his pocket looked lighter. She said she was practicing quiet visits too. They stood at the window together and did not tap the glass. Somewhere beyond the meadow the heron would be folding the day into the same patience he had lent them, one calm moment at a time.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as Emeka set his unused stone on the sill. "I did not need to make the pond exciting," he told the grown-up. "It was already full." The heron, far behind them, kept the afternoon in good order, one leg, then the other, a patient friend who had taught a bouncy boy how to stand still and see.
