Quentin dipped a soft brush into blue water and painted a round moon on thick paper. The circle wobbled and then became sure of itself. He smiled. "This is the Lampmoon," he said. "It borrows a little gold from our window lamp so the crickets will not think the sky forgot them." Creativity fizzed in his fingers. His bedtime slippers tapped a happy pattern. Hot milk stood nearby, far enough from the paints. A quilt covered the table's edge to catch drips. Outside, the real moon glowed above the balcony, and Quentin did not mind that there were two.

Grandma leaned in. "Will your moon be a copy?" Quentin shook his head. "A neighbor. Copies try to win. Neighbors sit beside." He added pale dots for paper-star cousins and a tiny balcony rail at the bottom, just a line. He painted a slipper in the corner because cozy things belonged in the picture. The brush made a whispering sound. Crickets answered from the garden, which Quentin took as encouragement, not as a demand to be perfect.

When the paint was dry enough, he taped the page inside the window, low and to the left of the real moon's view. From the rug, a person could see art moon and sky moon in one look. Quentin clapped for himself in a whisper. He cut three paper stars and taped them around the painting so the Lampmoon would have company it did not have to share with the real sky. "You keep your stars," he told the night through the glass. "These are the indoor kind." Grandma said the manners of that sentence were excellent.

They dimmed nothing yet. First they looked through the cardboard telescope at the true moon, so the painting would not replace the visit. Quentin saw the bright face and the soft gray marks. He came back to his page and added one gentle gray wash, not to compete, but to say he had been paying attention. "Art can listen," he said. Grandma nodded. "That is one of its jobs." He stuck a glow-in-the-dark sticker in the painted moon's cheek. Later, the sticker would give the Lampmoon a bedtime freckle of green.

Quentin told the story of his picture while they sipped hot milk. "The Lampmoon rolls along the window to keep the crickets company. It does not leave the house. It likes the quilt and the slippers. When the real moon is a thin smile, the Lampmoon still stays round, because paint can choose kindness over matching." Grandma repeated the last line, pleased. They hung a small card under the painting with the name written clearly. Quentin sounded the name out. It felt official and homemade, which was his favorite mixture.

He checked the tape so it would not peel onto the glass in a sticky scribble. One piece needed a smoother thumb. He fixed it. Making included mending the small bothers. Then he invited Grandma to add a dot, any dot, so the work could hold a second person's joy. She added a silver-gray cricket near the rail. Quentin grinned. "Now the music is in the paint." They opened the balcony door and listened, comparing the painted cricket with the living song. Both were welcome. Neither was frightened or frightening. The night was simply full.

Before bed Quentin rinsed the brush until the water ran clear and set the paints where they could not touch the quilt. He put the telescope away. He left the Lampmoon up. Art, he thought, was a way of adding light without taking any from the sky. His slippers went under the chair. His cup went to the sink. He stood between the painted moon and the real one and felt like a bridge, small and proud. The crickets played the last lively bit of their evening.

He told Grandma he might paint a spoon of stars another night, or a cup, or a mint leaf. Ideas lined up politely. He did not have to use them all before sleep. Creativity could save a place in tomorrow. He yawned, blue at the thumbnail, and wiped the blue onto a rag with a laugh.

Quentin made a second, smaller Lampmoon on a scrap, a practice moon, and he did not hide the wobbly one. He taped it inside the cardboard tube like a surprise picture at the end of a look. "If you peer in by day, you will find my moon waiting," he told Grandma. She tried it and laughed with real delight. Creativity, Quentin saw, could leave gifts in unexpected pockets, not only on the main window. He added a paper star beside the scrap so the tube's little gallery would feel finished. The crickets sang while the paint smell faded into the cleaner smell of hot milk. He washed the blue from his thumbnail until it was only a faint memory of the color he had loved using.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the taped Lampmoon, so the painted circle seemed to borrow real gold. Quentin slid under the quilt and watched both moons until his eyes grew heavy. The crickets kept the house and the garden in the same song, and his idea glowed among them without needing to be the only light.
