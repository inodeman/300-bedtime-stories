The school garden smelled like damp straw and tomato vines. Ollie stood with the lettuce seedlings in a tray, their leaves pale green and eager. Priya held the seed packet they had already used weeks ago, reading the back for fun. "Full sun," she said, mostly to herself. "That means the plant likes a bright chair." Ms. Ferris was helping another group tie peas. The lettuce group had chosen the corner by the shed because the soil there was soft and the shed wall felt like a cozy back. It was also in shade for most of the day. Ollie looked at the sunny row in the middle, which was empty and golden and a little harder to dig. His mouth felt full of a sentence that wanted a turn.

He had learned about sun last spring, when his own patio lettuce stretched tall and floppy in a dim spot, then perked up after his mother moved the pot. He did not want these school leaves to stretch and flop. He also did not want to sound like a boss. Priya was already marking holes with a stick, neat holes, good holes, in the wrong light. "Those are even spaces," Ollie said, because that part was true and kind. Priya grinned. "I measured with my shoe." The shade from the shed lay over her shoe like a cool apron. Ollie shifted the tray. A leaf tickled his wrist. He decided the sentence could be kind and still be clear.

"Priya," he said, "I think the lettuce needs the sunny row. The packet says full sun. This corner is a good nap spot for ferns, not a bright chair for these." His voice came out steady. He pointed with his chin so he would not point with a bossy finger. Priya stopped the stick. She looked at the packet, then at the shade, then at the bright middle. "Oh," she said. "I picked soft soil and forgot the light." She did not look crumpled. She looked interested. Another child, Ben, said, "But we already marked holes." Ollie nodded. "We can mark new ones. The sunny soil is soft if we loosen it. I will help dig." Offering his hands made the sentence feel like a bridge, not a wall.

Ms. Ferris came over because she had heard her name in the way groups get quieter when a plan changes. Ollie told her simply. "We were by the shed. Lettuce wants sun. I said so." Ms. Ferris touched the packet. "Ollie is right. Thank you for saying it before the roots went in." She did not take the tray away from him. She let the group move itself. They carried the seedlings to the bright row. The soil there was warmer under Ollie's fingers, crumbly and smelling like clean earth after a shower. They loosened it with hand forks. Priya measured again with her shoe. "Same even spaces," she said. "New address."

Ollie set the first seedling in, holding the stem gently, pressing soil around it so the plant stood as if it had chosen the spot. Water from the sprinkling can made dark stars. In the sun the drops shone and then sank, which was what water was for. Ben found a worm and moved it to the side bed with two sticks, a careful ride, so it would not be cut by a fork. "Even worms get a warning," Ben said. Ollie laughed. The garden felt friendly again, the kind of friendly that can include a correction. His chest, which had been tight as a seed coat, loosened. Speaking up had not knocked anything over. It had turned a tray toward the light.

They planted the whole row. Priya made a label: LETTUCE, SUNNY ON PURPOSE. Ollie liked that she wrote it. The courage had been his sentence. The better plan belonged to everyone who listened. Ms. Ferris gave them a minute to stand and see the line of leaves in the brightness. "Plants cannot walk to a better chair," she said. "Sometimes a person has to notice and speak." Ollie wiped a mud smile onto his apron by accident and then on purpose, a small badge. He promised himself he would also listen when someone else had the clearer view. Courage in one direction and ears in the other.

At tidy time they rinsed the forks. The shady corner did not stay empty. Ms. Ferris said mint could go there later, because mint liked a cooler foot. Priya was already curious. "Then my holes can be mint holes," she said. Ollie nodded. "Your measuring was never the problem." They walked back along the fence. Playground voices rose and fell like birds. Ollie carried the empty tray, light as a cracker. He felt the good tiredness of kneeling in sun. A ladybug rode the tray rim until the door, then flew back to the vines, a speck of red deciding for itself.

In the classroom he washed the sunny soil from his hands and watched it spiral away. He could still smell leaves on his cuffs. He told the story only when Mr. Adeyemi, passing in the hall, asked why the garden group was grinning. "We moved the lettuce to the bright chair," Ollie said. "I said the true thing, and Priya made a new label." That was the whole tale, and it was enough. No one needed a louder version.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the garden shed that evening, a small light the helper left for late waterers, and Ollie thought of it while he set his shoes by his own door. He was glad he had not swallowed his sentence. A clear voice had given the lettuce the sunny row, and the row would answer, slowly, with leaves that did not have to stretch for what they needed.
