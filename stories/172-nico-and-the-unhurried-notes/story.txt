Nico's recorder was the color of pale wood and smelled faintly of the cloth he kept it in. He stood on the porch while the town got ready for the evening festival, lanterns appearing in windows like polite stars. He wanted to play a four-note tune at the music corner: low, high, middle, home. When he rushed, the notes bumped into one another and sounded like a spoon in a jar. When he waited, they made a path. Mama sat on the step with a bowl of orange slices and did not tap her foot.

"Again, but with room between," Mama said. Nico put the recorder to his lip, tasted the cool mouthpiece, and blew gently. Low. He let the sound finish. High. The high note squeaked, and he stopped without frustration spilling over. "That squeak is a sign I hurried the air," he said. Mama nodded. "Air can be patient too." He tried the high note alone, smaller breath, and it came out round. A neighbor passing with a basket of rolls lifted a hand in greeting but did not ask him to perform. Nico was glad. Practice was a porch thing first.

He played the four notes with a silent count between them. One, two. The count was not music, but it made music possible. His fingers learned the count. The orange slice he ate at the pause was bright and cold. Juice touched the corner of his mouth, and he wiped it before it could reach the finger holes. "Sticky notes are not a kind of song," he said, and Mama laughed softly. Down the lane, a drum found a slow pulse. Nico did not chase the pulse. He let it pass like a cart that was not his ride yet.

At the music corner, chairs made a loose ring near the bakery. Mr. Ibarra, who guided the songs, welcomed Nico with a nod. "We have a quiet space after the flute," he said. "You may play your four notes twice, slowly. If you want to wait for another evening, the space will forgive you." Nico felt the offer of waiting and also the readiness in his fingers. "I will play the small path," he said. "Not more than I can hold." Mr. Ibarra looked pleased. "That is a musician's good sense."

Nico sat with the recorder across his knees while other sounds took their turn. A thumb harp plinked. A voice hummed without words. He noticed his shoulders rising and lowered them. Mama stood at the edge, not signaling, only present. The bakery air smelled like butter and warm sugar. When a tray passed, Nico shook his head so his hands would stay clean. He could eat after. Waiting to eat was a cousin of waiting between notes. He smiled at that thought and kept it.

His turn came without a spotlight. Mr. Ibarra simply tilted a hand. Nico lifted the recorder. Low, rest, high, rest, middle, rest, home. The high note stayed round. He did it again, the same size, not louder to prove the first time had worked. The drum, far from chasing him, rested with him. When he lowered the recorder, the quiet that followed was friendly. Someone said, "I could walk to that." Nico felt the words land softly. He had not filled the whole evening. He had laid four stones in a stream, and they were enough to step on.

Mr. Ibarra asked if he would play once more at the end, as people gathered their lanterns. Nico almost said yes to a longer tune. Then he remembered the squeak and the silent count. "The same four," he said. "I want them still to be a path." "Good," Mr. Ibarra answered. They shared a small buttered roll while waiting, Nico holding it in a napkin. The crust whispered. He wiped his fingers twice. When the ending came, the four notes were even kinder, because his breath knew the way. Lanterns bobbed as if agreeing.

Afterward he walked with Mama through the square, where cups of mint water sat in a row. He took one sip. The mint was cool and made the recorder taste a memory. He did not play on the walk home. The night already had its tune. He told Mama which count had saved the high note. She said counts were invisible friends. A cat on a windowsill blinked in time, or maybe not, and Nico decided wonder could share a porch with patience.

Nico listened to the flute player breathe before each phrase. The breath was a tiny door. He copied it without the recorder, just air across his lip, and the air felt smooth. "You are practicing the quiet part," Mama whispered. "That is half the tune." A moth circled a lantern and chose another path. Nico let it go. He pressed his fingertips together to keep them warm and ready. When he lifted the recorder for the ending, the wood felt friendly, not slippery. The four notes knew his patience and came out dressed for a walk, not for a race.

On the way through the square a woman asked if the tune had a name. Nico thought. "Not yet," he said. "It is only a path of four stones." She smiled and offered him a slice of pear for later, wrapped in paper. He tucked it into Mama's basket so his hands would stay free and clean. The pear smelled like late sun. He decided the tune could be called Stones, but he did not need to announce it. Some names are for porches. He hummed the rests as silent steps all the way to their lane, and the humming did not squeak at all.

He washed the mouthpiece with cool water and dried the holes with the soft cloth. The recorder went back into its sleeve, snug and unhurried. Nico set it on the shelf where morning would find it. He hummed the four notes once, silently, only in his head, with the rests still there. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and he watched the glow the way he had watched each note, letting it finish before he turned toward bed.
