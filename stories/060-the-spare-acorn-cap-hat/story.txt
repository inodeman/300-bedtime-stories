Joost turned a large acorn cap in his hands at the table of his mushroom cottage, lining it with the softest moss he could find. The cap was nearly as wide as his palm, a little hat waiting for ears that were not his. His fox friend visited when the fireflies woke, always a bit later than the first gold blink, and always a bit cooler at the tips of those tall ears. Joost had noticed the fox tucking them against the breeze. "A spare hat," he said, "kept on the sill, so you never have to ask, and I never have to forget." He stitched the moss in with a grass thread so it would not wander off during a happy shake.

He set the finished acorn-cap hat on the windowsill where the light could find it, then set out two oak-leaf plates and a small loaf of berry bread. Friendship, he thought, was often a place set before the knock. He unfolded his paper map and marked the cottage with a tiny hat shape, a private joke that still felt true. Outside, the oaks were warm from the day, and ferns along the path made a green aisle. Joost swept the step so paws would meet a welcome instead of crumbs from lunch. He practiced sitting quietly. Some friends arrived more easily when the room was not bouncing with impatience.

The first fireflies lifted from the ferns, little lanterns that needed no oil. Joost did not go out to fetch the fox. He stayed where he had promised to be. Soon a rustle, a pause, and the familiar gentle face appeared at the round door, ears high, eyes soft. "You are right on your own time," Joost said. "Come in. There is bread, and there is something for the breeze." The fox stepped inside, shook a leaf from its tail, and sat on the mat as if it had been set there just for this.

Joost offered the hat on his open hands. The fox sniffed the moss, then lowered its head. The cap settled between the ears, a little crooked, entirely charming. Joost adjusted it with one finger. "If you dislike it, we can let it be a bowl instead," he said. The fox stood, walked to the window, and studied its faint reflection in the glass. Its tail went up. It bumped Joost's knee, a yes that filled the cottage. They shared the berry bread, the fox taking tidy bites, Joost remembering to leave the warmer end toward his guest. The hat stayed on through the whole meal, only slipping once, and the fox nudged it back without help, proud of the trick.

After bread they sat by the open window and watched the fireflies write slow letters over the moss. Joost told a small story about the rope footbridge, how the ropes were there to touch if a breeze got playful, the way friends are there too. The fox listened, hat slightly tilted, the picture of comfort. Joost showed the paper map and the silly hat mark. "This means come as you are, and also come as you prefer to be, ears warm," he said. The fox put a paw on the mark, careful of the ink, sealing the joke into a plan.

They took a short dusk walk to the picnic clearing so the new hat could have an outing. Other gentle foxes resting in the clearing glanced over with friendly curiosity. Joost did not boast. He only said, "We made it together, one of us stitching, one of us willing to try it on." His friend walked a circle, modeling, then sat as if the show was over and the bread had been the real glory. Joost laughed and agreed. On the way back they passed the hollow tree library and did not go in. Not every good thing needed an audience.

At the cottage again, Joost poured water into acorn-cap cups much smaller than the hat. They drank. He asked, "Shall I keep this on the sill for you between visits?" The fox touched the sill, then touched Joost's sleeve, a double yes that meant the place and the person both mattered. Joost felt that answer settle warmly behind his ribs. Rituals, he decided, were friendships you could see, sitting in the light even when the friend was off among the oaks.

He walked the fox to the fern aisle and stopped where their paths usually parted. The hat stayed on. "If the moss flattens, bring it back and I will fluff it," Joost said. The fox gave a soft huff, almost a note, and disappeared under the leaves with the acorn cap riding like a small moon. Joost remained until the rustle faded, pleased by a goodbye that promised a next time. The step behind him was clean. The plates were waiting to be washed. The evening felt complete without being finished.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the empty place where the spare hat usually sat, a space that meant the hat was out doing its job. Joost washed the leaf plates and left one cup upside down for morning. Fireflies still stitched the ferns with gold. He rested his chin on his hands and smiled at the map's tiny hat. Friendship, he thought, was a moss lining sewn before anyone asked, a warmer end of the bread turned outward, and a sill that kept a promise between one visit and the next.
