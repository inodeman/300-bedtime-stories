The afternoon lay over the gardens like a warm blanket that had not been shaken out. Asha's lettuce stood up straight, already watered, each leaf holding a bright bead. Beyond the low stone wall, Mr. Okonkwo's seedling trays told a different story. The little tomato plants leaned. Their leaves had lost the perk of morning. He had gone to the market with his straw hat and a list, and he had said, yesterday and again at the gate, "If the sun bears down and I am not back, the dented can is yours to use on my row. Better a drink from a neighbor than a wilt from waiting." Asha remembered the exact kindness of that permission. She did not have to guess.

She climbed the two flat stones that made a stile, the way they had agreed, so no plants were stepped on. The dented watering can sat by his rain bucket. The dent was old and gentle, a thumb-print in the metal from some long-ago bump, and it did not leak. Asha filled the can halfway so it would not be too heavy for a careful pour. The water smelled cool, like stone and moss. A bee worked the clover on her side of the wall and left the seedlings alone. "I am only bringing a drink," Asha told the tomatoes. "Mr. Okonkwo would do the same for my lettuce. He did, last month, when my hands were full of pea strings."

She tilted the spout close to the soil, not over the leaves, so the sun would not turn drops into little magnifiers. Water darkened the compost in rings. The seedlings did not stand up all at once. Plants are not flags. They take a slow breath. Asha did not hurry them with a finger. She moved from pot to pot, counting under her breath, eight tomato babies and four basil sprigs that also looked thirsty. Basil sent up a peppery smell when the water touched the soil near it, and Asha smiled. "You may smell strong," she said, "and still need gentleness." A ladybug crossed a label that read PLUM in neat pencil. She waited until it reached the rim before she poured the next cup.

Her own arms grew warm. She set the can down and shook them out. From the lane came the rattle of a market cart, not yet Mr. Okonkwo's. Asha looked at a seedling that still drooped more than the others. The soil under it was dusty in the middle, where her pour had rolled off a slope. She pressed the compost lightly with two fingers to make a little bowl, then poured again. This time the water stayed. "There," she said. "A cup that fits." Kindness, she thought, was not only remembering to help. It was noticing the place the first help had missed. She wiped a mud speck from the label so PLUM could still be read.

When the can was empty, the trays gleamed dark and the air above them felt softer. Asha refilled it only a little and gave her own lettuce a second small sip, so neither garden would feel forgotten. Then she rinsed the spout, because basil flecks had stuck, and set the can back exactly where the dent faced the path, the way Mr. Okonkwo kept it. She washed her hands at the outdoor tap. The water was sun-warm in the pipe at first, then cool. She dried them on her skirt and sat on the wall, feet on her side, eyes on his, a bridge of a girl.

Mr. Okonkwo came along the lane with a full sack and a lighter step when he saw the dark soil. "Asha," he said, "the tomatoes look as if they have heard good news." She hopped down to open the gate for him so he would not bump the sack. "They were leaning," she said. "I made a bowl in the dry one's compost. The ladybug had the right of way." He laughed, a round sound like a wooden bowl set on a table. "That is masterful watering." He gave her a smooth plum from the sack, not the seedling kind, a real fruit, purple and dusty. "Payment in sweetness, if your family allows." Asha nodded. "They do, for plums and for helping."

She bit the plum on her own step. Juice ran, and she caught it with her wrist, then licked the sweetness, which tasted like the color of evening. The seedling leaves, when she glanced back, had begun to lift. Not all the way. Enough to show the drink had been welcome. Mr. Okonkwo was already singing low to the basil as he set out market greens. The song had no rush in it. Asha saved the plum pit in a cup to plant someday, even if it took years. Waiting was another kind of care. Today the care had been water, carried across a wall at the right hour.

She swept a few spilled crumbs of compost off the stile so the next walk would be clean. The gardens smelled green and watered, a smell that makes shoulders drop. Her lettuce clicked faintly as leaves brushed. Somewhere a window opened and a curtain lifted in the breeze she had helped the plants enjoy. Asha put the plum pit cup on the shelf by the seed envelopes. She had not needed a new tool. The dented can had been enough, and remembering to use it had been the whole of the job.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as the heat of the day finally loosened its hold. Asha stood with clean hands and a purple-sweet mouth, looking from her lettuce to the seedlings beyond the stones. Both gardens were darker, happier soil. She had crossed a wall, poured slowly, and left the can where its owner could find it. Kindness had fit in a dented spout, and the whole afternoon felt gentler because a neighbor's plants had not been left to wait alone.
