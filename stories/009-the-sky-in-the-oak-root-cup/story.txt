Wren walked the creek path with Nana Brook, their shoes quiet on the needle carpet. The woods smelled like wet bark and sweet resin. Light arrived in pieces, and the creek made a small silver comment beside them, never quite the same twice. Wren liked finding things that were not hidden on purpose: a striped feather, a stone with a ring, a leaf shaped like a mitten. Today the finding was a sound first, a drip that fell into somewhere round. Wren followed it off the path by three careful steps, to the base of an oak. Between two roots sat a hollow, full of last night's rain, still as a held breath.

"Nana, a cup," Wren said. The water was clear enough to show a brown leaf at the bottom and, on its face, a piece of sky. Cloud and blue and a crossing branch all lived in that small circle. When Wren leaned, their own eye appeared too, wide and pleased, then they leaned back so the sky could return. A drop fell from a twig and the picture broke into rings, then mended itself. Wren's hands hovered. The hollow was the size of a soup bowl. "I could tip it into my bottle," Wren said. "Then I could show the sky-cup at home." The bottle on their belt was empty and clean. It would be easy. The water looked like a treasure that might vanish if they left it.

Nana Brook crouched, knees ticking, and did not reach either. "Look at the rim," she said. Wren looked. Green moss made a soft lip around the cup, and in the moss a tiny garden of star-shaped leaves drank the splash. A beetle with a polished back walked the edge and did not fall. The bark was dark and damp and smelled like tea. "If we pour the cup out," Nana said, "the sky picture goes, and the moss loses its sip. If we leave it, the woods keep a mirror." Wren felt the wish to carry and the wish to protect sit side by side. Wonder tugged both ways. They set the bottle down unopened.

They stayed. Wren counted the rings from the next drip: one, two, three, and a fourth that was only a shiver. A gnat danced and did not land. From the side, the cup was just brown water. From above, it was a window. Wren tried seeing it both ways, tilting their head, and laughed when the sky slid. "It is a trick that is also true," they said. Nana smiled. "Most good wonders are." A chickadee called its name nearby, fee-bee in a tiny whistle, and Wren whistled back badly on purpose, then better. The bird did not come to their hand. It did not need to. It was complete in the leaves, the way the cup was complete in the root.

Wren took a pencil from a pocket and a fold of paper. They did not take water. They drew a circle, a branch, a cloud like a pillow, and the moss as soft scribbles. The drawing was not the cup, and that was all right. It was a way of carrying without emptying. Their fingers smelled of lead and leaf. When a breeze shuffled the canopy, the cup's sky changed to a new cloud, and Wren added a second small cloud in the margin. "Two skies," they said. "Both visited. Neither is in my bottle." Nana passed them a raspberry from a tin, bright and seedy. Wren ate it and let the taste be another kind of color.

Farther along the creek they found flat stones and skipped none, because the water there was a skating place for real water-striders. Wren watched the striders dent the surface without falling through. "Like the sky sits on the cup," Wren said. The sentence pleased them. They were learning the shape of the day: look, name, leave, remember. On the way back the oak was still there. The cup still held water. A new leaf had spun down and become a boat. Wren did not fish it out. The beetle was gone, which felt like a story continuing without them, and they found they liked being a visitor more than a collector.

At the trail's end the light was wider. Wren held the paper drawing up and compared it to memory. The real cup had more shine. Memory had more feeling. Together they were enough to tell at the table. Nana said, "You may visit it after the next rain. It might be a cup again, or it might be a dry socket full of leaves. Both are the oak's choice." Wren nodded. Choosing not to take had made the wonder larger, not smaller. Their bottle was still empty and ready for drinking water at home, which was a better job for a bottle than stealing a mirror.

They walked the lane with pine scent in their cuffs. Wren told the drawing, in a whisper, that it had done a good job. A neighbor waved from a porch. The creek sound thinned behind them until it was only something they knew. Wren's chest felt light, the way it feels when your hands are empty for a good reason.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as Wren set the drawing on the sill to flatten under a smooth stone. They did not pour anything into a bottle from the woods. They washed their hands, drank cool tap water, and looked at the paper sky they had kept. The real cup was still between the oak roots, holding cloud and branch for the next eyes, or for no eyes at all. Wonder, Wren decided, could stay outdoors and still come home inside a person, warm as a lantern and gentle as moss.
