Leila's basket of corn cakes rode against her hip, wrapped in a striped towel. The cakes were golden, still faintly warm, with crisp edges and soft middles. Baba had flipped them in butter and a little honey. The small town harvest supper spread across the green in calm clusters, not in shouting rows. Lanterns sat on low stools. A fiddle tried a tune and then let a singer hum it even more quietly. Leila smelled onion, pepper, and the sweet dust of cornmeal on her own sleeve.

Mrs. Nguyen's stew pot stood on a sturdy trivet two blankets away. The stew was thick with carrots and beans, and the spoon stood up for a moment before it leaned. A plate beside the pot had held bread. Now the plate held crumbs and a card that said, "Bread, all shared." Mrs. Nguyen looked at the empty plate and then at the patient people with bowls. She did not frown hard. She only tapped the card as if hoping bread might return. Leila looked at her towel. She looked at Baba. "We have cakes," she said. "They could sit beside the stew."

"That is a true share," Baba said. "Not only beside our own blanket, where our friends already know us." Leila carried half the cakes on a tin plate, walking slowly so the stack would not slide. The grass was cool through her shoes. "Mrs. Nguyen," she said, "would corn cakes help until more bread comes? They are a little sweet. They like stew." Mrs. Nguyen's face opened. "They would help beautifully. We will tell people there is honey in them, so anyone who wants plain can choose the other dish." Leila liked the honesty. She set the plate where hands could reach, and Baba wrote a new card in clear letters.

People tasted in small pairings. A cake torn in half became a scoop for beans. Steam from the stew met the honey smell and made something new. Leila stayed to explain, because sharing was also explaining. "The middle is soft," she told Mr. Ellis. "The edge is crisp." He tried a corner and then a middle and nodded. "Together they are a good conversation," he said. Leila kept one eye on the stack so it would not tip, and one eye on the line so no one was skipped. When a hand hesitated, she said, "There is enough for a small piece."

Leila counted the cakes on the visiting plate without saying the number aloud. There were eight. She tore two into halves so more people could taste without emptying the stack. Honey shone on the torn edges. "Halves are still a welcome," she told a shy hand that had been waiting. The person took a half and a spoon of stew and sighed in a happy, quiet way. Leila kept the card facing outward. When someone asked if the cakes were very sweet, she said, "A little. The stew can hold the sweetness, or you can enjoy the stew alone." Choice, Baba had taught her, was part of a real offer.

Back on their own blanket, the remaining cakes waited with pickled peppers and a jug of water. Leila did not hoard the prettiest cake. She took a cracked one and left the smooth tops for guests. Baba ate a cracked one too. "The cracks taste the same," he said, which was true and also funny. The fiddle started a slower air. Leila hummed and passed cups. A neighbor from the next blanket brought sliced cucumbers as a thank you, cool and wet. "Your cakes went traveling," the neighbor said. "They looked happy over there." Leila felt the basket's lightness as a good report.

Mrs. Nguyen came over during a pause in serving, wiping her hands on a clean apron. "The stew lasted better because people had something to hold it," she said. "Will you take a bowl?" Leila had hoped, and she had not asked first. She accepted a small bowl. The carrots were sweet, the beans soft, the broth warm with pepper that did not sting. She dipped a bit of cracked cake. Honey and pepper surprised each other and then became friends. "Oh," Leila said. Baba laughed quietly. "That is the sound of a recipe shaking hands."

They sat until the green's lanterns were the brightest things in the grass. Leila collected her tin plate, empty and shining with a little oil. She washed it at the water station, rubbing with a soft brush until the corn smell loosened. Mrs. Nguyen washed the stew ladle beside her. Their elbows did not bump. "Next time I will bring extra bread and you will bring cakes, and we will plan the handshake," Mrs. Nguyen said. Leila agreed. Planning a share, she thought, was another way to share.

A late neighbor arrived with a basket of plain rolls, still warm, and laughed softly to see the bread problem already solved by cakes. "Then these rolls can be tomorrow's breakfast for the hall workers," Mrs. Nguyen said. Leila helped tuck the rolls into a clean cloth so they would not dry out. The cloth smelled like flour. Her fingers brushed a crust and came away dusty. She clapped the dust over the grass, a tiny pale cloud that settled at once. "You shared before you knew help was coming," the neighbor said. Leila felt that was the right order. Sharing early had kept the supper gentle. Waiting for a perfect fix might have left the stew standing alone.

On the walk home the towel was folded small. Leila told Baba about the honey and the pepper. He said the town green was a table even without wood. The kitchen at home smelled faintly of the cakes that had not traveled, two of them saved for breakfast. Leila did not eat them now. She covered them. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and she pictured the empty bread card being turned over at last, because the evening had found another way to feed the line.
