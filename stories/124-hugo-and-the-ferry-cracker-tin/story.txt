The ferry smelled like rope, oranges, and a little salt. Hugo stepped onto the deck with Nana and felt the boards give a gentle answer under his shoes. The horn sang one low note, not loud enough to startle, just enough to say they were leaving the dock. Water rubbed the side of the boat in soft pats. Lanterns along the harbor made long gold pencils on the waves. "We have the crackers for the crossing," Nana said, patting the tin in Hugo's hands. The tin was warm from the kitchen and painted with a blue fish.

They sat on a bench by the rail where the breeze could find them but not steal a hat. Hugo opened the tin a crack. The crackers were square, pale, and dotted with sesame. They smelled like butter and toast. He took one and let it rest on his tongue until it softened. Across the aisle a child about his size held a red tin shaped like a little house. The child tipped it, hoping for a stack, and only crumbs came out, sliding into a napkin in a sandy pile. "Oh," the child said, very quietly. "They broke on the walk. I shook them too much."

Hugo looked at his own neat squares. His first thought was to close the lid and keep the crossing just as he had planned, ten crackers and the blue fish all to himself. His second thought was slower and better. The child's napkin looked sad, and the ride was long enough for hungry to grow. Nana caught his glance and did not decide for him. She only raised her eyebrows in a kind question. Hugo scooted the tin forward on his knees. "We can share," he said. "These are strong. They did not get shaken." The child blinked, then smiled with relief that showed in the shoulders.

"I am Jun," the child said. "I was going to count lights and eat one cracker for each light. Now the lights would have nothing." Hugo laughed a small laugh. "We can still count. We will just use both tins, even if one is mostly crumbs." He placed four crackers into Jun's palm and kept four, leaving two for Nana, who took one and waved the other back into the tin. The sharing was not a speech. It was a simple passing of squares. Jun's cracker crunched. "Sesame," Jun said, pleased. "Mine were cinnamon, but cinnamon already left."

They counted harbor lights the way Jun had hoped. One for the green lamp on the dock, two for the window of a boathouse, three for a string of bulbs over a quiet pier. At ten, Hugo's pile was smaller and his chest felt larger. The ferry slid past a bell buoy that rang only when the water asked. Gulls rode the air without hurrying. Nana told them the name of a low island that looked like a sleeping cat. Hugo repeated it so he would not forget. Crumbs stayed in napkins. Nobody's snack was a problem anymore. It was a plan they had made together.

Jun offered the red house tin as a cup for the last two sesame squares, a shared bowl between the benches. "So we do not forget they are for both," Jun said. Hugo liked that. They each took one when the ferry turned and the breeze brought a cooler smell of deep water. The blue fish on Hugo's lid watched with a painted eye. "Tomorrow I will pack extra," Hugo said. "Extra is a good friend to have in a tin." Jun nodded. "I will pack a towel so crumbs have a soft place, and I will not shake the house." They grinned at the lesson, which felt light enough to float.

When the far dock grew from a smudge into a real place, they stood and held the rail. The horn sang its low note again. People gathered bags. Hugo pressed the lid on tight and then, after a thought, opened it and tipped the last cracker dust into Jun's napkin. "For the walk up the hill," he said. Jun folded the napkin like a present. "For both of us if you are walking too." They were not going the same way, so Hugo shook his head, smiling. "You take the dust. I have the story." Nana's hand found his, warm and proud without making a fuss.

On the walk from their own landing, Hugo told Nana he had almost shut the tin. "Almost is honest," Nana said. "Opening it was the sharing." The evening air smelled like bread from a shop near the road. Hugo's stomach was content, not stuffed, which felt like the right size for a ferry. He swung the empty-enough tin and heard one crumb tick inside, a tiny clock. He thought of lights counted by twos and of a red house tin that had been sad and then useful.

At home he set the blue fish tin on the shelf and washed sesame from his fingers. He described Jun, the bell buoy, and the cracker dust folded like a gift. Grandpa listened while slicing a pear. The problem had been a spilled snack and a closed-lid feeling, and it had ended with two children counting the same gold lights. Hugo yawned so widely the pear slice paused in the air until he laughed.

Between the lights they watched a fishing boat far off, its small lamp nodding. Hugo wondered if the people on it were sharing soup from one pot. The idea made the harbor feel like a neighborhood. Jun pointed at their reflections in the dark window of the ferry cabin, two heads and a tin held between them. "We look like a story already," Jun said. Hugo waved at his reflection and the reflection waved back without being asked. The boards hummed under their shoes, a steady song for a calm crossing.

The lantern by the window glowed softly, laying a gold path like the harbor had, only smaller and indoors. Hugo watched it and heard, in his memory, the water patting the ferry. The blue fish seemed to swim in the lamplight. He hoped Jun was tasting cinnamon tomorrow in a tin that would not be shaken too hard. Sharing, he thought, made the crossing wide enough for two. He let the gentle horn of the day fade, and he slept.
