The orchard in the late light smelled like pears and dry grass. Baskets waited under the trees, already freckled with fruit. Rosa walked with Grandma between the rows, a small basket on her arm for the special pear, the one Grandma wanted for a gift to the neighbor who had shared seeds in spring. Most pears hung where Rosa could reach by standing on her toes. One, the goldest, hung just above her fingers. A short ladder leaned at the trunk, only three steps tall, with wide feet planted in the grass. Grandma patted a rung. "I will hold it. You may try one step, or you may choose another pear. Both are fine."

Rosa looked up. The gold pear seemed to glow from inside, like a lamp that had learned to be a fruit. Her hands felt brave and her feet felt thoughtful. She had climbed the porch steps a thousand times. This ladder was shorter than the porch. Still, the first step was a new kind of yes. She put her palm on the side rail. The wood was smooth and warm. "I want to try," she said. Her voice was steady enough to stand on. Grandma smiled and set her hands on the ladder's shoulders. "I have the feet of it. You have the steps."

Rosa climbed the first step and stopped. The grass looked a little farther away, and the leaves were closer, tickling her hair with a green smell. She did not need to rush. She named what she could feel: warm wood, Grandma's steady hands, her own shoes flat and true. "I am all right," she told the tree, which was a kind audience. A wasp drifted far down the row, busy with its own work, and did not come near. Rosa watched it go and felt the orchard stay friendly. She reached. The pear was still a hand higher. "Second step," she said, so the choice would be spoken.

The second step was a small lift. The ladder did not wobble. Grandma hummed one note, not a song, just a thread to follow. Rosa's fingers met the pear. It was smooth, heavy, and sun-hot on one cheek. She held the stem the way Grandma had shown on the low fruit, a gentle twist, not a yank. The pear came loose with a soft snap and sat in her palm like a treasure that had agreed. "I have it," she said, and the words shook a little from delight, not from trouble. She held it against her middle with one arm and kept the other hand on the rail.

"Down is a step too," Grandma said. "Same courage, smaller view." Rosa turned carefully, looked at the rung, and stepped down to the first, then to the grass. The ground met her shoes with a kind thud. She laughed. The special basket received the pear as if it had been waiting all season. Grandma let go of the ladder only when Rosa was clear of it. "You did the trying," Grandma said. "The ladder and I were the furniture." Rosa liked sharing the credit. She touched the pear once more through the basket's gap and felt its warmth.

They walked the row and added two low pears so the gift would not be lonely. Rosa told Grandma which step had felt bigger. "The first," she decided, "because it was the door. The second was a room I had already entered." Grandma nodded as if Rosa had said something wise enough for a recipe card. A chicken from the neighbor's yard sang a short silly song beyond the hedge and then got quiet. The orchard air cooled by one degree, a polite evening arriving. Rosa's knees felt proud in a bony, ordinary way.

At the gate she held the ladder while Grandma folded it, and she noticed she was not hurrying away from it. She could imagine another day and another gold fruit. Courage, she thought, had been a sentence, a hand on wood, and a twist of a stem. It had not needed a crowd. It had needed Grandma's hands and Rosa's own feet. They rinsed their fingers at the orchard tap. The water was cold and tasted like the metal pipe and a little like leaves.

At home the pear sat on a blue plate to cool before it would be wrapped for the neighbor in the morning. Rosa described the two steps with her hand climbing the air. Grandpa listened and tapped the table in a tiny applause that would not startle a fruit. "A gift picked kindly arrives kindly," he said. Rosa agreed. The problem had been a pear just out of reach and a ladder that asked for a yes. She had given the yes in two pieces, which was enough.

Rosa walked a last slow circle around the tree and thanked it in a regular voice, not a speech. The leaves answered with a dry whisper. She noticed a low pear she had missed, green and patient, and she left it for another week. Leaving it felt like part of the courage, a choice not to take every yes at once. Grandma squeezed her shoulder. The ladder, folded, clicked once in a friendly way, like a gate. Rosa carried the special basket with two hands and did not look back in worry. The row behind her stayed golden and calm. She hummed Grandma's one note all the way to the tap, and the note made the grass path feel like a song she already knew.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the blue plate. Rosa watched the light make the pear look even more golden. She pictured the short ladder standing in the grass, its job done, its feet steady. Tomorrow the neighbor would smell the orchard in that one piece of fruit. Rosa curled her toes in her socks and felt the memory of the rungs. She had climbed, and she had come down, and both were brave. Sleep came like a gentle step onto solid ground.
