Nabil's basket of peas clicked softly as he walked, each pod a little purse of green. He had picked them from the vines with the grown-up, choosing pods that were full and leaving the tiny ones to grow. The basket was heavier than a handful and lighter than a chore. He could already taste a snack of peas popped with his thumb. He sat on the garden bench and opened the first pod. The row of peas inside looked like beads made for him alone.

Then he heard ducks at the pond, comfortable and chatty, and he remembered Bastian's rule. Bread was not their best food. Oats and peas were a kinder snack. He also heard Hawa's chicken song drifting from the yard, the one that meant oats were coming, and he knew chickens liked a few peas too. Nabil closed his hands over the basket. If he ate every bead, the snack would be big and the sharing would be zero. If he gave every bead away, the sharing would forget the picker. He wanted a third idea.

Delphine was marking her lettuce rows with the two sticks of her plan. Nabil carried the basket over. "You divide clover and lettuce," he said. "How would you divide peas?" Delphine tapped her chin. "Name the shares before you nibble," she said. "Then your mouth does not spend what your friends were promised." The grown-up brought three small tins and a clean cloth. "One tin for ducks, one for chickens, one for the house," the grown-up said. "Equal piles, and the pods' empty jackets go to compost, not to the lane."

Nabil popped pods and counted peas into the tins in little rhythms. Three for ducks, three for chickens, three for home, then the same again. When a pod held one extra pea, he gave it to the home tin once, the duck tin next, the chicken tin after that, so even the extras took turns. The work made him hungry, and the hunger made him proud that his own tin was real and not an afterthought. Sharing was not disappearing. Sharing was portioning.

He visited the chickens first because they were near. He did not dump the tin. He added the peas on top of their measured oats, a bright scatter, and stepped back. They ate with neat beaks. He walked the pond path next and crouched where Bastian had crouched, scattering peas in the shallow edge. The pale duck came, then the speckled one, then the one with the dark bill. They did not crowd him. He did not crowd them. The peas floated and vanished in a calm, busy way.

At the bench he ate his share slowly. Each pea tasted like the garden and like a decision. He saved four for the grown-up and four for Delphine, because a home tin could have guests. Delphine accepted hers and said the sticks in the lettuce approved of clear math. Empty pods went into the compost crock with the other garden scraps. The basket, light now, hooked on its nail.

Nabil rinsed the tins. He felt the good balance of a day that had fed more than one kind of hunger. No one had been handed the whole basket. No one had been told to wait and hope. The plan had been spoken first.

Nabil walked the rows once more to be sure he had not left a pod in the grass where someone might slip. He found one, unopened, and added its peas to tomorrow's plan by setting it in a cool bowl rather than eating it on the spot. The tins were already washed. The compost lid sat straight. He liked a finished share more than a mysterious extra. Delphine passed by and said the lettuce sticks felt proud of him, which made them both laugh, because sticks do not have faces, and still the joke was kind.

At the pond the ducks had returned to their own circles. No one paddled up to ask for another tin. Nabil took that as a good sign that enough had been enough. He told Bastian's card thank you in his head. Bread had stayed in the kitchen. Peas had gone where peas belong. His own snack had been real. Sharing, when it is planned, leaves everyone, including the planner, with a peaceful mouth and an empty basket that can rest.

He set a single pea on the windowsill for a moment, then laughed at himself and ate it, because a symbol did not need to dry up and go to waste. The real symbol was the card with three labels. Nabil tapped each word: ducks, chickens, us. The tap was soft. Outside, evening insects began their far-off music, and he let them keep their distance the way he had let the ducks keep theirs. Fair shares make a quiet house.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Nabil drew three tins on a card and labeled them ducks, chickens, and us. He tucked the card beside Corin's picnic list. Tomorrow's peas, if the vines offered more, would already know where they belonged. Sharing, he thought, is a basket with enough pockets, and one of those pockets can honestly be yours.
