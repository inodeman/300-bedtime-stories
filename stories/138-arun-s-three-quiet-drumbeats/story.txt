The night market glowed like a necklace laid along the street. Lanterns made round pools of light, and the air smelled of oranges and warm bread. Arun walked with Papa past a corner where a drum sat on a low stand, its skin the color of honey. A wooden sign said Quiet beats welcome. The drum keeper, a person with bright thread on their cuffs, tapped the drum once. The sound was a ripe fruit dropped on a cushion, soft and round. "Children may try three beats," the keeper said. "No more, so everyone who wishes can have a turn. Soft hands only."

Arun stopped. His hands wanted to hide in his sleeves and also wanted the honey skin of the drum. A line of two children went first. One tapped a pattern like rain. One tapped a pattern like steps. People listened without crowding. Papa did not push. "We can walk on," he said, "or you can take the turn your hands are thinking about." Arun swallowed. The market noise was a blanket, and the drum was a clear patch in it. He had practiced tables at home, tapping spoons, and this was a cousin of that. Still, eyes were nearby. He nodded, a small yes that grew as he stepped forward.

"Three beats," the keeper reminded, kindly. "They can be the same or a little family of different." Arun set his fingertips, not his palms, on the edge to learn the feel. The drum was warm from other hands and from the lantern above it. He lifted his fingers and let them fall: one, a round sound; two, a round sound; three, a quieter one that felt like a period at the end of a sentence. The keeper smiled. "A tidy sentence." Arun's ears rang in a pleasant way. He had begun. The beginning was the brave part, and it was already behind him, small and complete.

He stepped back so the next child could come. His heart was busy, then calmer, like a cup set down. Papa's hand found his shoulder. "I heard you," Papa said. "The third beat was a good ending." Arun looked at his fingers as if they had been on a trip. "I thought I would only watch," he said. "Watching is good. Starting was good too." They did not stay to become a crowd. Courage did not need an encore. The sign still welcomed quiet beats. Arun liked a place that asked for quiet in the middle of a bright market.

They bought two small oranges and sat on a bench where the drum was a distant cushion-sound. Arun peeled his orange in one curling ribbon, mostly, and laughed when it broke. Juice smelled sharper than the lantern smoke. He told Papa the beats had felt like knocking on a friendly door that opened on the first knock. Papa said some doors are drums. Arun ate a segment and let the sweetness be loud while his hands stayed proud and sticky. A moth circled a lantern and drew a soft loop. The market's necklace of light went on and on, and he was part of its pattern now, a person who had added three notes.

Before they left the corner for good, Arun returned the orange peel to a compost pail the keeper had set out, because courage and care could share a corner. The keeper lifted a hand. "Your sentence is still in the drum," they said, which was a poetic way of saying the skin remembered warmth. Arun believed it enough to smile. He did not ask for another turn. Leaving room was part of the welcome on the sign. Papa bought nothing else. The walk home could be the applause, quiet and long.

Arun matched his steps to a remembered rhythm: one, two, three, then a rest. The rest was important. He told Papa the rest was where the courage sat down and had a sip of water. Papa laughed softly. The problem had been sleeves that wanted to hide hands, and a drum that offered a clear patch. Three beats had solved it without anyone needing to be grand. Arun's orange-sticky fingers dried in the night air. He felt taller by exactly the height of a small decision.

At home he tapped the same sentence on the table for Mama, softer than the market, because the house was already quiet. She heard the period at the end and nodded. "You brought the market home in your hands," she said. Arun washed the juice away and watched it spin down the drain like a little lantern going out on purpose. He was ready for sleep and also ready, someday, for another sign that said a gentle try was welcome.

Arun folded his market bag into a neat square so the orange peels would not surprise anyone later, though the peels were already in the pail. He liked finishing a small job after a brave one. The square of cloth looked like a quiet flag. Papa said the drum would be there on other evenings, and other children would bring their three beats. Arun hoped to hear a rain pattern again someday from the bench, a listener as well as a player. Both roles felt brave in different shoes. He set the bag by the door where morning could find it.

In the hallway he tapped the wall once, very softly, just to hear a house answer. The answer was a short warm sound, not a drum, and still a cousin of the sentence he had played. He smiled at the difference. Markets are honey and lanterns. Homes are spoons and walls. Courage, he decided, can travel between them without getting louder. Papa hung the damp orange-scented cloth to dry and said the night had been well used. Arun believed him down to his sleepy knees.

The lantern by the window glowed softly, a cousin of the market's round lights. Arun listened and almost heard a cushion-sound in the room's hush. He pictured the honey-colored drum and his three beats sitting inside it like seeds. Courage had been a step forward and a soft hand. He let the necklace of the night loosen into rest, one, two, three, and then the long kind rest.
