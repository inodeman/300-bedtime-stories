Mabel lifted the pear-shaped watering can and heard it glug once, a cozy sound, before she stepped onto the balcony. White flowers in a wide pot were opening for the evening, each petal unfurling like a small yawn in reverse. "They wake up when I get ready for bed," Mabel told Grandpa. "The day flowers are sleeping, and these ones are starting their shift." The moon silvered the petals. Crickets sang approval in the garden below. Mabel's slippers were indoors, waiting on the rug, because balcony boards could be damp. She had put on dry house shoes for the watering job.

Grandpa nodded. "These are evening flowers. They like the cool hour. If we are gentle, they will be here tomorrow too." Mabel tipped the pear can and gave the soil a slow drink, circling so no one root would get all the water. She did not shower the open blooms. Water on petals could make them heavy. She had learned that last week, and she liked using a lesson while the night was soft around her. The window lamp behind them made a gold frame of the door. Hot milk steamed on the indoor table. A quilt lay folded, ready for the looking part of the night.

A perfect white flower leaned toward her hand. Mabel almost pinched the stem. A bouquet on the table would be pretty beside the paper stars. Her fingers paused. If she picked it, the balcony would have a hole in its small white choir, and the flower would not open again tomorrow. She bent and sniffed instead. The scent was light, like clean cotton and a little honey. "I will leave you at work," she told the plant. Grandpa's smile was proud and quiet. "Enjoying is not the same as taking. You chose the bigger kindness for the garden."

They used the cardboard telescope to visit the moon, then returned their eyes to the flowers, because nature was not only far away. Near wonder counted. Mabel noticed a bead of water at a leaf tip, shining like a loose star. She noticed the soil grow richer as it drank. She noticed one bud still closed, saving its opening for later, which felt like patience growing in a pot. Grandpa showed her the Big Dipper and said the sky had gardens of light. Mabel said their pot was a garden of scent. Both sentences fit the evening.

Inside, she did not fill a vase. She set the pear can on its saucer and washed her hands. She stuck a glow-in-the-dark sticker on the can's handle so she could find it for tomorrow's drink without a fuss. Paper stars hung in the window, and she was glad they were paper, so no living stem had been asked to decorate the glass. She sipped hot milk and listened. The crickets seemed to sing from the same direction as the flowers. Mabel imagined the music brushing the petals. It was a gentle picture, and she kept it gentle.

She drew the pear can and three white flowers in a notebook, then closed the book. A drawing was a way to keep a flower without moving it. Grandpa pressed a fallen petal that had dropped by itself into a fold of paper. "This one offered itself," he said. "We did not demand it." Mabel liked the difference. She taped the fold into the notebook. On the balcony, the rest of the blooms kept their posts under the moon. The garden was safe, awake, and not asked to give up its best dress.

Before bed Mabel stood at the rail one more time and said goodnight to the open flowers, the closed bud, the crickets, and the soil. She did not need an answer. The scent answered anyway. She put the telescope in the basket, her house shoes by the door, and her slippers on her feet. The quilt covered her knees while she finished the milk. Caring for nature, she decided, was a kind of friendship that used soft hands and left room. The room indoors felt sweeter because the pot outdoors was still full.

She yawned toward the moon and toward the flowers, a shared yawn that made Grandpa yawn too. They laughed. The pear can gleamed faintly. The night had everything it needed, and Mabel had not taken a piece of it away. She was part of the evening by noticing, watering, and stopping at the right time.

Mabel checked the soil with one fingertip and found it damp like a wrung cloth, which was enough. She set the pear can down and did not give a second pour just because pouring felt useful. Stopping was part of care. Grandpa showed her how the closed bud had loosened a single petal while they were indoors. "You left it the time it needed," he said. Mabel watched the new white edge and felt rich without owning it. She matched a paper star on the window to the flower's height, a playful measure, then let both stay where they belonged. The cricket in the garden sang a longer line, and she counted it as the flower's evening music.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the pear-shaped can and the notebook of flowers. Outside, white petals stayed open for the moon. Mabel tucked herself under the quilt, glad the garden still had every bloom it wanted, and the crickets played on for anyone who listened without plucking.
