Anouk stepped onto the book barge with both hands free, the way the reader had taught the children, so she could catch a wobble if the river gave a small polite shrug. The barge smelled of paper, lemon oil, and tea. Cushions the color of weak tea sat in a ring. Open on the low table was the big map-book, the one that showed the hill orchard, the bridge, the fountain, and the ceramic studio all on one calm page. A breeze came off the water and shut the book with a soft clap. "It did it again," Anouk said. "The orchard page will not stay."

The reader, a grown-up with ink on one thumb, sighed in a friendly way. "The brass fish clip is supposed to hold that page. It has tiny scales and a little ring that jingles. I set it down when I fetched more cushions, and now the wind is the boss." Children leaned in, hoping to see the path of painted pears. The page fluttered and closed, fluttering like a shy bird that was not frightened, only undecided. Anouk put her palm on the cover so it would rest. "May I look for the clip?" she asked. "Yes," said the reader. "Look with your eyes and your ears. It is small, but it is not silent."

Anouk did not rush from cushion to cushion, tossing them. She stood still and listened. The river tapped the hull. A page whispered. Far off, the clock tower chimed one kind note. Then, under that, she heard a seed of sound: ting, very small, when the barge shifted. "It jingled," she said. "Near the barrel." The spare cushions lived in a wide barrel by the window, stacked like sleepy pancakes. She knelt. The reader held the barrel steady. Anouk moved one cushion aside, then another, and the ting came again, close to her wrist.

A loose thread on a honey-colored cushion had looped through the fish clip's ring. The brass fish hung there, scales winking, as if it had gone fishing in the cloth. Anouk smiled. "It is not lost. It is caught." She asked, "May I unloop the thread?" The reader nodded. Anouk eased the ring free without pulling the stitch wider. The fish lay in her palm, cool and bright, no heavier than a spoon. She imagined it swimming between pages to keep a map from swimming away.

She carried it back with two hands. "The orchard page, please," she said, and the reader opened the book to the hill of small trees and the path like a pale ribbon. Anouk set the fish clip on the page so its tail held the paper and its nose pointed at the pears. The breeze tried again. The page lifted a little and stayed. "It works," said a younger child. Anouk felt curious in a new way, not only about where the clip had been, but about how a sound could be a map too. "I would not have found it if I had hurried and shaken everything," she said.

The reader traced the orchard path with a gentle finger. "Curious people make room for small noises. Thank you for listening before you dug." Anouk looked at the other marks on the page: the ferry's dotted line, the plaza's circle, the studio window drawn as a tiny square of light. She wondered, quietly, what other small things on the barge had a sound if you waited. The fish clip held on, serious and shiny, doing its one job well.

They read the orchard part aloud, taking turns on the short lines. Anouk read the sentence about a watering saucer under a young tree. The words stayed open. No one had to press a hand on the page. When the breeze came stronger, the clip's ring gave one tiny ting, as if to say it was still there, and the paper did not close. Anouk laughed under her breath. Finding could be a kind of listening.

Outside, a slow ferry passed, and someone on it waved toward the barge window. Anouk waved back without leaving her cushion. The map-book showed that the ferry and the orchard belonged to the same town, and now the page could prove it by staying put. She touched the brass fish once, lightly, the way you thank a helper who does not need a long speech.

Anouk helped the reader turn one more page, to the plaza fountain, and the fish clip held that page too. She asked if the clip had a story of its own. "It was made at the ceramic studio," the reader said, "by someone who liked small useful things." Anouk turned the brass fish so its scales caught the river light. A tool could be tiny and still keep a whole map polite. The children leaned in, and nobody had to press the breeze flat with a hand. The orchard, the fountain, and the bridge stayed visible together, which was the point of a town drawn on one calm page.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the barrel of cushions, turning the brass fish a warmer gold. Anouk watched the orchard page rest under the clip. She had followed a jingle no louder than a seed, and the map had opened again for everyone. Curiosity, she decided, was not a rush to grab. It was a careful question the ears could ask.
