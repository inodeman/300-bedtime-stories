Tamsin sat on the rug with a pillow in her lap while a distant rumble moved along the far hills. The town sky outside the window was pale and quiet, and the street was dry. Anita sat beside her with a stack of paper and a box of crayons. A grown-up, Tamsin's mom, latched the window and pulled the curtain halfway, enough to soften the light and still let them see that the lane was calm. "The thunder is far away," Mom said. "We are indoors, and we are staying indoors. I will explain anything that sounds big. You do not have to be outside in weather that rumbles."

Tamsin squeezed the pillow. Courage, for her, needed a voice. "I can hear it in my stomach," she said. "I want to be brave, but I also want to be close to you." Mom sat on the floor so their eyes were level. "Brave and close can be the same thing. Staying in, telling the truth, and letting a grown-up help is a steady kind of courage." Anita nodded. "We can draw the far hills and our safe room. Drawing gives the rumble a small size on paper." Tamsin liked that. A small size was easier to look at.

Mom explained in a slow voice. Lightning was a spark in a far cloud. Thunder was the spark's late voice. A long wait between a faint flicker and a rumble meant the spark was far from their street. They tried it. A tiny blink touched the horizon. Tamsin counted with Anita, and Mom counted too, all the way to seven. The rumble arrived soft and already tired. "Seven is far," Mom said. "The hills are having weather. Our rug is having crayons." Tamsin let out a breath she had been saving. The explanation did not tease her. It held her.

They made a comfort list and taped it to the low table. Stay inside. Count. Draw. Ask questions. Sit near a grown-up. Tamsin added the pillow to the drawing of the list, because the pillow was part of her courage. Anita drew a lantern even though they would light it later, a promise of evening calm. Another rumble came, lower than the first. Tamsin checked the list instead of running to the door. "I am following step one," she said. Mom squeezed her hand. "That is brave in a way I trust. Nobody is going out to look for a closer sound."

Tamsin asked the question she had been hiding. "Can thunder find a person who is quiet?" Mom answered clearly. "We are not hiding from a creature. We are respecting weather that is far away. Quiet or talking, we are safe in the house. The choice to stay in is what keeps us comfortable." Anita added, "And we can talk as much as we want." So they talked. They talked about kites and puddles and hats and gardens, all the gentle sky days they liked. The rumble became one voice in a room full of better stories. Tamsin's stomach unknotted by degrees.

She practiced a brave sentence between sounds. "I am indoors. I am with Mom and Anita. The count was seven." Saying it did not erase every jump, and she did not pretend it did. When a new rumble made her blink hard, she told them. "That one still startled me." Mom nodded. "Thank you. Startled can sit beside brave. They can share the pillow." Anita moved closer so their shoulders touched. The courage in the room was not loud. It was company, facts, and a latched window. Tamsin decided she liked that kind best.

After a long quiet, Mom opened the curtain wider. The hills were soft and ordinary. No new flicker came. The street shone in normal daylight. "The distant weather is moving along," Mom said. "We will wait a little more, and we will go out only when it is time for ordinary air." Tamsin did not argue for the door. Finishing the wait was part of the brave plan. She colored the last hill on her paper a peaceful blue. Anita colored the rug a warm gold. The picture looked like the truth. Far weather, near comfort.

They put the crayons away together, each cap clicked on, a calm chore. Tamsin hugged her mom and then hugged Anita. "I was courageous because I stayed, I counted, and I said how I felt," she said. Mom kissed the top of her head. "Yes. That is the whole lesson, and you did it." Outside, the day settled toward evening without another rumble. The house felt like a good place to have been. The pillow stayed on the rug like a soft reminder that comfort and courage had shared the same afternoon without either one being sent away.

The lantern by the window glowed softly once the sky was fully quiet and the room took on its evening shine. Tamsin set her drawing beside the light. The far hills on the page looked gentle, and the real hills outside looked gentle too. She rested easy, knowing courage had been a calm explanation, a grown-up nearby, and the wise choice to stay indoors until the distant thunder was gone.
