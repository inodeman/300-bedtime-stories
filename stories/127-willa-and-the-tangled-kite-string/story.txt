The hill above town wore a green coat and a ribbon of path. Willa carried a kite shaped like a red fish, its tail made of spare cloth strips that whispered. The breeze was polite, enough to lift hair and not enough to steal hats. Papa spread a blanket where the grass was shortest. "The sky is ready when the string is," he said. Willa felt ready in her feet. She wanted the fish to swim the air before the sun moved even one hand's width.

She ran a few steps, let out string, and the fish rose. It tugged like a friendly dog. Then the string jerked, and the kite dipped. Willa looked down. A nest of loops had gathered near the handle, tight as a thought she could not finish. She pulled. The nest became smaller and harder. The fish wagged, confused. "Easy," Papa said from the blanket. "Yanking ties the knot a bow it did not ask for." Willa's cheeks were warm from the run and from the hurry. She wanted the sky now.

She sat down with the tangle in her lap. The grass prickled in a cheerful way. Up close the string was cream-colored and a little fuzzy. She found one loose end that led nowhere useful and set it aside. "I will do one loop," she told herself. The first loop slid free after she widened it with two fingers. The kite, resting on the grass now, looked patient, which helped her be patient too. A beetle climbed a blade and waved its feelers, in no rush to be anywhere but there.

The second loop hid under the third. Willa almost tugged. She remembered the bow the knot would become and breathed out. She turned the whole nest over. Light showed a gap. She fed the string through, slowly, the way Mama threaded a needle at the table. Papa did not take the tangle away. He watched the town's roofs and let her own the job. "You are doing the quiet kind of work," he said. Willa liked that name for it. Quiet work. It made the waiting feel like skill.

A cloud shaped like a spoon drifted over. Willa used it as a timer without counting too strictly. When the spoon's handle thinned, another loop had opened. Her fingers smelled like grass and string. The fish's tail twitched whenever the breeze visited, as if to say it could wait without wilting. She told it, "I am coming." A child far down the hill flew a yellow diamond already high, and Willa did not let that picture pull her hands faster. Their sky was big. Hers would be ready.

The last knot was the size of a pea. She loosened it by pushing the middle, not by pulling the ends. It sighed apart. The string lay in one honest line across her knees. Willa laughed once, a surprised sound. "It listened," she said. Papa smiled. "You listened first." They stood. She held the handle, walked into the breeze, and let the string go out in a smooth feed. The red fish lifted, nose first, then tail, then the cloth strips writing soft letters. The tug in her hands was clean.

They flew the kite until Willa's arms hummed a happy hum. She practiced letting out a little and taking in a little, never a yank. When the breeze napped, the fish hovered, and she waited for the next breath of air instead of reeling in a panic. The yellow diamond far away dipped in a friendly way. Willa dipped hers back. No words crossed the hill, but the strings understood. Papa poured water that tasted like the cool inside of a jar.

On the walk down she coiled the string the way she had uncoiled the knot, loop by wide loop. The fish rode under her arm, still smiling its painted smile. "Patience is a kind of winding," she told Papa. He nodded. "And a kind of unwinding." The town grew larger with each step. Windows flashed. Willa's problem had been a small nest of string and a big wish to skip the work. The wish had waited, and the work had made the wish true. At home she hung the kite where it could rest without creasing its tail. She showed Mama the place on her finger where the string had left a pale line, not a bother, just a mark of holding on. They ate toast and listened to the evening begin. Willa felt proud in a quiet way that matched the quiet work. Tomorrow she would check the string before she ran. That plan felt as light as the red fish.

Willa laid the loose string in a wide circle on the blanket so it could not knot itself again while they drank water. The circle looked like a pond drawn with cream-colored thread. She stepped over it instead of through it. Papa said that was respect for work you had already finished. The red fish lay with its tail spread so the cloth strips would not twist. Willa checked each strip with her fingers. Smooth, smooth, one little fold she eased flat. The hill wind combed the grass in one direction, a green brush painting the slope.

Far below, someone rang a bicycle bell, bright as a berry. Willa waved even though the rider could not see which child meant it. The kite string in her hands stayed calm. She let out one more arm's length and the fish rose a polite step higher, then held. That small rise felt better than a wild dash because she had earned it loop by loop. She told Papa the pea-sized knot's story in full, including the moment she had wanted to yank. He said wanting is weather, and choosing is the kite. Willa repeated that so she would remember it on the next windy day. She added that the spoon-shaped cloud had been her timer, and Papa said clouds are the most patient clocks he knows.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beneath the hanging kite. Willa watched the cloth tail give one last whisper in a draft and then be still. She pictured the spoon cloud and the pea-sized knot and the clean tug. The hill, she thought, would keep their breeze for another day. Her eyes closed on a sky the color of grass and patience.
