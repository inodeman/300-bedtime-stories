Luca paused on the harbor boards while a friendly fog thinned around the masts. It was not a wall. It was a pale curtain with bright seams. Haru stood beside him, already smiling because the morning felt like a riddle with a soft answer. A harbor helper swept the pier nearby and hummed. Luca had a notebook and three blank lines waiting. "I want to know why the fog hides a boat and then gives it back," he said. Haru tapped the rail. "Then we watch the giving-back. Curiosity likes a front-row seat."

They chose one mast to study. At first only a gray smudge showed. Luca wrote question one. Where does the fog leave first? They looked up, not just out. The brightness was stronger above the smudge. Little by little, a tip appeared, then a rope, then the whole mast, as if someone were drawing it with a steady pencil. "It clears from the top," Luca said. Haru nodded. "Like a lid lifting, the way it did on my walk." Luca liked that the harbor could repeat a lesson for anyone who asked.

Question two went on the page. What can we know even before we see? They closed their eyes for three breaths. The buoy bell rang. Water tapped wood. A gull laughed once. Luca opened his eyes. "We can know the harbor is still here by sound." He wrote that down. Haru added that the yellow rail was a sight that stayed even when boats were shy. The helper overheard and said, in a calm voice, "Fog is a cloud resting low. On a friendly morning it moves aside when the air warms and the light grows." Luca wrote the sentence carefully, glad for a clear answer.

The crates at the end of the pier faded in next, solid and ordinary and wonderful because they had been missing. Luca asked question three out loud. "Does the fog leave all at once, or in patches?" They walked slowly toward the crates, hands near the rail, because curiosity did not mean carelessness. A patch of water shone, then a patch of fog lingered by a low boat, then that patch slid away too. "Patches," Haru said. "The harbor returns like a puzzle that wants to be kind." Luca sketched little boxes on the page, some filled, some still pale.

He noticed his own feeling changing with the view. At the start he had felt a tickle of not-knowing. Now the tickle had become a list. That seemed like a good trade. "Questions are handles," he told Haru. "They let me hold the morning without squeezing it." Haru borrowed the notebook and added a small drawing of the bell. "And answers can be drawings, not just words," Haru said. Luca agreed at once. A curious person could collect more than facts. A curious person could collect ways of noticing.

They asked the helper one last question. "How do sailors stay comfortable when fog is friendly but thick?" The helper pointed to the rail, the bell, and the slow pace. "We use what still speaks to us. We do not rush the white air. We let it clear." Luca liked that this answer matched what he had seen. He was collecting comfort that was also true. The last ribbon of fog drifted toward the open water and became a shine instead of a veil.

Back at the start of the pier, Luca read the three questions and the three answers aloud. Haru corrected nothing unkindly, only added the sound of the gull, which Luca had almost forgotten. "Good," Luca said. "A second pair of ears is part of curiosity too." They watched a boat's reflection settle into the clear water. The sky above the harbor was mild blue now, with a leftover softness at the edges, like the fog had signed its name and left.

Luca closed the notebook but did not close his wonder. Tomorrow there might be a breeze, or a puddle, or a cloud picture, and each would take new questions. He thanked the helper and bumped elbows with Haru. The boards were easy to see. The morning felt larger because they had looked closely at a small change. That was the gift of a curious hour.

Luca and Haru stood by the bell post after the fog had fully cleared and tested their notes against the real harbor. Every crate was where the sound had promised. Every rope was ordinary and wonderful. Luca added a fourth line even though he had planned on three. What changed in me? He wrote that his tickle of not-knowing had turned into a calm list. Haru drew a small smile next to the line. Curiosity, they decided, felt best when it ended in comfort as well as facts. The helper waved them toward home, and the pier boards answered their steps with a steady, sunny sound.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Luca read his harbor notes one more time in its gentle light. The page showed masts, a bell, and three questions with room for more. Outside, the evening water was clear and quiet. He felt wonderfully curious and wonderfully safe, sure that asking was a cozy way to greet the world.
