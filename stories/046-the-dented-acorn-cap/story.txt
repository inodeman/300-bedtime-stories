Felix turned the borrowed acorn-cap button over in his palm outside the hollow tree library. It was perfectly round, polished by many careful paws, and it fastened the flap of his leaf satchel so his paper map would not slide out. The library fox had lent it for the afternoon with a simple request. "Bring it back when the sun leans low," the fox had said, in the way foxes speak with bright eyes and a tap of a paw on the cap. Felix had nodded hard. He meant to be careful. He really, truly meant to.

He sat on a mossy root to eat a berry and study his map. The rope footbridge was a wavy line. The picnic clearing was a circle. His mushroom cottage was a small dome near the ferns. Without looking, he set the acorn-cap button on the root beside him so he could unfold the map with both hands. When he shifted to catch the light, he sat down again, right on the little cap. There was a soft tick, a sound too small for the oaks to notice and too big for Felix to ignore. He lifted himself at once and picked up the button. A dent, pale and crooked, marked one side.

Felix's face went hot. He glanced toward the library hollow, then toward the ferns, where moss could cover almost anything. For three long breaths he imagined tucking the button under a green cushion and saying he had lost it on the path. The idea felt oily, like berry juice on his fingers that he did not want to lick. He closed his hand around the dented cap. "That would not be true," he said to a firefly sleeping on a fern. "The fox trusted me with something small and special. I can walk back with the truth in my hand."

He stood, brushed moss from his knees, and walked straight to the hollow tree. Leaf-pages rustled as the library fox looked up from a stack the color of fresh tea. Felix held out the button before he could lose his nerve. "I sat on it," he said. "I did not mean to, but I did, and now it has a dent. I am sorry. I will not pretend the wind did it or that the path swallowed it." His voice shook, then steadied. The fox's ears stayed kind. It took the cap gently, turned it in a beam of sun, and touched the dent with one careful claw tip, not angry, only interested.

"We can coax it," Felix said, hopeful and humble at the same time. He remembered that warm water made bark and seed shells a little softer. He fetched his oak-bark cup from the satchel, filled it at the dew-dark moss by the door, and let the cup sit in a patch of sun until the water lost its chill. Together they dipped the acorn cap, not to drown it, but to warm the dented side. Felix pressed with the pad of his thumb, slow as a sigh. The fox pressed from the other side with a smooth pebble wrapped in a leaf so the shell would not scratch.

The dent rose a little, then a little more. It did not become invisible. A faint moon of a mark remained, honest as Felix's words. He almost apologized again for the trace of the dent, but the fox set the button in his palm and nudged his wrist toward the satchel flap, inviting him to try the fastening. The cap still held. The leaf flap stayed shut. Felix laughed once, surprised. "It works," he said. "The mark is part of its story now, and so is telling you." The fox's tail drew a pleased arc across the library floor.

They sat on the threshold and shared the rest of Felix's berries. He told the whole small tale again, including the moment he had looked at the ferns and thought about hiding. "I want you to know I almost chose the easy quiet," he said. "Then I chose the true quiet, the kind that comes after you speak." The fox leaned its shoulder against his arm. Around them, leaf-pages waited in tidy piles, each one a place where someone had written what they really saw. Felix thought the library itself was a kind of honesty, leaves that did not pretend to be oaks or maps or bridges.

Before he left, he fastened the satchel with the mended button and promised to return it before dusk, just as asked. He walked to the picnic clearing and back, not to delay, but to feel the truth sitting lightly now instead of hotly. On the rope footbridge he held the soft rail and did not hurry. When he placed the acorn-cap button in the fox's paw at the library door, the sun was leaning, and both of them looked satisfied. Felix bowed his head in thanks. "Next time I will set precious things where I cannot sit," he said, and the fox's eyes glittered like a smile.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in Felix's mushroom cottage as he told the empty room the same true story, so he would remember how good the ending felt. He set his paper map on the sill where the light could find it. The dented button was back in the library, useful and honestly marked, and his chest did not feel oily anymore. A gentle breeze moved the ferns outside. Felix drank cool water and let his shoulders drop. Honesty, he thought, was a walk back to a friend with the small broken thing still in your hand, and a voice that said exactly what your hands had done.
