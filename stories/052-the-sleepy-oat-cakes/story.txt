Ivo read the leaf-page recipe for the third time, his finger under the line that mattered most. Let the oat-cake batter rest until it looks sleepy and thick. He had copied the recipe at the hollow tree library, and the words were clear, yet his spoon hand wanted to rush. The batter sat in a wide tin on his mushroom cottage table, pale and dotted with berries, smelling like a promise. "If I bake it now, we could eat sooner," he told the tin. A gentle fox on the doorsill flicked an ear, unconvinced. Ivo sighed. "All right. We will wait. Waiting is the secret ingredient, even if it has no taste yet."

He set the tin away from the sunny window so the rest would be even, not hot on one side. Then he faced the long, soft stretch of time. His foot tapped. His eyes kept hopping to the tin. "I need a job for my impatience," he said. The fox tilted its head toward the dusty step. Ivo took the hint. He swept the step with a whisk of dry twigs, shook the mat, and lined the boots up neatly. The work used his hands without bothering the batter. Each time he finished a task, the cakes were still resting, and the cottage smelled a little more ready for a guest.

He turned to the table next. Patience, he decided, could look like a beautiful place. He set two oak-leaf plates, two acorn-cap cups, and a tiny jar of cool berry syrup. He folded napkins from soft green leaves and weighed them down with smooth pebbles he had found by the ferns. The fox watched as if judging a festival. "Is the left cup too far?" Ivo asked. The fox nudged it closer to the plate. "Thank you. You have a good eye for reach." Ivo laughed, and the laugh made the waiting feel lighter, like a basket with a handle.

He checked the batter only once, because hovering seemed rude even to a tin. The surface had gone silkier. Berries floated like little boats. "Sleepy and thick," he whispered. "Not yet, but closer." He took his paper map outside and sat on the freshly swept step with the fox. Together they traced the path to the rope footbridge and the picnic clearing. Ivo told a story about a bridge knot that held because someone had tied it slowly. The fox's tail thumped the moss once, a yes. While they talked, the sun moved a finger-width along the sill. Time was doing its quiet baking.

When the batter finally looked like the recipe's sleepy picture, Ivo slid the tin toward the warm stove shelf, not a roaring heat, just the steady warmth his cottage kept for cooking. He did not peek every minute. He polished the acorn-cap cups instead, and he filled a small bowl with water for the fox. "Guests should not have to ask," he said. The smell changed, slowly, from raw and cool to toasted and sweet. Oats hummed at the edges. Berries burst into purple pockets. Ivo's stomach sang, but he kept his hands busy folding one more napkin, just in case the first ones were not perfect enough for a friend.

The cakes came out golden, with tops that sprang back when he touched them with a careful fingertip. He waited again, a smaller wait, so no one would burn a tongue. That second patience felt easier, because the first wait had taught his body the shape of it. He cut the round into generous wedges and set the best-looking piece on the fox's leaf plate. "You waited with me," he said. "You get a fine wedge and the first pour of syrup." The fox ate neatly, then licked a purple drop from its whisker and looked extremely pleased. Ivo tasted his own piece. The crumb was tender, not tough, not soggy. The rest had done real work.

"I almost argued with a recipe," he admitted, mouth sweet. "I thought sooner was the same as better. It is not, not for cakes, and maybe not for most good things." The fox leaned on his knee, heavy and warm. They ate until the tin held only a fragrant shine. Ivo wrapped two small wedges in leaves for a later walk to the library, a thank-you for the leaf-page that had told him the truth. He washed the spoon and watched the water carry oat specks away. Outside, ferns nodded in a mild breeze. The step was clean. The table was kind. The day had not been wasted while the batter slept.

He carried the wrapped wedges only as far as the sill for now, because dusk was the right time for delivering treats, and dusk was still a little off. Waiting, he realized, had filled itself with sweeping, stories, and a fox's good advice about cup distances. Nothing empty had happened. The cottage felt larger from all the small readiness. Ivo stretched his arms and let out a laugh that surprised him. "We did it," he told the fox. "Slowly, which was the only speed that worked."

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the wrapped oat cakes, turning their leaf packets gold at the edges. Ivo lit nothing in a hurry. He simply sat with the gentle fox and listened to the stove tick as it cooled. The taste of berry and oat lingered, proof that rest had been an ingredient and not a delay. Patience, he thought, was a clean step, a cup nudged closer, and a batter left alone until it was truly ready to become something worth sharing.
