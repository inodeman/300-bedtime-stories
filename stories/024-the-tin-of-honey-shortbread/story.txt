Theo settled on the dune with the round tin balanced on his knees like a small drum. Inside, the honey shortbread was stacked in pale golden moons, each one smelling of warm kitchens and summer grass. He had counted them on the cottage step. Eight. Eight was a very good number when a boy planned to eat slowly and watch the little wooden boats draw white lines on the blue cove. The picnic blanket was just his, striped with sand at the edges. A gull landed a respectful distance away and fluffed itself into a comfortable ball. "This is a looking day," Theo told it. "And a tasting day. Mostly tasting."

He worked the tin's clasp with his thumb. It clicked, a neat sound. The first shortbread broke with a soft snap. Butter and honey met the sea air, and Theo thought he might be the luckiest person on the whole shore. Below, the friendly lighthouse threw a clean shadow. Tide pools winked between the rocks. He was lifting the cookie to his mouth when he saw two figures on the path, Auntie with her hat in her hand and the lighthouse keeper with a coil of light line over one shoulder. They looked warm from work and a little dusty. Their hands were empty of any basket.

Theo paused with the shortbread an inch from his lips. He could take a quick bite. He could close the tin and sit on it, casual as a rock. The gull made a small sound that was not a demand, only a hello. Theo's stomach did want the whole stack. He had thought about these cookies since breakfast, when the tray was too hot to touch and the waiting had felt enormous. Now the waiting was over, and here came company. He lowered his hand. "If I eat them all," he said quietly, "I will be full, and they will still be walking on biscuit-less feet."

Auntie reached the blanket and dropped onto a corner with a happy sigh. "We reset the boat fenders and lost track of time," she said. "The cove smells like a bakery up here. That seems unfair." The keeper laughed and rubbed sand from his palms. "I was promised a view, not a rumor of honey." Theo felt the tin grow heavier in the best and hardest way. He could still choose. Sharing was not a rule shouted from the lighthouse. It was a door he could open. He turned the tin so the golden stack faced them. "I brought enough if we make the pieces friendly," he said. "They taste like the kitchen decided to visit."

He broke the first moon into three uneven parts, then tried again so the shares looked kinder. Crumbs fell onto a clean napkin. Auntie did not grab. She waited, eyebrows up, letting Theo be the one to pass the pieces. The keeper took his with both hands, as if it were a small ceremony. "To boat fenders and dunes," he said, which was a silly toast and a perfect one. Theo tasted his third at last. The honey was still honey. The butter was still butter. Nothing important had been subtracted from the flavor. If anything, the bite was brighter because of the smiles happening at the same time.

They did not rush the tin. Between pieces they pointed out a starfish the color of tea resting far down in a pool, safe from fingers. The gull edged closer, hopeful in a polite way, and Theo set one crumb on a flat stone well away from the blanket. "Wild birds get wild snacks," Auntie said. "Not the whole bakery." The gull took the crumb and sailed off to a post, free as ever. Theo watched it go and felt a roomy feeling in his chest. The tin still held more shortbread. He had not given himself away down to nothing. He had made the circle bigger.

A breeze tried to skate a napkin toward the marram grass. Theo caught it, and the keeper caught the tin before it could tip. They laughed at the almost-mess. "This is better than eating in a hurry by myself," Theo admitted. "I thought the best part was the number eight. The best part is the crunch while somebody tells about a stubborn fender." Auntie wiped a shine of honey from her thumb. "Keep a piece for the walk home," she said. "Sharing can include your later self, too." Theo liked that. He wrapped one moon in the napkin and tucked it beside the tin, a promise that did not feel like selfishness.

The sun moved. Shadows of the boats stretched into long blue ribs on the water. Theo passed the last broken piece around until only sweet dust remained on the metal. He let the keeper tap the tin like a tiny drum. The sound was bright and finished. Nobody's face looked disappointed. Theo's stomach was pleased, not stuffed, and his hands smelled like honey and salt. He leaned back on his elbows. The shore kept its sparkle. He had arrived wanting a private pile. He was leaving with a story that three people could tell at supper without leaving anyone out.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as they came down the dune, the empty tin swinging from Theo's finger by its little wire handle. He could hear a crumb ticking inside, a leftover music. Auntie put her hat back on against the low sun. The keeper whistled a tune that matched their steps. Theo touched the wrapped piece in his pocket and smiled. Sharing had not shrunk the shortbread into a sad story. It had given the honey more people to land on, and the walk home tasted warm all the way to the cottage door.
