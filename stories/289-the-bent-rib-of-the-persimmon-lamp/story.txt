Gio arrived at the fruit and lamp market while the sky was still apricot and the lamps were only beginning their work. Nothing felt late or frightening. Stalls held figs, starfruit, and persimmons with neat green tops. At the corner stall, a paper lamp shaped like a persimmon should have glowed orange over the pale slices. Instead the light pooled on one side. A wire rib inside the paper had bent, making a dim pleat. "The fruit looks half hidden," Gio said. "People will miss the best slices."

The lamp maker, a grown-up with careful fingers, nodded. "The rib met a basket corner. Paper does not like a sudden rescue." Gio's hands wanted to reach in and pull the wire straight in one proud motion. He even lifted his fingers. Then he stopped. "If I yank, I might tear the persimmon cheek," he said. "Yes," said the lamp maker. "We will warm our hands, so the wire is not a cold surprise, and we will ease it a thumb's width, then rest, then ease again. Patience is the tool."

Gio rubbed his palms until they felt kind. The lamp maker opened a small flap at the bottom, not a rip, a made place for mending. Gio could see the rib, a thin silver line gone crooked like a bent straw. "I will touch, not tug," he said, mostly to himself. He pressed the bend with the pad of his thumb. The wire moved a little and the paper sighed, a dry small sound, not a tear. He stopped. They both looked. The pleat was softer. "Again," said the lamp maker, "only after a breath."

Gio breathed. He eased the rib once more. The persimmon shape grew rounder. Light began to reach the slices, turning them the color of honey. A customer paused, saw the fruit clearly, and smiled without being asked to. Gio felt the hurry rise anyway. "It is almost done. I could finish it fast now." The lamp maker shook their head in a friendly way. "Almost is when paper tears. Stay with the small moves." Gio stayed. One more thumb's width, then a rest, then the last gentle press. The rib lay true. The pleat opened into a smooth cheek.

They closed the flap and set the lamp on its hook. The glow became even, a warm orange that made the stall look like a piece of evening fruit itself. Gio's thumb had a faint red mark from patience, not from a yank, and it faded while he watched. "I wanted to be the person who fixed it in one go," he said. "You are the person who fixed it without a hole," said the lamp maker. "That is the better story." Gio laughed quietly so the paper would not feel a puff of air too strong.

He helped arrange the pale slices in a fan so each one caught the new light. The market murmured, calm and busy, with no rush that pushed. Somewhere the clock tower chimed its kind note, and the lamp seemed to brighten in answer, though that was only the sky deepening into a softer blue. Gio thought of other bent things: a bookmark, a basket handle, a feeling. Not all of them needed a yank. Some needed a warm hand and a little time between tries.

The lamp maker gave him a spare paper leaf to practice folds, not to fix anything, just to learn the feel of going slowly. Gio folded one crease, waited, and folded the next. The leaf looked like the green cap of a persimmon. "I can wait between creases," he said. "Then you can make more than you break," said the lamp maker. They hung the practice leaf where it could be seen, a small green flag of a lesson.

Gio stepped back to the walking side. From there the stall looked whole. The bent rib was a secret the light no longer told. He felt proud and also calm, which was a new mixture. Patience had not been doing nothing. It had been doing a little, then letting the paper catch up. The slices shone, and a neighbor chose three, able to see what they were choosing.

Gio stayed one more minute to watch the even glow, because leaving too fast felt like yanking in a different way. He told the lamp maker, "My hands still want to hurry. I can notice that and wait anyway." The lamp maker tapped the stall wood twice, a small yes. A child asked why the persimmon lamp was round now, and Gio said, "A rib inside was bent. We warmed our hands and moved it a little at a time." The child nodded as if that were a normal way to treat light. Gio thought it should be.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the stall's own window, the persimmon lamp among other fruit-shaped lamps, round and even and kind. Gio looked at the smooth cheek he had eased back into place. Going slowly had kept the paper whole and shown the fruit. He walked on with his grown-up, the apricot sky still friendly above the lamps.
