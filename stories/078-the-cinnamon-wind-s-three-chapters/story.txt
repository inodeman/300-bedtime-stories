A cinnamon wind came through the Saturday market like a friendly ribbon. It slipped past the picnic baskets, curled around the honey jars, and tugged Amira's sleeve. She stopped in the lane. Karim stopped too, because the air suddenly smelled like warm trees and orange sunshine. Yara, arranging peels at the stall beside the neighborhood bakery, lifted her head and smiled as if the wind had said her name.

"Where is it going?" Amira asked.

"Where did it begin?" Karim asked at the same time.

They decided to follow it, not in a race, but in a notice-walk. Nature was speaking in smells. The bakery door let out a wave of bread-oven heat, and the wind braided that heat with something wilder, the smell of bark and peel before they became recipes. Soup pots chimed inside. The wind did not knock the lids off. It only invited noses.

They passed a stall where orange peels dried on a cloth in the sun. Amira paused. The sunny peels smelled louder than the ones in the shade. "The light is stirring them," she said. "Like a wooden spoon made of sun."

Karim found a curl of cinnamon bark in a bowl at Yara's stall. He did not take a handful. The bowl was full, and a little was enough to learn. He held one dry curl. It looked like a tiny hillside. When the wind crossed it, the smell rose, sweet and woody, the same ribbon that had found them in the lane.

"It is the bark," he said. "And the peel. And the sun on both."

Yara nodded. "They grow before they reach our jars. The tree makes the bark. The fruit makes the peel. The weather dries them. We only borrow the smell for bread and honey." She spoke with care, the way the kind grocer at the next cloth had taught her to talk about food, as a gift that was already plenty.

They made a small nature trail on the shared table so others could notice too. A sun peel. A shade peel. A cinnamon curl. A slice of bakery bread to show where the smells were going. A spoon of honey that had taken on a faint orange halo from a peel left in the jar overnight. Each item got a quiet label, not a loud sign. Sun. Shade. Bark. Borrowed sweetness.

Amira closed her eyes and named the wind's parts. Warm crust. Bright oil. Tree spice. Market dust that did not smell bad, only like footsteps and baskets. Karim tried and mixed up crust and bark, then laughed and tried again. Patience made the second sniff smarter. Yara told them to breathe out first so their noses could start fresh. It worked. The next breath was a story with three chapters.

They did not pick the stalls clean. They took only what the trail needed, and the bowls still looked generous. A picnic basket received a wrapped heel of bread and a few peels for a later walk, because nature's smell could travel. The rest stayed for the neighborhood. Sharing the trail meant leaving it intact for the next curious nose.

Karim wrote a card. Follow the cinnamon wind. Stop where the sun is cooking the peels. Listen with your nose. Thank the tree and the fruit by taking a little and leaving a lot. Then bring the smell to the table and share it. He drew a wavy line for the wind. Amira added a small sun. Yara added a bark curl that looked like a smile.

They sat and tasted bread with a dot of the halo honey. The flavor was the walk, pressed into one bite. Crust, orange, tree. The ovens hummed behind the window like distant bees that had decided to be gentle machines. The market kept shining. The wind came back, lighter now, as if it had finished its errand.

"I thought cinnamon began in a tin," Karim said.

"The tin is a pocket," Yara said. "The pocket is not the tree."

Amira lifted her face into the breeze. "Then our recipe starts outside."

They left the nature trail on the table for the afternoon, with the card propped where it would not blow away. People could look, sniff, and put a peel back in its dish. The lesson was attention, not collecting. Attention felt like a full basket that did not weigh anyone down.

They walked the lane once more with empty hands, only noses working. The wind had softened. It still carried crust from the bakery door, peel from the sunny cloth, and bark from Yara's bowl. Amira named them in order. Karim named them backward and got them right the second time. Yara showed them a cinnamon curl that looked like a tiny ear, listening. They returned it to the bowl. Taking a little and leaving a lot was a promise they could feel, because the bowl's mound hardly changed. On the shared table the trail waited, labels neat, bread ready for another dot of halo honey. Nature's recipe did not run out when it was read. It grew clearer. The market awning cast a cool stripe, and the sunny peels beyond it kept doing their slow, bright work.

The lantern by the window glowed softly at the end of the little trail of peel and bark. Amira, Karim, and Yara sat with the cinnamon wind still in their hair. Nature had written the first recipe in sun and trees, and they had read it slowly enough to share.
