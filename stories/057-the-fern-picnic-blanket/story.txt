Lila spread a half-made fern picnic blanket in the clearing and watched the warm breeze lift every corner but one. She had woven long fern fronds through a frame of fallen oak twigs, a green cloth that was really a garden you could sit on. It was wider than her arms. When she smoothed the left side, the right side flipped. When she knelt on the right, the far edge curled like a smile that would not stay put. "This blanket wants a whole team," she said, laughing instead of fussing. "I cannot be four corners at once."

A gentle fox trotted in from the oaks, then another with dusk-colored ears, and a small fox who still had extra bounce in its step. Lila waved them over. "I am trying to lay a picnic blanket that keeps flying away. If each of you holds a corner, I can tie moss weights so the ferns stay friendly with the ground." She showed them the soft moss balls she had rolled, light enough not to crush the fronds and heavy enough to mean stay. The foxes took corners in their mouths, careful, and backed away until the blanket opened like a calm pool of green.

"A little lower, please," Lila said to the small fox, whose corner was trying to be a flag. The small fox crouched, proud to be useful. Lila tied the first moss weight with a grass knot and patted it. "One corner done. Three to go, and nobody let go yet." The dusk-eared fox shifted its paws and kept the weave tight. Lila crawled along the edge, tying as she went, talking so everyone knew the plan. "Hold. Knot. Breathe. Hold again." Her knees stained green, and she did not mind. Teamwork was allowed to be a little messy if the mess was shared.

The breeze tried one more joke and slipped under the middle. The blanket rose in a gentle dome. Lila quickly sat in the center, and the dome became a lap. The foxes stayed at their corners, eyes bright, as if they were playing a game with rules they liked. "You are excellent anchors," she told them. She tied the last two weights and tested each knot with a tug that was firm but kind. When she stood, the fern blanket lay almost flat, only a soft ripple left, like a pond that had remembered it was a picnic. Lila clapped once. The foxes dropped their corners and sniffed their work.

Now the picnic could begin. Lila unpacked acorn biscuits, berry slices, and a bark cup of water to pour into acorn caps. She placed the food in the middle so every corner-holder could reach a share. "Workers eat first," she said, then corrected herself with a grin. "Workers and friends, which is all of us." They ate sitting on the ferns, and the blanket smelled like fresh shade. The small fox discovered that a biscuit crumb looked like a pebble and nosed it onto Lila's leaf plate as a trade. She traded back a bigger crumb. Fairness made the meal taste better. Overhead, oaks clicked their leaves, and a firefly rehearsed its lamp under the blanket's edge, then changed its mind and floated free.

Lila unfolded her paper map and showed where the rope footbridge crossed a dip in the path, and where the hollow tree library kept leaf-pages dry. "We could carry this blanket to the library steps another day," she said, "but only if we all go. It is a four-corner friend." The dusk-eared fox tapped the library mark, interested. The other fox tapped the bridge. The small fox tapped the clearing, clearly voting to stay. Lila laughed. "Today the clearing wins. That is what happens when a team gets a vote." She did not overrule the smallest voice. She tucked the map away and poured more water.

After eating, they shook crumbs onto the moss beyond the blanket so the weave would stay clean. Lila showed the foxes how to roll the ferns inward without snapping them, each helper keeping a side even. The roll was bumpy and wonderful. They tied it with grass and set it under an oak where dusk could not dampen it too fast. "Tomorrow we can unroll it again," Lila said. "Blankets like this want return visitors." The foxes stretched, full and pleased. One leaned against Lila's shoulder. She leaned back, careful not to tip either of them. Resting together felt like the last knot, the one that held the day.

On the walk toward her mushroom cottage, Lila's hands remembered the grass ties and the way four efforts had made one flat green place. She could have pinned the blanket alone with enough rocks and time, but it would have been a struggle instead of a game, and the picnic would have started grumpy. Asking had turned the breeze into a joke they could all answer. She hummed as the path darkened into warm gold. Ferns on the wayside seemed to nod at their woven cousins waiting under the oak.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Lila rinsed berry juice from the bark cup and set it upside down. She could picture the rolled blanket under the tree, a team project sleeping until morning. The small fox had followed her as far as the gate of ferns and then trotted home with the others. Lila smiled at the empty step, which did not feel empty at all. Teamwork, she thought, was a corner for every set of paws, a vote for the smallest voice, and a blanket that only lay flat when nobody had to hold the whole wind alone.
