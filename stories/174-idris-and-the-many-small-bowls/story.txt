Idris could smell the lentil soup from the gate: cumin, lemon, and a softness like warm earth. Uncle Samir carried the pot with two towel-wrapped hands, and Idris carried the stack of small bowls that clicked quietly against one another. The town green held the harvest festival in a low, friendly way. Blankets made islands. A lute player picked notes that never tried to be the loudest thing in the grass. Lanterns rested on crates, their glow puddled and calm.

"Big bowls or small?" Uncle Samir asked as they set the pot on a trivet. Idris knew which choice would give him more at once. His stomach voted for big. He looked at the green, at neighbors still arriving with careful steps and covered plates. "Small," he said. "Then the pot can meet more people. I can have a small bowl too." Uncle Samir's eyes approved. "A wise host lives in you," he said. They put a card in front: lentil soup with lemon. Mrs. Okonkwo, arranging bread, nodded. "Small bowls keep a line kind," she said.

Idris ladled with a deep spoon that wanted to be generous. He paused at halfway up each bowl. The soup coated the spoon and let go slowly. Steam warmed his wrist. "Enough to warm, not enough to spill," Uncle Samir coached. Idris repeated it under his breath until it became a rhythm. He handed the first bowl to an older neighbor with both hands, the way you pass something that matters. "Lemon is bright," he warned gently. "Cumin is warm." The neighbor tasted and sighed a happy sigh. "This is the size of comfort," she said.

People came without crowding. Idris kept the stack neat. When a bowl wobbled, he steadied the table, not the person's fingers. Bread moved along beside the soup. A flute joined the lute, and the two instruments seemed to share the air the way the bowls shared the pot. Idris's own hunger walked in circles, patient and not forgotten. He told it, silently, that last was a kind of honor. Mrs. Okonkwo brought him a sip of water so his voice would stay friendly. The water was cool. He thanked her and ladled on.

Halfway down the pot, he saw the rich part thicken. He stirred once, slowly, so the bottom would not keep all the goodness. "Everyone gets some of the soft center," he said. Uncle Samir hummed yes. A child-sized breeze carried the lemon smell across the blanket and brought back the smell of buttered bread. Idris did not take a spoonful for himself yet. He wiped a drip, set the towel straight, and smiled at a newcomer who looked unsure. "There is room in the pot and room on the blanket," he said. The newcomer sat.

When the late walkers arrived with their lanterns still glowing, Idris was glad of the small bowls. The pot met them too. He scraped nothing harshly. He simply gave what was left in even shares, a little smaller still, and explained with a smile. No one frowned. One man said, "A little of a good thing is a full hello." Idris liked that more than a mountain of soup would have liked him. He took his own bowl last, and Uncle Samir took a small one beside him. They sat on the blanket with knees touching the grass.

The soup in his bowl was hot at the center and kind at the edge. Lemon woke his tongue. Cumin followed like a drum behind a song. He ate slowly so he would taste the choice he had made. The lute found a lullaby walk. Lanterns made coins of light on the pot's side. Mrs. Okonkwo passed a slice of bread, and Idris dipped a corner. "You served like a person who trusts there is enough," she said. He nodded, mouth full, and felt the trust had been practiced, not just spoken.

They washed bowls at the water station until the stack shone. Idris's arms were tired in a clean way. A few lentils dotted the towel, and he shook them into the scrap pail. Uncle Samir dried the pot and let Idris carry the empty bowls home, lighter and still faintly lemony. The lane was quiet. Their steps matched. Idris thought of the big bowl he had not taken, and it did not feel like a loss. It felt like many warm hands.

Idris noticed one bowl had a chip on the rim. He set it aside for his own use later, so a guest would have a smooth edge. "The host can take the chip," he told Uncle Samir. "The guest can take the easy sip." Uncle Samir touched his shoulder. A man returning his empty bowl said the lemon had made the evening feel tidy. Idris rinsed that bowl at once in the waiting basin so the stack would not grow sticky. Warm water, a soft cloth, the scent of cumin lifting and fading. Service, he was learning, continued after the thank you.

When his own turn came, the chipped bowl was ready, and he did not mind the little moon of missing glaze. The soup met his spoon just the same. He ate a lentil that was whole and soft, then another. Uncle Samir told a short story about a feast where the smallest cups were passed first and somehow everyone laughed more. Idris could believe it. Around them the lute grew quieter, matching people who were full enough. Crumbs and lemon peel rested on a napkin like a tiny map of the meal. Idris folded the map gently and carried it to the scrap pail, careful not to spill a single peel under the soft light.

At the door he set the bowls down carefully so they would not click too loudly. Uncle Samir lit the house lamp. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and Idris looked at the clean stack, hoping the next pot he met would also be shared in small, welcoming bowls.
