Farah stopped where the sand turned into dock boards. The boards were dry, pale, and close together, and still her stomach did a small fish-flip. The little wooden boat she loved, the one with the rubbed brown bow, had drifted along the dock until its yellow painter rope lay in a loose smile on the planks, almost within reach and somehow very far. The water was calm, clear enough to show pebbles, no taller than a gentle breath against the posts. Auntie stood at Farah's shoulder. "We can do this as a team of steps," Auntie said. "None of them has to be a leap."

Farah named the steps so they would shrink. Step one was putting her foot on the first board. She did it. The board answered with a warm, ordinary creak. Step two was the next foot. Step three was walking to the second post while her hand followed the rail. A gull sat on the third post and did not flap or scold. It shifted aside as if saving her room, then stayed to watch like a quiet friend. "Thank you for not making sudden noises," Farah said. Her voice shook only on the word sudden. She laughed at herself, and the laugh helped.

The yellow rope was soft from use, bright as a peel, one end still fastened to the boat's bow cleat and the free end waiting. Farah knelt because kneeling felt steadier than bending from the sky. She kept her toes tucked so they would not dance over the edge. Auntie's hand rested on the rail, not grabbing Farah, just a fact of company. "I am here," Auntie said. "The boat is here. The rope is only a rope." Farah breathed in salt and sun-warm wood. She reached. Her fingers missed, brushed the yellow, and caught it on the second try. The rope was real. Her brave moment was real too.

She pulled the boat the last hand's length until the fender met the dock with a soft polite bump. No rush, no splash in her face. "Now the tie," she said, mostly to her hands. Auntie had taught her the friendly knot on a chair at home, using a dressing-gown belt. The dock was not a chair, but the rope remembered the same loops. Around the post, over, through, snug. Farah talked herself through it. "Around the post, which is only wood. Over, like a scarf. Through, like threading a bead." The knot settled. She tested it with a small tug. The boat stayed, content as a cat in a patch of sun.

Farah stayed kneeling a moment longer because standing up too fast felt like leaving a finished picture. Her heart was still quick, but it was quick like hopping, not like fleeing. "I thought the rope might yank me," she admitted. "It did not. It was waiting to be useful." Auntie nodded. "A lot of brave things are ordinary jobs wearing a nervous costume." They looked at the knot together. It was not the fanciest knot in the cove. It was correct, and it was hers. The gull inspected it, found no snack, and sailed off to a tide pool rock to resume being wild.

They walked to the end of the dock and back, Farah practicing the creaks until they became a song she knew. On the second pass she untied and retied the yellow rope once, not because it was wrong, but because practice turns a flutter into a skill. Her fingers were surer. She remembered to keep the loose end coiled so nobody would trip, a kindness tucked inside the courage. Below, a starfish glowed faintly in the water by a post, unbothered by knots and lessons. Farah was glad her work had not asked the pool to change.

Uncle waved from the beach with two mugs of something warm. Farah waved back with the hand that had held the rope, and the waving did not unbalance her. She told the story on the sand without skipping the fish-flip in her stomach. Uncle listened as if the story were a voyage. "Next time the flutter will have to share space with the memory of the knot," he said. Farah liked that. Memories could be co-pilots. She sipped, and the drink tasted like milk and a little honey, and her knees felt like they belonged to her again.

Before they left, she checked the boat one last time. The yellow rope held its curve. The bow did not wander. She put a palm on the rail and felt only wood, warm and dry. "I can be nervous and still finish," she said. Auntie bumped her shoulder. "That is the whole definition I like best." They shook sand from their shoes. The cove kept its gentle clap against the posts. Farah looked back once, not with worry, with recognition. The dock was a place she had been now, fully, on purpose.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when Farah coiled a spare bit of yellow practice rope on the cottage hook. She did not need to prove anything else today. The boat was safe, the knot was tidy, and her stomach had gone back to being a stomach. She watched the light sit on the floorboards and imagined they were a tiny dock inside the house. Courage had not been a shout. It had been steps, a kneel, a loop, and Auntie's calm voice nearby. Farah smiled, washed honey from her fingers, and let the evening be easy.
