Selma had a jar of water ready and a picture in her mind of a table crowded with color. She wanted armfuls of clover, purple and white, enough to make the kitchen look like the meadow had come indoors for a visit. She took the jar and the garden scissors and stepped onto the path. The hum met her at once, warm and busy, and she slowed because Flora had told her the path was the place for people.

From a good distance she could see the bees at work. They entered flowers and left again, legs dusty, routes important. The clover was not only pretty. It was a pantry that moved. Selma lowered the scissors until they pointed at the ground. "If I take an armful," she said to the grown-up, who had come to walk with her, "I take their cups and their maps." The grown-up nodded. "The meadow can share a little at the edges on some days. Today the patch is full of workers. Leaving it is a way to care."

Selma felt the bouquet picture tug at her, the way a lovely idea tugs even after you know better. She walked the long way around, still far from the hum, and looked for fallen petals already let go by the wind. She found three. They were beautiful and too few to pretend they were an armful. She put them in her pocket as treasures, not as a harvest. The scissors stayed shut. The jar of water would have another job.

Flora was on the porch with her stripe drawing. Selma sat beside her and explained the almost-bouquet. "I wanted the meadow in the kitchen," she said. "The meadow is already using itself." Flora slid a spare paper over. "We can make a bouquet that does not pick anyone." They cut paper blooms, purple and white, and gave them green paper stems. Selma drew tiny bees beside the blooms, far to the edge of the page, so even the picture remembered distance. They tied the stems with a bit of yarn and stood the paper flowers in the jar.

The jar looked festive in a new way. It would not wilt by supper. It would not empty a pantry. Selma set it on the table and turned it until the paper bees faced the window, as if they too preferred a view to a touch. She told Corin, who came in hungry, that real clover was still outside doing its work. Corin saluted the paper bouquet with a cup and said the picnic list would never include armfuls of working flowers.

In the late light Selma walked back to the path without scissors. The hum was softer and just as busy. She counted seven bees and then stopped counting so she would not creep closer for number eight. A rabbit nibbled grass far from the clover, on Delphine's shared plan, and Selma left that story alone too. Nature was a neighborhood of different jobs. Hers, today, was to admire and to leave.

She poured the jar's extra water on the herb bed when she changed her mind about nothing at all, and the paper flowers stayed dry on the table. The three fallen petals went into the notebook Flora and Orhan sometimes shared, a tiny proof that the meadow gives gifts when it is ready.

Selma took the shut scissors back to their hook and felt the click as a promise. The jar of paper flowers caught the late light and looked cheerful beside the bread box, where Bastian's food card also lived. Two different lessons sat near each other: do not give ducks the wrong snack, and do not take the bees' pantry for a table. Selma read both and nodded. Nature around a home is a set of careful recipes. She could follow them and still have a beautiful room.

Flora added a paper bee no bigger than a fingernail to the bouquet's edge. They left a gap between the bee and the blooms, even on paper, because the habit of distance mattered in play as much as on the path. Selma stood at the door and listened. The hum was faint and fine. She did not step toward it. An empty cutting hand, a full paper jar, and a meadow still working: that was the shape of her care, and it was enough to make the evening sweet.

Selma carried a cup of water to the porch and drank it herself instead of pouring a toast toward the clover. The bees did not need a ceremony. They needed flowers left in place. She told the paper bouquet good night as if it were a toy, not a substitute she regretted. The real meadow could stay outside in the dark, humming more softly, doing its own work while the house grew quiet.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the paper bouquet. Selma touched a yarn stem and listened toward the darkening clover she would not disturb. Caring for nature, she thought, can look like an empty hand and a full table made of paper, while the real flowers stay outside, feeding the small workers who make the meadow sweet.
