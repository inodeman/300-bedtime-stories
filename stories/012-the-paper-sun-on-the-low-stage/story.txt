The paper sun lay on the classroom table, bigger than a platter, yellow at the middle and orange at the rays. The class had colored it in turns, and it still smelled faintly of wax. Mr. Adeyemi stood by the low stage, which was only two steps high and used for songs and good news. A blank square of wall above it waited. "We need a hanger," he said. "Someone who will take the sun up, hold it level, and pin the corners so it can shine on our play." Several children looked at their shoes in a thinking way. Rosie looked at the sun. Her paintings were usually small. Her hands knew paper. They did not yet know the stage. Still, the sun was partly hers. A ray near the bottom had her careful orange. "I can do that," she said. Her voice surprised her by sounding like she meant it.

Mr. Adeyemi handed her four brass pins with soft round heads. "I will stand beside the steps," he said. "The class will keep their bodies in their squares on the rug. You are the one on the stage." Rosie liked having the shape of the job. She carried the sun in both hands so a ray would not bend. The paper whispered. Up the two steps, the air felt the same as below, only slightly prouder. She turned to the wall. The class was a field of faces, not a storm, just faces of people who had also colored things. Rosie breathed in the orange-peel smell of the room and breathed out. "Top first," she told herself.

She held the sun against the wall with her forearm, the way you hold a parcel while finding a key. The first pin went in at the top corner, a gentle push into the soft board. It did not need force. It needed aim. "Is it level?" she asked, without turning all the way around. Nico, from the rug, held his hands up like a frame. "A little lower on the pin side." Rosie eased the paper and pressed the pin home. The second pin was easier. Her fingers had learned the brass warmth. She felt a smile arrive and let it stay. Courage could include a smile. It did not have to be a tight mouth and a grim job. It could be a sun and a careful thumb.

The bottom pins were lower, so she knelt on the stage. The boards were smooth and dusty in the lines between them. She blew a puff of dust aside and said, "Excuse me, dust," which made Lina giggle in a friendly way. Rosie pinned the left bottom, then the right. She checked the middle. The sun did not buckle. The rays spread evenly, a yellow hello above the place where songs would be. She stood and stepped back one shoe-length, still on the stage, and looked. It was straight. Her heart, which had been tap-dancing, settled into a walk. "It is up," she said. The class clapped in the quiet way they used indoors, palms that patted more than smacked. The sound was like rain on a leaf.

Mr. Adeyemi did not fix any pin. There was nothing to fix. "Rosie," he said, "you gave our play a sun." He invited her to show how she had kept the paper from creasing: forearm as a shelf, pins from the top, ask a friend to sight the level. Rosie showed them with an empty hand, miming, and her voice stayed. She had not known she could teach a thing she had just learned. The newness did not fall off. It fitted itself to her, like a sleeve rolled the right number of times. She came down the two steps and felt the floor welcome her without any fuss.

At rehearsal, the paper sun watched. Rosie stood in the chorus row and sang the short song about markets and loaves. She did not stare at her pins, but she knew they were holding. When a ray's shadow fell across the stage in a pretty way, she noticed and saved the picture in her mind. Afterward Lina said, "If we make a paper moon next month, will you hang that too?" Rosie thought of the kneeling and the level and the clap like rain. "Yes," she said. "And you can pass me the pins. Passing is part of hanging." Lina agreed at once. A job shared at the edges was still a job you had been brave enough to start.

Rosie washed yellow from her thumb at the sink. The soap smelled green and squeaked. She dried her hands on the rough towel and helped stack the coloring boards. The room emptied slowly, chairs up, lights still bright. Mr. Adeyemi took down nothing. The sun would stay for the week. "You may look at it each morning," he said, "and remember that your hands know a new road." Rosie liked the word road better than test. She had not been tested. She had been trusted, and she had answered.

On the walk home she told her father about the forearm shelf and the dust she had excused. He listened as if the story were a song with four pins for notes. They bought nothing and needed nothing. The day already felt furnished. Rosie swung her bag and did not swing it into anyone. At the corner she could almost see, in her imagination, the paper sun still level above the low stage, doing its quiet job.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the classroom as the afternoon folded, and Rosie, home by then, pictured that glow keeping the paper company. She set her bag down and stretched her fingers, proud of their new skill. Courage had been two steps, four pins, and a voice that asked, "Is it level?" The sun would shine on the play because she had been willing to try a part her hands had never held before.
