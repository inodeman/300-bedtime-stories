Rain had left a wide, shallow puddle across the lane, brown at the edges and silver in the middle, where a piece of sky seemed to rest. It was not deep. It was not a river. It was only a puddle that liked to borrow shoes. Rita held a tin of cookies wrapped in a clean cloth. The tin was warm, and the cloth smelled like butter. Soren stood beside her with two short planks from the dry pile under the eaves. They could see Grandpa's porch just beyond the shine, his rocking chair waiting, and they wanted the cookies to arrive dry.

"We could go around the long way," Soren said. The long way passed the prickly hedge and took the whole song they had been humming. Rita shook her head gently. "Or we could make a little bridge of planks, low and wide, with Grandpa watching so it stays steady." A drop fell from a leaf onto the puddle and drew a round ripple. Their faces appeared in the water for a moment, wobbly and serious, then a cloud changed the picture. Rita laughed. "Even the puddle is making faces," she said. "It can keep my reflection. It cannot keep my socks."

Grandpa came to his side of the water with a careful smile and dry slippers. "I like a bridge that is only as tall as a pancake stack," he said. "Show me the planks." They were smooth, not splintery, short enough to lift together. "We will set them side by side, not one skinny line," he said. "You two carry. I will guide the ends. Fingers on top of the wood, not underneath, so nothing pinches when it settles." The wood smelled like rain that had dried. It felt cool and heavy in a way they could still manage if they shared the weight.

They laid the first plank. It touched both edges of the puddle, a pale path over the sky-water. Soren stepped on with one shoe, slowly. The plank rocked. A little wave slapped the side and darkened a corner of the wood. He stepped back at once onto the lane. "It has a wobble," he said. Rita put her hand on his arm, not to stop him, but to say she had seen it too. Grandpa nodded. "Good stop. A bridge should feel boring. Boring means steady." The cookies clicked softly in the tin, as if they agreed.

Rita looked at the second plank. "It wants a partner," she said. "One plank is a guess. Two planks are a team." They lifted the second board together. Grandpa set flat garden stones at each end, broad stones that did not teeter, so the wood could not kick up. He pressed with his foot. The planks answered with a solid, quiet sound, not a rock. Soren tried again, both feet, arms out like a slow bird, then down at his sides when he felt the firmness. "Boring," he announced, proud. Rita grinned. "The best kind."

She started across with the tin held in two hands. Soren walked just behind, close enough to share a balance if she asked, not so close that their shoes bumped. The puddle showed their moving legs in pieces. Water whispered under the wood but did not touch them. Halfway, Rita felt a tiny shift and paused. "Your turn to be the steady one," she said. Soren put a hand lightly on the tin, and they took the last steps together, counting under their breath. One, two, three, porch. The cookies were dry. Their socks were dry. The bridge behind them looked small and exactly right.

Grandpa poured milk into cups that had been waiting. The cookies were round, with sugar that sparkled and a soft middle. They told him about the wobble and the stop. "That stop was teamwork too," he said. "Not only the carrying." Rita brushed a crumb from the cloth and folded it into a square. Soren looked back at the planks. A leaf had landed on them like a little green traveler. He let it stay. The puddle was already thinner at one edge, giving the sky back bit by bit. The lane smelled like wet stone and sweet butter.

They agreed to lift the planks home before supper, so the lane would be clear for anyone else. Grandpa helped again. The wood was a little damper on the underside and still safe to hold on top. Rita carried one end. Soren carried the other. They walked in step without planning it. At the woodpile they stacked the planks where the next rain could not surprise them, and Rita tapped the tin, empty now, so it made a happy hollow note.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when they came inside, lighting the empty cookie tin on the table. Their socks were still dry, which made Soren wiggle his toes and laugh. Rita looked out at the lane, where the puddle held one last bright scrap of sky beside the place their small bridge had been. The evening felt steady, like two planks lying side by side, stronger because they had not tried to do the job alone.
