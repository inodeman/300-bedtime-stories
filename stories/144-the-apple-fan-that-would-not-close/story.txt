Felix pedaled slowly along the lane with both hands on the grips and a full basket on the front of his bicycle. Mama rode just behind him, close enough to talk. The common was only a few minutes from home, past the post box and the low hedge. In the basket, wrapped in a clean cloth, were more apple slices than one boy could eat, plus crackers in a tin and a little jar of sunflower seeds. "You packed for a team," Mama said. "Did you mean to?" Felix glanced down at the bouncing cloth. "I meant to pack enough so I would not be hungry. I think my hands got excited."

On the grass, kites were already up, but the wind was polite, not wild. Children held strings and laughed without teasing. Felix set his bicycle on its kickstand where the sign said bikes rest. He opened the cloth. The apple slices smelled sweet and a little like the kitchen. A girl named Pim, who lived above the bakery ovens but was just Pim to him, peered over. "That is a lot of apples," she said. "My string-hand is busy, and my snack is still on the kitchen table. I forgot it." She did not look sad in a heavy way. She looked hopeful and a bit shy to ask.

Felix felt the old pinch that sometimes said mine. He had cut the slices himself. He had even arranged them in a fan. Then he looked at the basket, which was so full the cloth could not close. "Would you like a fan of three?" he asked. "I brought extra by accident, and accidents can be for sharing." Pim's smile arrived fast. "Yes, please. I will not take the whole basket." Felix liked that she said it. He handed her three slices on a napkin. She ate one and saved two on the napkin's corner, tidy as a picnic.

A little farther off, twins named Bo and Ness sat with a kite that would not lift yet. Their grown-up was tying a shoe. "We have water," Bo called, "but our bread went to the ducks by mistake. The ducks were very pleased." Ness giggled. "The ducks did not share back." Felix walked the tin over, staying where Mama could see him. "Crackers?" he offered. "They are plain and a little salty." The twins took two each and said thank you with cracker crumbs on their lips. Felix still had a heap. The basket looked more comfortable, like a shirt that was no longer too tight.

He returned to his bicycle and found Mama sitting on the rug. "You are having a sharing morning," she said. "How does the basket feel?" Felix lifted it an inch. "Lighter," he said. "And my inside feels less crowded too. I was going to eat past full just so nothing was left. That is a silly plan." Mama nodded. "Food is for people, not for proving you can finish it." Felix kept a good plate for himself and for Mama. The apples still tasted like his kitchen. Sharing had not made them smaller in flavor.

Pim came back with a kite tail in her hand and a handful of daisy heads that were not pulled from a protected bed, only picked from the mowing edge where the sign said picking was fine. "Trade?" she asked. "You gave me apples. I can show you the knot that keeps a tail on." Felix did not need a trade, but he liked the friendship in it. "Show me," he said. They worked the knot together. When his own small kite, the spare one from the basket, rose a little, he laughed. "The apples started this," he said. "The apples and the extra."

Before they rode home, Felix checked the cloth. Enough remained for a snack on the step, and not so much that the basket would tip. He gave the last sunflower seeds to the twins' grown-up to share out fairly. "Tell the ducks they already had their bread," he joked. Everyone laughed. Mama clipped his helmet strap again, though he had not taken it far off. "Ready?" she asked. Felix patted the handlebar. "Ready. The basket is a better size now."

They rolled home past the same hedge. Felix did not race. He liked hearing the quieter rattle. "Next time I might pack extra on purpose," he said. "Not a mountain. Just a little more than me." Mama smiled at the road ahead. "Then your bicycle can be a kindness with wheels." Felix thought of Pim's napkin and the twins' crumbs and decided that a basket was a good place to keep more than one person's joy.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Felix rinsed the tin. He set one apple seed, flat and brown, on a bit of paper to look at, not to plant in the wrong season. "Sharing made room," he told Mama. "Room in the basket and room on the grass." Mama hung the cloth to dry. "And room for a knot lesson," she said. Felix watched the lantern light touch the bicycle bell on the table. Tomorrow the common would still be there, close as the lane, and he would know how to pack.
