The little skiff sat on the damp sand like a wooden spoon set down between stirs. Its name, painted in blue, was Pebble. Nia had one hand on the bow and a plan to pull. The harbor water was still a walk away, thin and shining, busy being far. Uncle Sol checked a rope and did not grab the other side. "Tide is coming," he said. "If we wait, the water will do the heavy hello. If we drag now, we will plow a frown into the sand and tire out two good backs." Nia looked at the distance. It was not enormous. It was enough to make a person want to start. Her fingers tapped the gunwale. The wood was warm on top and cool underneath, and it smelled like salt and varnish.

"How long is a tide?" she asked. Uncle Sol pointed at a dark band of seaweed on a post. "When the water touches that lower nail, Pebble will feel it. We can time the wait with useful things." Nia liked a wait that had a shape. They set the oars ready in the locks but did not row the air. They checked the drain plug. They shook a sand flea back onto the beach, a tiny hopper going about its own day. Nia sat on the side of the skiff with her feet on a board so she would not dent the hull with impatient heels. The harbor smelled like wet rope and far-off bread. A gull watched, then lost interest, which was fine. Not every bird needed a role.

Nia tried a small pull anyway, just to know. Pebble answered with a stubborn hush and moved a thumb's width. Sand sighed. Nia stopped. "You are heavier than my plan," she told the boat, patting it so the words would not be rude. Uncle Sol nodded. "Boats are honest about weight. Water is the friend that makes them light." They walked the line of the incoming ripple instead of hauling. Nia placed a stick at the water's edge. They went back to the skiff and coiled a painter, the little front rope, into a neat spiral. When they looked again, the stick was an island. Nia felt something unclench in her shoulders. The sea was not late. It was on the way, which was its job.

She used the minutes to learn a knot Uncle Sol called a slipped hitch, easy to undo. Her fingers were clumsy, then less clumsy. "Again," she said, and did it again without being told. A ferry mumbled at the far dock. Its wake would arrive later as a gentle wash. Nia imagined Pebble lifting, not by a yank, but by a rising palm. She told Uncle Sol that picture. "That is good seeing," he said. "You are already riding the idea. The hull can catch up." They ate two crackers from a tin. The crackers were salty, which matched the air. Crumbs went into the tin, not onto the sand, so gulls would not be invited into a squabble. The water touched a new line of pebbles and made them darker, one by one, like lighting small lamps.

When the first thin sheet of water slid under Pebble's stern, Nia stood up and did not push. She watched. The boat made a tiny creak, the sound of wood thinking. Another sheet came, lace-edged and quiet. Pebble shifted, barely. Nia put a hand on the bow to guide, not to drag. "I am the usher," she said. "The tide is the guest of honor doing the lift." Uncle Sol laughed. "Ushers are patient people." More water gathered. The skiff grew lighter under her palm, as if it had taken a breath. Then, with a soft kiss of a sound, it was afloat. The frown in the sand never had to be dug. The boat simply stopped being a thing on the beach and became a thing in the harbor.

They walked it out until the water reached Nia's shins, cool as a bracelet. She climbed in when Uncle Sol held the side. The oars dipped. Pebble moved with almost no argument. Nia rowed three strokes and paused, feeling the glide continue after the effort, which was another lesson in not overdoing. "We waited, and now we are early to the fun," she said. The sentence made her grin. Across the harbor, masts drew pale lines on the sky. She trailed a finger in the water and it braided behind them. Patience had not been doing nothing. It had been knots and crackers and a stick that became an island and a hand that guided instead of yanked.

They rowed home on the same rising water, easier than any drag. On the beach, the place where Pebble had rested was only a smooth wet oval. Nia liked that the sand had forgiven the thumb's width of her test pull. Uncle Sol let her tie the painter to the rail with the slipped hitch. It held. She could undo it later with one tug on the loose end, a knot that understood leaving as well as staying. Her calves glittered with drops. She shook them and laughed when the drops made a private rain.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the boathouse window as they hung the oars. Nia looked back at the harbor, fuller than it had been, doing its slow sure work. She had not forced the little skiff down the sand. She had waited for the tide to lift it, and the waiting had turned into a float. Patience, she thought, was a kind of tide inside a person, arriving stroke by quiet stroke until the heavy thing grew light.
