Samir was early to the dock on purpose. The weekly row mattered more than a perfect start, and being early was how he told the lighthouse keeper, without a speech, that their friendship had a place on the calendar. The little wooden boat waited, cream-colored inside, with a puddle of last night's dew. Samir bailed it with a cup. A gull stood on the bow like a volunteer lookout, then politely hopped to a piling when Samir needed the space. The morning smelled of wet rope and sweet mud. "We will go even if the breeze is lazy," Samir told the boat. "That is the deal."

The keeper arrived with one oar over his shoulder and a puzzled smile. "The partner oar has gone on an adventure," he said. "I found this other one in the shed. It is longer, and it is the color of tea, and it does not match our short blue oar even a little." Samir held them side by side. The blue oar was smooth where his hands knew it. The tea-colored oar was heavier near the blade. A mismatched pair could pull the boat in a rude circle. Samir felt a dip of disappointment. Then he looked at the keeper's face, hopeful and a bit apologetic, and the dip filled in. "We can learn a lopsided song," Samir said. "Friends can row a lopsided song."

They pushed off into water so calm the lighthouse made a second lighthouse underneath them. Samir took the long oar. The first strokes proved the joke. His side shoved harder, and the bow wandered toward the tide pools. "Your oar is enthusiastic," the keeper said. "My blue one is thoughtful." They paused, laughing, blades dripping silver coins. Samir shortened his stroke. The keeper lengthened his. They counted, not like a race, like a shared recipe. "Pull, and gentle," Samir said. The boat agreed on the third try and drew a line that was almost straight. The gull followed for a roof's length, then peeled away to its own errands, still wild, still helpful by simply not blocking the channel.

Halfway along the usual loop, Samir's wrists began to argue with the heavier wood. He did not pretend otherwise. "The long oar is tiring my side," he said. "Could we swap before I start rowing grumpy?" The keeper nodded at once. They traded carefully, blades flat so nobody got a splash in the face. Now the keeper hummed when he pulled, saving effort with rhythm, and Samir's blue oar felt like a familiar spoon. The boat wobbled, found them again, and continued. Honesty about tired arms was part of the friendship, Samir thought. So was changing the plan without making the other person feel like a problem.

They drifted a minute beside a rock pool to watch a starfish the color of butter rest in the clear. Neither of them reached in. The oars lay across their knees like two different sentences that still made a paragraph. "Do you mind that it is not our usual pair?" the keeper asked. Samir considered, because a real answer was a gift. "I mind only a little, the way I mind a pebble in a shoe before I shake it out. I do not mind being here with you. The loop is the promise. The oars are just oars." The keeper's shoulders eased. "Then we are keeping the right thing."

They finished the curve past the sandcastle beach, where a collapsed tower had become a soft hill, and past the bell post, quiet in the clear air. A little swell, no higher than a hand, asked them to pay attention. Samir called the counts. The keeper matched him. The boat rose and settled, never in trouble, just alive. Spray salted Samir's lip and tasted like the whole bright day. He thought of other friendships, the kind that need everything even, and decided he liked this kind better. Even was nice. Adjusting was care in work clothes.

At the dock they shipped the oars and sat while the boat bumped gently, in no hurry to be finished. Samir's palms were pink and happy. "Next week I can help sand the long oar's handle so it grips," he offered. "Or we can find the lost partner. Or we can become famous for our uneven style." The keeper laughed. "All three ideas sound like you will come back." "I will come back," Samir said, and the words sat solidly in the boat between them. The gull returned to the piling, as if checking the promise off a list, then sailed away over the dune grass.

They carried the mismatched oars up together, Samir on the blue, the keeper on the tea-colored, footsteps matching better than the wood did. The shed received them side by side. Samir wiped dew and salt from the seat so the next rower, even if it was only them, would find a dry welcome. Friendship, he decided, was not a set of identical things. It was two people keeping a small weekly courage, fixing what could be fixed, and laughing when the boat drew a curve instead of a ruler line.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as Samir and the keeper shared a mug of warm water with lemon at the cottage table. Their hands smelled like oar wood. Through the glass the cove held the path they had drawn, already erasing itself into shine, which was fine. The path was never the souvenir. The souvenir was the promise still intact, a little stronger for having been uneven. Samir lifted his mug. "To thoughtful and enthusiastic," he said. The keeper clinked back, gentle so nothing would spill. Outside, both oars rested. Inside, the friendship did not need them to match.
