Malik held a cardboard arrow up and felt the breeze try to take it from his fingers. The morning was bright and full of flapping sounds. Rita stood by a basket of damp sheets that smelled like soap and rainwater even though the sky was clear. The clothesline hummed. A shirt already up there had twisted itself into a shy bundle. "If we knew where the wind was going," Malik said, "we could hang the big sheets so they do not hug each other." His arrow pointed at the fence, then at his nose, then at nothing, because his hand was the thing moving it.

Rita squinted at the leaves. They nodded toward the lane, then changed their minds. "Wind is a fidget," she said. "We need a helper that can fidget honestly." They had a plan that used a cup, a stick, and the arrow. Auntie had agreed to set the stick in a pot of soil so it would not fall on toes. The children would tape the arrow to the cup, and the cup would sit on the stick and turn. "If it sticks or pinches, we stop and call me," Auntie had said. The rule was written in Malik's head in big letters.

They worked on the step. The cardboard was pale and a little fuzzy at the cut edge, which Auntie had smoothed. Malik drew feathers on the tail of the arrow, silly ones, because tools can be funny and still tell the truth. Rita taped the arrow across the cup so one end was longer. The tape made a crackly sound. They blew on it. It wanted to spin and could not, because they were holding it. Curiosity tingled in Malik's elbows. He wanted to know if the real wind agreed with his drawing.

Auntie set the stick in the pot. The soil smelled rich and dark. She slid the cup onto the stick with a small gap so it could turn, and she kept her fingers on the rim. "Hands back," she said, kindly. They stepped back. The arrow twitched, stuck, and pointed at the kitchen door as if the kitchen were a cloud. Rita frowned in a thinking way. "It is grabbing the stick," she said. Malik wanted to yank it. He did not. "Auntie, it is stuck," he said. She lifted the cup, saw a tape edge rubbing, and folded that edge down flat. "Try your eyes, not your tug," she said. The arrow shivered and then swung toward the lane.

They checked the answer with their faces, which is a good instrument if you stand still. Malik closed his eyes. Cool air touched his left cheek first. Rita's hair moved toward the garden, away from the lane, so the breeze was coming from the lane side. The arrow's nose pointed that way too. "It matches," Rita said, delighted. They opened their eyes and laughed because both of them had been standing like statues in a museum of wind. A sheet on the line gave a soft thunderclap of cloth and then calmed.

They hung the laundry with the arrow as their guide. Big sheets on the end that could fly without wrapping the shirts. Socks in a row, toes down, dripping a little coolness onto the grass. The soap smell grew stronger as the sun worked. Whenever the arrow turned, they noticed, and once they moved a towel that had started to brush the fence. Curiosity kept them visiting the pot, asking the arrow questions. "Are you sure?" Malik asked it. The arrow did not speak, but it pointed, and their cheeks agreed. That was enough science for one morning.

By the time the bread was ready indoors, the sheets were only damp at the hems. Rita pressed a corner to her cheek and felt coolness leaving. Malik drew the arrow's direction on a card and wrote, "Lane wind today." Tomorrow it might be a garden wind. The card would help them remember that guessing and checking were different. Auntie took the cup off the stick and set it on the shelf, arrow still attached, so the next breeze could have a job. The pot of soil stayed, a round brown foot holding the stick like a patient friend.

They folded what was dry and left what was not, without rushing the sun. The basket grew warm and sweet-smelling. Rita shook a sock and a tiny seed of grass flew out and went nowhere important. Malik watched the leaves, already practicing the cheek test, storing it in his body. The invention was simple. The noticing was the real treasure, light as cardboard and just as useful.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while the last towel finished its slow drying outside. Malik set the wind card on the sill, and the arrow on the shelf pointed nowhere in particular, resting. Rita touched her cheek and remembered the cool answer. Curiosity had spent the day outdoors and come back indoors, tidy and glad, ready to ask the breeze a new question in the morning.
