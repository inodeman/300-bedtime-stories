Orla stood in the middle of the common with a kite that refused to be a kite. The morning was bright and almost windless. Grass stood still. The pear-shaped tail hung like a sleepy scarf. Grandpa held the winder and did not look rushed. "There is no breeze to borrow," Orla said. "I already ran. Running without wind just makes a girl with a string." She was not cross at Grandpa. She was cross at the air for being so calm. Her shoes were dusty from three short tries.

Grandpa sat so their eyes met. "Patience is not doing nothing," he said. "It is doing the right small things until the moment arrives. Feel your cheek. The wind tells the truth there before it tells the kite." Orla closed her eyes. One cheek was warm. The other was only slightly less warm. "I feel sunshine," she said. "Sunshine is not a string-lifter." Grandpa nodded. "Then we wait for a cooler touch. While we wait, we can check the knot, drink water, and watch the grass tips. They will move first."

Orla checked the knot. It was a bow, firm and fair. She drank. She lay on her stomach and stared at the grass from ant height. A single blade trembled, then the tremble vanished. "A rumor of wind," she whispered. She did not leap up. She had leaped too soon last time, and the kite had dragged like a mop. "I will count twenty breaths," she decided. Grandpa counted with her, not fast. At breath twelve the rumor returned and brushed her cheek like a cool fingertip. "Now?" she asked. "Now we stand ready, not yanking," Grandpa said.

They stood. Orla held the kite up by its center stick. Grandpa gave the string a little room. The breeze was thin, a ribbon not a river, but it was real. Orla released the kite at the moment the ribbon strengthened, the way you let go of a swing when the push is true. The kite rose to the height of the hedge, dipped, and stayed. "It is thinking," Orla said. She did not jerk. She let the string talk. The kite climbed another stretch and wagged its tail, pleased with itself. Orla's grin was so wide it almost bumped her eyes.

"See," Grandpa said. "You did not argue with the air. You kept it company." Orla fed the string in small gifts. A child nearby started to sprint with a flat kite and then saw Orla's quiet hands and slowed. "Are you waiting?" the child asked. "I was," Orla said. "Now I am matching. You can feel the cheek test. It does not cost anything." The child tried, eyes shut, and smiled when the cool touch came. Their grown-up nodded thanks across the grass. Orla felt patience spill over the way extra lemonade spills into a second cup, on purpose.

The breeze lasted long enough for a song she hummed, then softened. The kite descended like a leaf that had decided to be polite. Orla wound the string as it came, no tangle, because she was not grabbing. "I am not sad it sat down," she said, surprised at herself. "I saw it fly. If I had yanked at the start, I would have seen a mop." Grandpa laughed. "Patience lets a thing be what it is, when it is." They sat and ate oat biscuits. The common smelled like dry grass and orange peel from someone's snack.

A stronger puff arrived while they were still chewing. Orla looked at Grandpa. "Second chance?" "If your arms are willing," he said. They launched again, smoother, because her body remembered the calm. The kite found the hedge height quickly and then a little more. Orla's elbows stayed soft. She noticed birds using the same breeze, not pushing against it. "We are all passengers of the air," she said, which sounded like a Grandpa sentence, and Grandpa looked proud to have his language walking around in her mouth.

When they packed the kite into its sleeve, the grass was still mostly still. The wind had been a visitor, not a promise to stay all day. Orla touched her cheek once more out of habit. "I can use the cheek test while I wait for my turn at the sink," she said. "Or for the little train. Waiting has a texture now." Grandpa squeezed the winder into the bicycle basket. "Then the morning taught your hands and your calendar." They rode home slowly, no race, with the basket clicking a patient beat.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the kite sleeve, which Orla had stood in the corner like a slim guest. She did not unwind the string to fuss. She let the bow she trusted stay tied. "The wind came back because I made room," she told Grandpa, even if she knew the wind had its own plans. Grandpa turned the lantern so the glow reached her dusty shoes by the door. "And you were ready when it did," he said. Outside, the common kept its quiet, saving a breeze for someone else who was learning how to wait.
