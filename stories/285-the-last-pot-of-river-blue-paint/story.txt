Cressida stood before the long community mural, where the town had been painted in friendly colors: the bridge, the hill orchard, the book barge, and a fountain throwing quiet arcs. She held the last pot of river-blue paint. It was small, no bigger than her cupped hands, and the color inside was the same blue as the real river when the sun was kind. The mural painter, a grown-up with a shirt full of polite speckles, said, "That pot is the end of the blue until next week. Use it where the picture needs water most."

Two places needed water. The painted ferry had a window that was still the color of bare plaster, and a younger painter's sky puddle, a little mirror on the path, was only a pencil circle. The younger painter looked at the pot and then at the ground. "I can wait," the child said, though the waiting sat sadly on the words. Cressida felt the pot grow heavier, not in weight, but in deciding. She could make the ferry window glow and leave the puddle empty. The ferry was bigger. Bigger was not the same as only.

"What if the blue can be two things?" Cressida asked. The mural painter tilted their head. "There is not much. A thick brush would drink it." Cressida opened her wooden box and took out the thin liner brush, the one with bristles like a whisper. "I can paint a line, not a puddle of paint. The ferry window can be a frame of blue and a pale wash. The sky puddle can be a small true oval. We share the pot, and we do not glob." The younger painter's shoulders lifted. "I would like a true oval," the child said.

They set the pot on a steady stool, not on the ground where a shoe might find it. Cressida dipped only the tip of the liner. She painted the ferry window as a neat rectangle, then a lighter wash inside, so it looked like glass with river behind it. The blue went farther than she had feared. "Your turn," she said, and held the pot while the younger painter, guided by the grown-up, filled the pencil oval with a careful shine. A little blue remained, enough for a reflection under the painted bridge. "We still have a sip of it," Cressida said, surprised and glad.

She used that sip for the reflection, a short stroke that made the bridge seem to stand in water. Then the pot showed its bottom, shiny and honest. No one scraped it into a mess. "It is finished," said the mural painter. "And both watery places are finished too." The younger painter touched the air near the oval, not the wet paint. "You did not keep it," the child said. Cressida shook her head. "Keeping it would have made one pretty window and one blank sad circle. The mural is a together picture."

They stepped back to the plaza edge. The ferry window caught the eye, and the little puddle caught it again, like a rhyme. Cressida's hands were blue at the thumb, a badge she did not mind. She thought about how sharing was not only handing over a whole thing. Sometimes it was dividing a small thing with a tool that did not waste. The clock tower chimed, and the real river answered with a glitter. The painted river looked related to it now.

The younger painter offered Cressida a clean rag and a smile that was not shy anymore. "Tomorrow I can help you with the green leaves, if the green is small too," the child said. "Yes," said Cressida. "We will use the thin brush again if we must." The mural painter wrote a tiny note on the plan: blue done, shared. The note looked as neat as the window frame. Cressida liked seeing a plan tell the truth about kindness.

People walking toward the night market, still in early light, paused to look. One grown-up said the ferry seemed ready to move, though of course the paint stayed happily still. Cressida did not need the mural to move. She needed it to have room for more than one patch of blue. The last pot had been enough because they had not asked it to be a flood.

Cressida dipped a clean brush in water and painted nothing, only to feel the difference between a thirsty brush and a dripping one. "Next time I will look at the pot before I look at the wall," she said. The younger painter nodded and copied the motion in the air, a thin invisible line. They both laughed, quiet so the wet blue would not get a surprise puff. Sharing, Cressida thought, was a plan you could practice with an empty brush until your hand believed it. The mural kept its new window and its small oval, blue enough, and the empty pot did not feel like a loss.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the mural shed's window, where brushes soaked in jars and the empty blue pot sat like a small bowl of evening. Cressida rinsed the liner brush until the water ran clear. Sharing the last river-blue had given the ferry its window and a friend a puddle of sky. The wall looked complete in that corner, and so did the feeling in her chest.
