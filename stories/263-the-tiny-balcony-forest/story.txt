Deepak pressed his nose close to the balcony glass and frowned in a thinking way, not a cross way. The pots outside looked small against the big sky. “Grandpa Kamal,” he said, “can a garden even live out there? A forest needs room. These pots are only big enough for cups.” Grandpa Kamal slid the door open. Warm air met them, smelling like wet soil and mint. “Come and see the tiny forest,” he said. “It uses a different kind of big.”

They stepped onto the balcony in soft shoes. A tomato plant held one yellow flower like a small lantern of its own. Beans climbed a string in a neat spiral. Mint crowded its pot with leaves that looked crowded and happy. Deepak rubbed a leaf and sniffed. The smell was cool and green. He felt surprise, bright as the flower. He had walked past these pots all week and called them decorations. They were busy living things.

“I want to plant something that becomes a forest today,” Deepak said. He heard the hurry in his own voice. Grandpa Kamal chuckled kindly. “Nature likes steady days more than one giant leap,” he said. “We can plant basil seeds. Today we give them a bed. The leaves will come in their own time. Meanwhile, this mint is already a little forest for bees and for soup.” Deepak touched the soil. It was dark, crumbly, and cool. A worm the size of a noodle curled and went deeper, safe and unbothered. Deepak whispered, “Sorry to disturb your roof.”

They filled a clean pot with soil, leaving room at the top so water would not run off in a rush. Grandpa Kamal showed Deepak how to make shallow lines with one finger. Deepak placed the tiny seeds so they were not piled in one spot. “They need neighbor space,” he said, remembering the beans. He covered them with a veil of soil and patted, not punched. The pat was gentle. He liked being trusted with something so small. His curiosity, which had started as doubt, turned into care.

He watered with the small can. The first pour was too fast and made a muddy river toward the balcony drain. Deepak felt a flick of worry. “I messied their bed,” he said. Grandpa Kamal nodded. “We can mend a bed.” Together they tipped the pot just enough to let extra water out, then pressed the soil smooth again. The second pour was a sprinkle, like rain that has decided to be polite. The seeds stayed put. Deepak’s worry melted into learning. Nature, he saw, needed both water and gentleness.

They made a leaf journal from folded paper. Deepak drew the tomato flower, the bean spiral, and the mint. He counted leaves on one stem. Yesterday’s page, which Grandpa had started, showed that same stem with a tighter curl. Today a new leaf had opened, small as a fingernail and brighter green. Deepak gasped. “It grew while we were inside eating lunch.” Grandpa Kamal smiled. “Growing often happens when we are not staring. Our job is to notice when we come back.” Deepak added the new leaf to the drawing and wrote, in careful letters, TODAY.

A bee visited the tomato flower and left without fuss. Deepak did not wave his arms. He watched. The balcony did not feel small anymore. It felt layered. Soil, roots he could not see, stems, leaves, air, and one careful boy with a watering can. He told Grandpa Kamal the feeling’s name. “I feel amazed,” he said. “And a little patient, even if patient is still wobbly.” Grandpa Kamal squeezed his shoulder. “Wobbly patience still counts. The basil will not mind.” Deepak wanted one more way to help the tiny forest. Grandpa Kamal brought a clear jar and a mint sprig with a stem that could sit in water. “This is not to take the whole plant,” he said. “One sprig can drink and show you roots.” They set the jar on the balcony table, out of the hardest sun. Deepak checked that the leaves were above the water and the stem was below. He drew the sprig in the journal with a question mark under the water line. “I will look tomorrow,” he said. “I feel curious, and I can wait to see.” Grandpa Kamal tapped the page. “Curiosity is a good gardener when it is gentle.”

They washed their hands at the kitchen sink. Dirt made pale rivers and then was gone, but the green smell stayed on Deepak’s fingers. He looked back through the glass at the pot of hidden seeds. He imagined a tiny forest waking under the soil, not today, but soon enough. He set the leaf journal on the table so the family could see the new leaf too. He told the empty kitchen, practicing the words, “Our balcony is a tiny forest. We planted basil. We mended a muddy bed. A new mint leaf opened while we ate lunch.” Saying it made the amazement settle into his bones in a sturdy way. He filled the watering can halfway for the morning so he would not have to hurry. The balcony garden had invited him in, and he had answered with careful hands.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the glass door. Its light touched the rim of the basil pot and made the wet soil shine. Deepak whispered good night to the seeds, the mint, and the yellow flower. The tiny forest was already alive, and he was part of its quiet neighborhood.
