The soup on the stove needed a green smell. Una stood on the balcony with cousin Amina and a small bowl, and the mint looked like a fluffy green cloud that could fill ten bowls. “Let’s take a lot,” Una said. “More leaves, more flavor. I want the soup to shout mint in a friendly way.” Her hands were already ready to gather a whole side of the plant. Amina held the bowl and did not cheer the handful yet. Grandma Soraya stepped out with the kitchen scissors and a calm face. “The plant is cooking with us,” she said. “It is not only an ingredient. It is a neighbor that has to keep living.”

Una felt a pinch of impatience and also curiosity. “If I take a lot, will the soup be better?” she asked. Grandma Soraya knelt by the pot. “If we take every leaf, the plant cannot drink sunlight and make new ones. Bare stems get tired. A few leaves give a true mint smell. The rest stay so the plant can grow tomorrow’s soup and tomorrow’s tea.” Amina nodded. “Nature likes leftovers on the stem,” she said, proud of the sentence. Una looked at the cloud of mint again. It was not a pile of treasure to empty. It was a little creature with plans. She felt her grabby-garden feeling step back.

Grandma Soraya showed them how to choose. “Pick above a pair of leaves, so new growth has a door,” she said. “Take from different stems, not one stem bald.” Una snipped one sprig. The smell rose, cool and sweet. She almost snipped five more in a rush. She stopped and sniffed the bowl. “It is already strong,” she admitted. Amina chose a second sprig from the other side, so the plant would stay balanced, like a haircut that was fair. They laughed at the idea of the mint wearing a stylish trim. A third small sprig completed the bowl. The plant still looked like itself, only slightly tidier. Una felt relief. She had not made a bald neighbor.

They carried the bowl inside as if it held something rare, because it did. Grandma Soraya torn the leaves gently and dropped them into the soup at the end, so the heat would wake the smell without boiling it away. Una stirred once, slowly. The kitchen changed. It smelled like a garden walking through the door. Amina closed her eyes. “That is enough,” she said. “My nose is having a picnic.” Una tasted a careful sip from a spoon that had cooled. The mint was clear, not muddy. More would not have made it kinder. It might have made it bossy. She understood with her mouth, which is a good way to understand soup.

Back on the balcony they gave the plant a small drink, not a flood. Una touched a stem she had not cut and whispered, “Thank you for sharing. I left you your green.” Amina pointed to tiny new leaves at the tips, smaller than fingernails. “Those are tomorrow,” she said. Una drew them in a square of paper and taped the square inside the balcony door, low, where cooks would see it. The note said, A few leaves. Leave the growing. She liked rules that protected something alive. Her grabby-garden feeling read the note and nodded, as if it had wanted a boundary all along.

They ate the soup with bread at the table, three bowls, plenty of flavor, and the plant still working in the sun. Una told Grandma Soraya the feeling names. “I felt grabby. Then I felt careful. Now I feel proud and leafy.” Grandma Soraya clinked her bowl softly against Una’s. “Careful is a kind of plenty,” she said. Amina added, “We can pick again another day because we did not take the future.” Una liked that sentence so much she repeated it. Nature was not a cupboard that stayed full no matter what. Nature was a friend who shared when asked gently and needed time between askings.

Before the bowls were washed, Una looked out and waved at the mint, a little embarrassed and a lot sincere. The fluffy cloud was still a cloud. The soup had been better than a shout. It had been a conversation between kitchen and balcony. She rinsed her hands and smelled mint on her fingers, a souvenir that did not cost the plant its next week. Amina stood beside her at the sink, and they both breathed in at the same time, then laughed at the chorus. Una added one more line to the balcony note. The smell can be big even when the handful is small. She read it aloud so the words would stick to the minty air. Grandma Soraya said the soup still tasted like a garden that had been asked politely. Una felt the politeness in her chest, warm as the bowl had been.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the balcony door and the note about leaving the growing. Una watched the light reach toward the pot. The mint held its remaining leaves up, green and unbothered, and the kitchen kept the gentle smell of enough.
