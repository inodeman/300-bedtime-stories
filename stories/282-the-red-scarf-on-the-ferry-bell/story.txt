Zayd sat on the butter-yellow bench of the slow ferry with his grown-up beside him, close enough that their sleeves touched. The river moved like a wide green ribbon. Ahead, the bridge made a calm arch, and beyond it the plaza fountain flashed. A red scarf was supposed to flutter from the ferry's kind bell, high on its little post, so people on the bridge could see the boat coming and wait with a wave instead of stepping to the rail at the wrong moment. Zayd liked that scarf. It looked like a poppy against the pale sky. Today he frowned. "The scarf is not up there," he said.

The ferry guide, a grown-up in a sun hat, was coiling a soft rope. "It should be tied under the bell," the guide said. "That bell does not clang to startle anyone. It only gives one low note, and the scarf helps eyes from far away." Zayd stood, holding the bench, because the rule was to stand only when a grown-up said the deck was steady. The deck was steady. He looked along the post. The red scarf had slipped its knot and hung down the river side, dark at the tip where spray had kissed it. From the bridge it would look like nothing at all.

"May I help bring it up?" Zayd asked. "Yes," said his grown-up. "We will do it together, and no one leans over the water." The guide turned the ferry a little slower, which was already slow, and nodded. Zayd walked the middle of the deck, one hand on the rail, until he reached the post. The scarf's end was caught on a brass hook no higher than his shoulder. He did not yank. Wet cloth can tear if it is pulled like a stuck boot. He lifted the tip with two hands and held it out. "It is heavy with river drops," he said.

The guide brought a dry bit of twine. "We will wring the end over the bucket, then tie a bow that the wind can still open." Zayd twisted the scarf gently above the bucket. Clear drops with a blush from the dye tapped the bottom. He thought of the people on the bridge: a child with a counting stone, a neighbor with oranges, someone who might not hear one low bell if the breeze was talking. Seeing the red was a kindness. "If they cannot see us," he said, "they might think the river is empty and miss our wave."

They held the scarf up together. Zayd made the first loop, and the guide made the second, and his grown-up pressed a finger on the knot so it would not slip back down. The bow sat just beneath the bell, bright again, the wet tip already paling as the air found it. "Try the bell once, softly," said the guide. Zayd tapped the padded clapper. The sound was round and kind, not sharp. On the bridge, a small figure stopped and lifted a hand. Zayd lifted his too, the scarf fluttering beside the note like a flag that knew good manners.

The ferry slid under the arch. Shade cooled Zayd's cheeks, then sun returned, and the plaza steps came nearer. The guide said, "You noticed before I did. I was busy with the rope." Zayd felt a warm pinch of pride that did not need to be loud. "The scarf was in the wrong place, but it was not lost," he said. "It only needed to be put where eyes could find it." His grown-up squeezed his shoulder. "That is a good kind of helping. You saw a gap and mended the view."

At the landing, the butter-yellow bench emptied in an easy line. No one hurried. The red scarf stayed high, drying into its proper poppy color. Zayd looked back once from the ramp, still beside his grown-up. The bell gave its low note for the next crossing, and the scarf opened in the breeze so the whole bridge could read it. He thought of how a small knot, tied with company, could make a wide river feel polite.

He watched a paper cup of mint tea pass from the guide to a passenger who had been looking only at the floorboards. Now that passenger looked up at the scarf and smiled, as if the color had given permission to be part of the ride. Zayd liked that a scarf could do so much without speaking. It did not push. It simply showed where the slow ferry was, so nobody had to guess.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the ferry cabin, a square of gold beside the coil of rope. Zayd watched the red scarf hold its bow as they stepped onto the landing. Noticing where it had slipped, and helping to lift it without a yank, meant the next people on the bridge would see the boat coming and have time to wave. He walked toward the plaza with the kind bell still humming in his ears.
