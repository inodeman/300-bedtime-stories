Hana stood on a stool that did not wobble and looked into the wide pot. The rice pudding was pale gold, dotted with cinnamon and a thread of orange peel. Milk shone at the edges. The smell was sweet and round, like a hug that had learned manners. Grandma slid the spoon through once, slowly, and the rice whispered against the metal. Outside, the small town was gathering for the evening festival supper, but the hall bell had not given its soft tone. People were still walking with covered dishes and unlit lanterns.

"It smells finished," Hana said. Her stomach agreed with a polite growl. Grandma smiled and set the lid on so the steam would stay inside. "It smells hopeful," she said. "The grains need a quiet rest so they finish softening. If we taste now, we will meet them too soon." Hana put her hands on the warm sides of the pot through the towel. The warmth was strong but not harsh. "Can I stir?" she asked. "Once," Grandma said. "A slow circle, like a lantern walk, not like a race." Hana drew the spoon around the pot one time. The pudding folded over itself and settled.

They carried the pot in a small wagon lined with a folded blanket, because the hall was three lanes away and arms deserve help. The wagon wheel hummed on the smooth parts and ticked on the cracks. Hana walked beside it with one hand on the handle and one hand near the lid, not pressing, only guarding. A flute from a side street played a tune that seemed to match their steps. "Are we late?" Hana asked. "We are on time for resting," Grandma said. "The table can hold a pot that is still becoming." Hana liked the word becoming. It made waiting feel like a job with a name.

Inside the hall, long tables wore pale cloths. Spoons were lined up like a quiet choir. Mr. Sol stirred a pot of bean soup that was already being ladled into a few early bowls for the people who had come from the farthest road. The soup smelled earthy and kind. Hana felt the tug to lift her own lid, just for a look. She set a clean wooden spoon across the top instead, a little bridge that meant not yet. "That is a good sign," Mr. Sol said when he noticed. "A spoon on a lid is a promise." Hana nodded. "The promise is rest."

At the corner by the bakery they paused so a neighbor could light a lantern from the sheltered candle. Hana kept both hands on the wagon handle. The lid trembled a little when the wheel settled, and the cinnamon smell puffed once, then stayed inside. "Still resting," she told the pot, almost laughing at herself. Grandma heard and approved. The neighbor offered a crust of warm bread. Hana took a small piece and ate it slowly, letting the salt make the sweet smell even sweeter. "Your pudding can arrive last and still be loved by the table," the neighbor said. Hana nodded, crumbs on her lip, and did not lift the lid to prove anything.

While the pudding kept its promise, Hana helped set bowls in a gentle curve so the line would not feel like a fence. She counted them in pairs. She placed napkins beside them, each napkin a small square of blue. The hall windows showed the sky turning the color of the pudding's cinnamon. Someone tuned a thumb harp, plink by plink, never loud. Hana's ears followed the plinks and her hands followed the bowls. "You are keeping busy in a calm way," Grandma said. "Busy can be patient if it does not poke the pot." Hana grinned and did not poke.

Grandma lifted the lid at last. A softer steam rose, sweeter than before. She tasted one grain from the wooden spoon and closed her eyes. "Now," she said. "They have finished their quiet work." Hana's chest felt like a bell that did not need to ring. She ladled small bowls, not mountains, so the pot would meet many hands. The pudding slipped into each bowl with a slow shine. She told people, "It has cinnamon and orange peel, and it rested." Mr. Sol took a bowl and ate a careful bite. "The rest is in the texture," he said. "I can taste the time."

Hana served Grandma, then took the last small bowl for herself. She sat on a bench with her feet on the rung. The first spoonful was creamy, not chalky, warm enough to comfort and cool enough to hold. Orange brightened the cinnamon. She did not hurry the second bite. Around her, people talked in low voices and passed bread. The thumb harp found a simple song. Hana listened and ate as if both were the same kind of nourishment. "Was the waiting long?" she asked. Grandma shook her head. "It was the right size. You gave it a guard and a job."

They washed the pot later at the hall sink, where the water ran warm and the cinnamon scent loosened into the steam. Hana dried the lid until it squeaked. Mr. Sol stacked their clean bowls and thanked them for the curve of the serving line. "People could see where to stand," he said. "That is a kindness made of patience." Hana folded the wagon blanket and felt a few grains of rice in the hem. She shook them into the scrap bowl for the baker's hens, who would have a tiny festival of their own in the morning.

Before the second ladle, Hana walked the curve of bowls and saw that everyone who wanted pudding had a first taste. Only then did she offer a little more to Mr. Sol, who had praised the time in the texture. "A small more," she said, "so the pot still meets the late arrivals." He accepted exactly that. The thumb harp played the same four notes, patient as a clock that knew how to be kind. Hana wiped a drip from the pot's side with the towel. The drip was sweet on her thumb. She licked it and smiled, because a drip was not the same as rushing a whole bowl.

At home the kitchen was quiet in a new way, the pot upside down on the rack. Hana told Papa about the spoon bridge and the grain that had needed time. He listened while buttoning his sweater, nodding at the important parts. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and Hana watched it as if it, too, were something that became itself by resting in the evening.
