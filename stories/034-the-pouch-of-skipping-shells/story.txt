Hattie shook her pouch and listened to the music. The empty shells inside were flat, oval, and pale, the kind that could skip if you offered them to calm water with a sideways wrist. She had spent the early hour walking the high line where the tide had left treasures that were finished being homes. No creature clung inside. She had checked each one. Now the pouch was fat and satisfying against her hip. The cove beyond the sandcastles was a smooth gray-blue sheet. "Three skips at least," she told the best shell, a cream one with a faint pink rim. "Maybe five if I am polite to the angle."

Grandpa and the lighthouse keeper were already at the water's edge with their trousers rolled, comparing the friendly lighthouse reflection to the real one. They had no shells. They had hands in pockets and the look of people who would clap for someone else's throws. Hattie felt the pouch and felt the old pinch that said mine, I found them, I walked the long line. The pinch was not cruel. It was small and tight. She flipped the cream shell and imagined its skips belonging only to her. Then she imagined Grandpa's surprised laugh if a shell hopped under his hand. The second picture was wider.

"I have extras," she said, which was almost the full truth. She had favorites too. She opened the pouch and divided the shells into three piles on a flat rock. The piles were even. Then she saw that she had quietly given herself the cream one and the two next-best. Her cheeks warmed. She swapped the cream shell into Grandpa's pile and a speckled beauty into the keeper's. "Best ones travel," she said, before she could snatch them back. Grandpa blinked. "You hunted for these with real patience." "I can hunt again tomorrow," Hattie said. "The tide is a generous shop if we only take the empty ones."

They took turns so the water could settle between throws. Hattie went first to show the wrist, sideways, low, thumb and finger agreeing. Her shell skipped twice and sank with a polite plip. "The sea kept a tax," she said. The keeper's speckled shell skipped three times. He looked so startled that Hattie clapped, and the clap scared nobody, only sent a gull a little farther down the beach to continue its own hunt. Grandpa's cream shell hopped four. Four. He turned to Hattie with shining eyes. "That was your champion," he said. "It wanted your hand." Hattie shook her head. "It wanted a good story. This is a better one."

She did not feel smaller after the champion left her pile. She felt bubbly. They threw again. Hattie's second shell skipped three, as if the water had decided to be fair. She coached the keeper's elbow without touching him, only words. "Lower, like you are sharing a secret with the surface." His next try skipped four, and he whooped a whoop that was all delight and no roughness. A child-sized joy looked right on his weathered face. Grandpa tried Hattie's wrist trick and spun one straight into a single dignified plop. They laughed until they had to sit on the sandcastle-smooth sand and breathe.

Hattie kept one shell back without hiding it. "This one is for my pocket, so I remember the shape," she said. "Everything else can be for the morning." Saying it out loud kept the keeping honest. They used the rest. Some throws failed, and failure was funny, not sour. The piles disappeared. The water stitched rings into rings. Hattie noticed she was cheering more than she was counting her own score. The joy had stretched to fit three people and a gull that wandered back, looked for crumbs, found none, and went about being a gull, unbothered and unowned.

Afterward they built a tiny sandcastle with a moat and set no shells on it, because the shells had already done their work in the water. Hattie rinsed her hands. They smelled like salt and stone. "I almost kept the cream one," she told them, because sharing the truth was part of sharing the morning. Grandpa squeezed her shoulder. "Almost is where choosing lives. You chose the wide picture." The keeper found a new empty shell at the tide line, checked it carefully, and offered it to Hattie. "For tomorrow's pouch," he said. She took it as a gift, not a replacement. It was speckled like a tiny map.

They walked toward the dunes, Hattie's pouch light against her hip, the new shell clicking alone. Light was all right. Light meant the music had been played, not hoarded. She looked back at the flat water repairing itself into one sheet. Somewhere under it the cream shell rested, finished with skipping, back to being a small piece of the shore. Hattie did not need to own that ending. She had seen Grandpa's four hops. That picture would last longer than a pocketful.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the single speckled shell Hattie set on the sill. She turned it so the lantern could find its freckles. Her pouch hung on a hook, soft and nearly empty, ready for another generous tide. She could still hear the plips and the whoop and her own clap. Sharing had not erased her skill. It had given the skill more wrists to live in. Hattie washed the sand from between her fingers and left the shell in the light, a small bright proof that the best things sometimes leave your hand on purpose.
