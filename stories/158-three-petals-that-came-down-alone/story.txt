Uma reached the hill lookout with a basket that she hoped would come home full of color. The path was the familiar one, rosemary then the bent sign, and Grandma walked beside her. At the top the view was gentle: roofs, canal thread, common. Along the rail, where feet were not supposed to trample, wildflowers held themselves in little crowds. Cream, purple, and a yellow that looked like sunshine had decided to sit down. Uma's fingers wanted all of them. "There are so many," she said. "A few would not be missed. I could make a jar on the table."

Grandma knelt at the edge of the path, not in the flowers. "Let us look before we take. See the bee in the purple one? She is working. If every walker took the brightest, the bee's map would get holes." Uma saw the bee's legs dusty with gold. A second bee arrived like a tiny determined bus. "I did not think of their map," Uma said. Her want did not vanish, but it made room. "What about seeds?" Grandma tipped a dried bloom that had finished. "These heads will fall and become next year's color. If we pick every fresh face, next year the hill is only grass and a disappointed lookout."

Uma sat on her hands, which made Grandma smile. "Hands can be enthusiastic," Uma admitted. "I will give them a different job." She opened her sketchbook. "I can take the flowers with my eyes and leave them with the hill." She drew the purple crowd, then the yellow sitters, then one cream flower larger because it was nearest. The bee would not hold still, so she drew a bee-shaped blur and labeled it "busy." A butterfly, pale as paper, rested long enough for a simple outline. Uma's basket stayed empty of stems and slowly filled with looking.

A fallen petal, already on the path, trembled in a breeze. "That one has left by itself," Grandma said. "Fallen petals are fair to collect, if we do not shake the plants to force them." Uma picked up three petals, light as crumbs of color, and laid them in a tin. She did not shake anything. She even moved her shoe so she would not bend a low leaf. "Nature is not a shop," she said. "It is a neighborhood with very small neighbors." Grandma nodded. "We are visitors who know how to say excuse me."

They ate orange slices on the bench that faced the view, not the flower edge. Uma counted roofs to rest her eyes, then went back to drawing. She added the rosemary from the path, which they were allowed to sniff and which Grandma sometimes trimmed at home from their own pot, a different plant with a different job. "Our pot is for kitchen leaves," Uma said. "The hill flowers are for the hill's kitchen, and the bees are the cooks." Grandma laughed warmly. "That is a fine way to remember." A walker passed, saw the sketchbook, and whispered, "Lovely that you are drawing, not grabbing." Uma's cheeks warmed in a proud way.

Before they left, Uma stood and spoke to the flowers without feeling silly. "We are going. Thank you for the picture. Please make seeds." Grandma did not tease. Some thank-yous were part of paying attention. On the way down Uma kept the tin closed so the petals would not blow back onto the road in a wasteful way. She noticed ants crossing the path in a neat concern of their own. She stepped over the line. "Everybody has an errand," she said. The sentence made the whole hill feel companionable.

At the bottom they turned to look up. The flowers were too small to see, which Uma liked. They did not need to perform for the lane. They could just go on being a pantry and a nursery. "If I had filled the basket, the jar would last a day and the hill would have a bald patch," she said. "My drawing can last on the wall, and the purple can last for the bee." Grandma squeezed her hand. "You chose the longer kindness." Uma swung the empty-of-stems basket and felt it was full anyway.

Home smelled like the orange slices still on their fingers. Uma arranged the three fallen petals on a white plate, a tiny exhibit, and set her drawing beside it. She wrote a caption: "These live on the hill. These came down on their own." Grandma read it and pinned a copy of the caption in her recipe book, not as a recipe, as a reminder. Uma washed her hands and watched a real bee, far in the garden, visit their own lavender. "Our garden can share too," she said. "Without being stripped."

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the plate and the page. The petals looked even thinner in the light, almost ready to become dust and story. Uma did not press them to see the color stronger. She let them be nearly finished. "Loving a place can mean leaving it," she told Grandma. Grandma set the lantern steady. "Leaving it so it can keep loving you back with blooms." Outside, the hill held its flowers for the evening bees, and the glow in the glass was a small echo of all that yellow.
