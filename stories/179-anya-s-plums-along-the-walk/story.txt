Anya's basket held twelve plums, counted twice, each one dusty purple and cool from the backyard shade. The town lantern walk was starting at the corner. Papa carried their lantern, a cream shade with no picture, just a steady glow. Anya carried the fruit. The rule she had chosen was simple: offer, do not insist, and keep three for home so morning would have a share too. The lane smelled like damp walls and a little like cinnamon from someone's pocket of cookies.

"Who gets the first?" Papa asked. Anya looked ahead. Mrs. Nguyen was tying a scarf and humming nothing in particular. "A neighbor before a stranger," Anya said, "and then anyone whose hands are free." She lifted a plum. It was smooth and heavy for its size. "Mrs. Nguyen, would you like one from our tree?" Mrs. Nguyen's hum turned into a smile. "I would. The color alone is a gift." She rubbed the plum on her scarf and bit. Juice shone at the corner of her lip. "Sweet," she said, "and a little like flowers." Anya felt the basket's count become eleven, which still felt like plenty.

They walked. Lanterns bobbed at a calm height. A flute played somewhere behind, not chasing them. Anya offered a plum to the guide of the walk, who had been nodding and greeting so long that supper had missed him. He took it with a thank you that was almost a laugh of relief. "I will eat it at the square," he said, "so my greeting hands stay clean." Anya liked a plan. She did not hurry him. The next offer went to a musician sitting on a low wall with a lute at rest. "Fruit for the pause," she said. He nodded, mouth too full of a smile to need many words.

Halfway, a man waved the offer away because he had already eaten. Anya did not feel refused. She felt the choice working. "Then the next person can have a bigger chance," she said. Papa squeezed her shoulder. The plums clicked softly when the basket swung, so she held it closer to her body. One had a tiny split in the skin. She set that one aside in a napkin for herself, not because it was worse, but because it might drip on a neighbor's sleeve. Thinking ahead, she decided, was part of sharing well.

At the square, cups of mint water waited. Anya took the smallest cup and drank before she ate, so the plum would taste like itself. Her split one was juicy and bright, cooler than the evening air. She closed her eyes for the second bite. Papa ate one too, and that left the home three still tucked under a cloth. Mrs. Nguyen found them again and showed her clean pit, wrapped neatly. "I will plant nothing in a crack of the walk," she said. "Pits go in the scrap pail, or in a pot if we are feeling hopeful." Anya dropped her pit into the pail. The little clink was satisfying.

She offered two more to people who had arrived late and looked as if the walk had been their long way around. Both said yes. The basket grew light. Anya kept the cloth over the last three so they would not look like leftovers begging. "Hidden is not the same as selfish," Papa said. "You made a plan, and you are keeping it." Anya agreed. A plan was a way to be generous without emptying tomorrow. The flute arrived at the square and played a tune that sounded like walking downhill slowly. Lanterns made purple shadows inside the basket.

They turned toward home when the square began its quiet ending, people gathering cups, no one clapping hard. Anya's arm was pleasantly tired. She let Papa carry the basket for the last lane, and she carried nothing but the lantern for a few steps, the handle smooth, the light steady. Then they traded back, because the plums were her promise. At their gate the tree in the yard was a darker shape against the sky, full of leaves and empty of today's reach. "Thank you," Anya told the tree, and felt only a little shy about speaking to it.

Between offers, Anya checked the cloth so the home plums would not bruise against the basket wall. She rolled a spare napkin into a soft nest. "You are packing a future breakfast," Papa said. Anya grinned. A breeze cooled the damp place on her wrist where a plum had touched. She did not wipe it on her coat. She used her own handkerchief, stitched with a yellow dot. The dot made her think of little suns. Sharing, she thought, could be planned in a basket and still feel light in the hands.

At the square she watched the guide finally eat his plum. He closed his eyes the way people do when a flavor is kind. Juice did not run, because he had waited for a calm moment. Anya felt proud without needing to be announced. She rinsed her fingers in a cup of water meant for that, not for drinking, and dried them on the towel by the pail. The towel was rough and clean. A seed of mint stuck to her thumb, and she let it stay for its smell.

On the last lane the lantern and the basket traded places once more so neither arm kept all the work. Anya noticed she could still smell plum blossom even though the fruit was the part they had carried. Maybe the smell lived in her hands now. Papa said smells are travelers too. They passed a closed window where cups were stacked, and the stack caught their light for a heartbeat. Anya lifted the basket a little, a goodbye wave made of fruit and cloth, then brought it gently down.

Inside, she washed the juice from her fingers until they smelled like soap instead of flowers. She set three plums in a bowl in the middle of the table, where morning could find them and share them again. Papa put the lantern out with a snuffer. The house made its small night noises, a clock, a settling board. The lantern by the window glowed softly, and Anya looked at the bowl of three plums, glad the walk had carried the others to open, happy hands.
