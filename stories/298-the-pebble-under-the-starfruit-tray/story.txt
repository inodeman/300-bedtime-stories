Puck stood at the starfruit stall while the sky was still the color of a ripe apricot and the lamps were just waking. The market was early evening, busy and gentle, not a place of shadows to fear. Four cane trays, flat and woven, were stacked with yellow-green starfruit, each slice-end showing a star. The stack leaned. Puck put his hands out, not grabbing fruit, just hovering. "It is going to tired-lean," he said. "One foot is on something."

The fruit seller, a grown-up with a clean apron, looked at the front foot of the bottom tray. A gray pebble, small as a thumb nail, sat under it. "That pebble thinks it is a hill," the seller said. "If one of us yanks the tray, the stars will roll. We need a team." Puck nodded. "I can steady. I cannot lift and sweep at the same time." A lamp helper from the next stall, also a grown-up with a soft brush for dusting paper lamps, stepped over. "I can sweep, if someone makes a little space." Three people, three jobs. Puck felt the plan click like a well-set tray.

"Hands on the sides, not the fruit," said the seller. Puck placed his palms on the cane edges. The seller held the opposite corners. "We lift a finger's height, no more," the seller said. "Lamp friend, the pebble is yours." They counted, "One, two, lift." The stack rose a breath. The lamp helper swept the pebble onto a leaf, not into the walking path where a shoe could meet it later. "Clear," the helper said. "Down slowly." They lowered the tray. It sat with a small contented creak and did not rock. Puck kept his hands a moment longer, then let go. The stars stayed in their rows.

"It was only a pebble," Puck said, "but it made a whole tower unsure." The seller laughed kindly. "Towers are honest. They tell you when their feet are wrong." Puck picked up the pebble from the leaf and walked it to a little dish of found stones the market kept for mosaics, not for leaving under trays. "There," he said. "You can be art later, not a wobble now." The lamp helper brushed a curl of dust from the cane. "My brush is usually for paper ribs. It was glad to meet a pebble." Puck grinned. Different stalls, one stack. That was the town, he thought, being a team without making a speech.

They checked the other feet, all four, because one clear pebble did not prove the ground was kind everywhere. A second tiny grit sat near the back. Puck pointed. "Almost nothing, but almost nothing is how leans begin." They lifted once more, a smaller lift, and the brush did its short work. Down. Still. Puck pressed each corner with two fingers. No rock. "Level," he said. The seller set one starfruit in his hand. "A thank-you, not a payment you must take if you do not want it." Puck wanted it. He ate it in small bites. It tasted like cool sunshine.

A customer came and chose fruit without the stack shivering. Puck watched the customer's careful hands and felt proud in a shared way. The pride did not need his name on a sign. The tray was flat because three people had done one part each. If he had tried to be the lifter and the sweeper and the steadier, the stars might have traveled. Teamwork was letting the brush be the brush. He wiped his palms on a dry cloth and stacked two empty trays somewhere else, feet checked first.

The clock tower chimed its kind note over the market. Lamps glowed warmer, still early, still friendly. Puck looked along the row of stalls: persimmon lamps, fig baskets, a jar of honey. He wondered how many other small pebbles were pretending to be hills. He did not go hunting to be a hero. He only said to the seller, "If I see another lean, I will call a team before I call it bad luck." The seller said that was the right order of words.

Puck's grown-up, who had watched from the walking side, joined him. "You waited for help instead of heaving," the grown-up said. "That is strong." Puck nodded. Strong, today, was a finger's height of lift and a pebble in a mosaic dish. He looked at his starfruit core and found the little star pattern still there, a reminder that the fruit had kept its shape because the tray had kept its calm.

The seller called, "Feet still flat?" Puck checked the corners once more and gave a thumbs-up. "Flat as the plaza," he said. The lamp helper waved the soft brush like a tiny flag and went back to paper lamps. Puck felt the team loosen in a good way, job done, nobody holding the stack anymore. He dropped the starfruit core into the compost pail, not on the ground, so the next feet would be as safe as the trays.

The lantern by the window glowed softly at the fruit stall's window, lighting the level cane trays and their quiet stars. Puck set the mosaic dish where the next found pebble could rest. Teamwork had taken the wobble out of the stack, each person doing one safe part. He walked on under the apricot sky, hands open, ready to steady but not to yank.
