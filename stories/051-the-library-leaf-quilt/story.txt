Esme carried a bundle of dry, colorful leaves into the hollow tree library and set them beside the reading bench. The bench was smooth and cool, a nice seat for a short look, but a long visit made even a careful sitter wriggle. She had noticed a gentle fox shifting from paw to paw yesterday while sorting leaf-pages. "You need a softer place," Esme had told the empty bench this morning. Now she was back with her plan, a quilt of stitched leaves, big enough for one person and one fox to share without bumping elbows too much.

She laid the leaves in rows on the mossy floor where the light was good. Gold ones, russet ones, and a few still green at the edges. None were snatched from living branches. They were gifts the oaks had already dropped. Esme poked tiny holes with a blunt dry stem and sewed with long grass threads, in and out, slow enough not to tear the delicate skins. "Easy," she murmured when a leaf crackled. "You are allowed to be fragile. I will give you neighbors so you can be strong together." The sound of stitching was small and pleasant, like rain that had decided to be polite.

The library fox peered around a stack of leaf-pages, ears first, then the rest of its curious face. Esme patted the floor. "Come see. This will be for the bench, and you may test it before anyone else, since you sit here so often." The fox approached, sniffed a gold leaf, and settled nearby without stepping on the work. Esme liked that kind of company, close and careful. She told the fox about her mushroom cottage, where a thick moss mat already made the window seat kind, and how she wanted the library to feel just as welcoming. "Books of leaves deserve a reader who is not shivering," she said.

Stitch by stitch the quilt grew. Esme made a border of smaller leaves so the edges would not flake. When her grass thread ran short, the fox nudged a fresh green blade across the floor, a quiet offering. "Perfect," Esme said. "You are a finder of the right pieces." She tied a knot no bigger than a seed. Her fingers smelled like dry shade and sweet dust. Outside the hollow, the afternoon glowed on ferns, and somewhere a firefly practiced glowing and then thought better of it. Esme hummed a tune without words. Kindness, she felt, could be a song you sewed instead of sang.

She held the quilt up at last. It was uneven in a handmade way, longer on the left, brighter on the right, and entirely soft to look at. "Bench, prepare to be kinder," she said. She spread the quilt over the cool seat and smoothed every curl. The fox waited until she nodded, then stepped on, turned once, and lay down with a sigh that melted into the leaf pile. Esme laughed. "That sigh is the best review in the wood." She sat at the other end, and the quilt held them both. The coolness of the bench was still there, far underneath, but it no longer bossed the visit.

They read for a while, if looking closely counted as reading. Esme studied a leaf-page that showed the rope footbridge drawn in fine veins of ink. The fox studied a page of pressed fern shapes. Now and then Esme shifted so the fox would have the thicker part of the quilt, where she had doubled the leaves. "Your paws do more walking than mine," she said. "Take the softest corner." The fox blinked slowly, the way content animals do, and tucked its tail along the border. Esme felt a glow under her ribs. Giving the better seat did not make hers worse. It made the whole bench feel fair.

Before she left, she folded a spare corner up like a pillow and set an acorn cap on it as a marker. "If other readers come, the pillow side can be for whoever is most tired," she told the fox. She wrote nothing bossy. She trusted the library to share. On her paper map she drew a tiny quilt square inside the hollow-tree mark so she would remember to bring new leaves when the old ones grew crisp and weary. Quilts, like kindness, needed mending later, and that was not a failure. It was another visit.

The walk home passed the picnic clearing, where moss already knew how to be a blanket, and Esme waved at it as if it were a teacher. Her hands were tired and grassy and proud. She thought of the fox's sigh and smiled at the oaks. At her round door she wiped her boots so she would not track the library's dust onto her own mat. The wood felt linked, cottage to bridge to bench, by little comforts people and foxes made for each other.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across Esme's spare grass threads, coiled in a cup for the next mending. She pictured the quilt in the hollow tree, warming a bench that used to be only smooth and cool. A gentle fox was probably still reading with half-closed eyes. Esme poured berry tea and let the steam touch her cheeks. Kindness, she thought, was a leaf given a neighbor, a softer corner offered first to someone else, and a sigh that meant stay as long as you like.
