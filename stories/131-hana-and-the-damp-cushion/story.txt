The treehouse smelled like warm wood and the orange peels someone had dried on the sill. Hana climbed the ladder with a tin cup of water held carefully against her shirt. Leaves tapped the roof in a friendly way, not a storm, just the oak saying hello. Her cousin sat on a plum-colored cushion with a picture book open to a page of boats. Auntie waited on the grass below, reading her own pages in the shade. "Story time can start when you are ready," the cousin called. Hana smiled and stepped inside.

She set the cup on a low shelf beside a tin of crayons. The shelf was narrower than it looked. When she turned to sit, her elbow nudged the cup. Water leapt in a clear arc and landed on the second cushion, the pear-green one nobody was using yet. The sound was a soft splash, not a crash. Hana's face went hot. She could flip the cushion over and hope the damp side stayed a secret until everyone went home. The idea sat in her hands like a wrinkled plan. The green cloth darkened in a shape like a small lake.

The cousin looked up from the boats. "Did something spill?" The question was gentle. Hana could still say no. The lake shape would tell on her later, when someone sat down and felt the cool. She touched the wet spot. It was only water, clean and cool, but it was not where water belonged. She took a breath that tasted like orange peel. "I bumped my cup," she said. "The pear cushion got a drink it did not ask for. I am sorry. I will help dry it." Saying it made the room feel wider again.

The cousin closed the book with a finger saved in the page. "Thank you for telling. Hidden damp becomes a colder surprise." They lifted the cushion together and held it at the window where the breeze could work. Hana pressed a dry cloth from the supply basket against the lake shape, then lifted, pressed, and lifted, the way Auntie blotted spills at home. The cloth grew cool. The cushion grew lighter in its color. "It does not have to be perfect before we read," the cousin said. "It has to be honest and on the way to dry."

Hana climbed down a few rungs and called to Auntie, not a shout, just a clear voice through the leaves. "We need the sunny rail for a cushion." Auntie stood and received the pear cushion with both hands, promising a spot on the garden bench where the light was wide. "Water tells the truth," Auntie said, "and so did you." Hana climbed back up with empty hands and a steadier chest. The plum cushion was big enough for two if they sat with knees folded. They put the tin cup on the floor in a corner where elbows could not find it.

They read the boat page and the next, about a harbor that hung its lights in a row. Hana's voice joined on the short lines. When she stumbled on a word, she pointed, and the cousin sounded it out like steps on the ladder, one rung at a time. The breeze turned a paper star. Below, the pear cushion steamed faintly in the sun, a shy little cloud. Hana glanced down once and felt glad it was not hiding under anyone. The problem had been a bump and a wish to pretend. The mending had been a sentence and a cloth.

Before the chapter ended they checked the supply basket and found a second dry cloth, folded and ready for the next ordinary spill. Hana set it on top where a hand could find it. "A kindness for later," she said. The cousin nodded. "Later is lucky when someone thinks ahead." They shared the last of the water by pouring it into two tiny lid-cups, slow enough not to repeat the lake. It tasted like tin and oranges. The oak leaves kept their soft tapping, a roof that did not mind a small mistake.

When the cushion on the bench was only cool and no longer wet, Auntie passed it up in the string basket. Hana hugged it once, then set it in the sun patch inside the treehouse to finish. She told the cousin she had almost flipped it. "Almost is a true part of the story," the cousin said. "The telling is the part I will remember." Hana liked being remembered for the telling. They put the book away with the boat page easy to find tomorrow. The ladder waited, smooth and ordinary, for the climb down.

At home Hana washed her hands and described the lake shape, the blot, and the bench. Mama listened while slicing cucumbers that smelled green and fresh. "You made the treehouse kinder than you found it in that minute," Mama said. Hana set her tin cup on a tray, far from the edge, and smiled at the lesson without feeling scolded. The day still held orange peel and pages. A small problem had been met with a clear voice.

Before they climbed down, Hana and her cousin shook the crumbs from a napkin out the window for the ants that lived in the oak's shade. The crumbs drifted like tiny leaves. Hana watched one land on a fern and felt the treehouse was part of the yard, not a secret above it. She capped the tin cup and set it in the string basket so her hands would be free for the rungs. The paper star turned once more, slow as a thought. She was ready to go down with a lighter chest than the one she had carried up.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the table where the tin cup stood in a safe place. Hana thought of the pear cushion drying in a square of sun and of a book of boats waiting in the oak. She was glad she had not hidden the spill. Kindness, she decided, could be as simple as a cloth and a true sentence. The leaves in her memory tapped good night, and she let the treehouse grow quiet in her mind.
