Leila stood at the balcony door in rabbit-soft slippers and looked at the small wooden stool. A yellow moon was painted on the top step, a little crooked, because she had painted it herself on a rainy afternoon. The real moon was outside, high over the garden. Leila wanted to step up and say goodnight to it from the rail, the way Mama did. Her hands felt wiggly. The balcony was safe, with a sturdy rail and Mama right beside her, but Leila had always watched from the rug before.

"I can hold the stool," Mama said. "You can decide the rest." She did not hurry. She set a cup of hot milk on the indoor table so it would be waiting, and she folded the quilt on the chair in case the night air felt cool. Crickets sang in the mint leaves below. Paper stars hung in the window, turning slowly whenever someone walked past. Leila touched the painted moon on the stool. It felt smooth under her thumb.

"I want to try," Leila said. Her voice was small and clear. Mama smiled and placed the stool on the balcony boards, close to the rail but not too close. Leila put one slipper on the first step. The wood gave a tiny friendly creak. She put the other slipper on the second step. Now she could rest her hands lightly on the rail and see over the herb pots. The garden looked neat and silver. Nothing was hiding. The moon seemed to nod, though Leila knew it was only bright and steady.

"Goodnight, moon," she said, a little louder. "Goodnight, crickets. Goodnight, mint." Saying it from the step made her chest feel wide. Mama stood where Leila could see her sleeve. "Would you like the tube?" Mama asked. The telescope was a cardboard tube with a green paper ring. Leila had thought only tall people could use it well. She took it in both hands, balanced on the stool, and aimed it with Mama's finger pointing the way. The moon popped into the circle, bright as a lamp in a round window.

Leila laughed. "I found it." She had not needed to be taller than she was. She only needed the stool, the try, and someone close. She looked until her arms grew tired, then she handed the tube back carefully. Mama showed her three stars in a straight line. "Some people call those a belt," Mama said. "They are just stars sitting in a row, like buttons on a coat. They are very far away, and they are fine." Leila repeated, "Buttons on a coat," so the idea would stay gentle.

They climbed down together. Leila's knees felt proud rather than shaky. Inside, she wrapped the quilt around her shoulders and drank the hot milk in slow sips. The cup warmed her palms. She told Mama which part had felt big. "The first step," she said. "After that, the rail was only a rail." Mama nodded. "Courage often lives in the first small move. The rest of the path hears that you have started." Leila liked that. It did not sound like a test. It sounded like a door opening.

Before bed she stuck two glow-in-the-dark stickers on the side of the stool, one on each edge of the painted moon, so she could find the step if the lamp was dimmed. They were not there to scare the shadows away. They were there like little cheers. She also taped a new paper star to the window, a blue one, because the night felt like it had made room for her voice. The crickets kept their same sweet ticking, as if they had been cheering in their own language all along.

Leila set the cardboard tube in its basket and lined her slippers up beneath the stool. She looked once more through the glass. The moon was still there, not asking her to be bold every minute, only glad she had said goodnight. She practiced the sentence in a whisper, not because the words were hard anymore, but because she liked the shape of them. "I want to try." The words felt like slippers that finally fit.

Leila asked if she could carry a paper star out for a second goodnight. Mama nodded. On the stool again, Leila lifted the star so its yellow point lined up under the moon, a small echo of a big light. She felt the wiggle in her hands settle into steadiness. "I can hold still," she told herself, and she did, long enough to see one cricket hop from one mint leaf to another and stop to sing. She did not chase it. She only watched, glad the garden was busy in a peaceful way. When she stepped down, she put the star back on its string herself, pressing the tape smooth. The second climb was easier, and the ease felt like a friend she had earned.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the painted moon on the wooden stool. Leila's blue paper star hung among the yellow ones. Out on the balcony the mint leaves shivered in a small breeze, and the crickets went on sewing their song through the night. Leila slid under the quilt, brave in a quiet way, and the house held her try like a treasure.
