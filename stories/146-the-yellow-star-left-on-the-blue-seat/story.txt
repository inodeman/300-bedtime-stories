Idris liked the village bus because the windows were tall and the seats bounced just a little. Auntie held their passes, and Idris held the rail until they sat. They were riding two stops to the street where the library van parked on Thursdays, a trip so short they could see their lane from the first corner. The bus smelled like warm dust and apple cores someone had wrapped neatly. "Window or aisle?" Auntie asked. "Window," Idris said, and pressed his forehead near the glass, not on it, because the driver had asked for gentle heads.

At the next corner the bus sighed and opened its doors. People stepped off with baskets. When the seat in front of Idris emptied, something pale stayed behind on the cushion. It was a ticket stub, and clipped to it with a tiny wooden pin was a paper star, folded sharp and yellow. Idris's hand went toward it before his thoughts did. The star was bright. It would look fine in his window box. He pinched the pin, then stopped. The stub had a number written in pencil, and the star was not his.

"Auntie," he said, low, "someone left a star. I want it, but I think wanting is not the same as owning." Auntie looked at the stub and nodded. "That is an honest sentence. We can give it to the driver. Drivers know how to reunite small things." Idris stood carefully while the bus was still, because the rule was to walk when the wheels rested. He carried the stub flat on his palm so the star would not bend. His cheeks felt warm. Part of him still hoped the driver would say he could keep it.

The driver, Mr. Solano, had kind eyes and a timetable tucked by the mirror. "Found on the blue seat?" he asked. Idris nodded. "I did not open anything. I only picked it up so it would not be sat on." Mr. Solano turned the stub over. "I remember this star. A child named Lale folded a pocketful at the fair booth and clipped one to a stub so it would not fly off. Lale and her father are still on the bus, three seats up, looking under their bags." He smiled. "Would you like to return it yourself? I will walk with you."

They went forward together. Lale was kneeling on the seat backward, peeking at the floor, while her father checked the aisle. "It had a pin," Lale said, not crying, only busy with worry the way you worry about a favorite pencil. Idris held out his palm. "Is this yours? It was on the blue seat. I did not fold it. You did." Lale's face opened like a window. "Yes. The point is a little soft because I hugged it. Thank you for not putting it in a pocket and forgetting whose it was." She clipped it back to her own sleeve for the rest of the ride, where she could see it.

Idris sat down again with empty hands and a full feeling. "I still like the star," he told Auntie. "But I like the look on her face more. If I had kept it, the bus would have felt crooked." Auntie squeezed his shoulder. "Honesty puts a thing back in the right story." The bus rolled on. Outside, the common slid by, and a kite far off was a red comma in the sky. Idris did not wish the comma were his. He named what he really had: a seat, a window, an auntie, and a truth he had said out loud.

At the library van stop, Mr. Solano paused before opening the door. "Idris, some children would have been quiet and kept the pretty thing. You were quiet and then you were clear. Both can be good, but clear was right today." Idris shrugged, pleased and a little shy. "It was only a stub," he said. "But it was somebody's only stub." Lale waved the star once from her seat. "I will fold you one at the next fair," she called, "a new one, so it starts as yours." Idris waved back. A gift that began as a gift was different from a treasure that began as a find.

On the walk from the stop to the van, which was half a street and all in sight, Idris told Auntie the order of what he had done. "Saw it. Wanted it. Stopped. Told you. Carried it. Returned it." Auntie said, "That is a good list to remember when a shiny thing is sitting where it does not belong." They chose a picture book about boats and sat on the van's step to look at the cover before going in. The paper star was already a happy ending somewhere behind them on the green bus.

The lantern by the window glowed softly that evening while Idris drew a yellow star on a fresh scrap, not a copy he pretended was found, but a star he made himself. He wrote his name on the back. "This one can live here," he said. Auntie taped it to the sill, beside the glow and not over it. Outside, the last bus of the evening hummed along the lane and did not stop, because everyone was already where they belonged. Idris watched the light touch his pencil lines and felt the day settle into the right hands.
