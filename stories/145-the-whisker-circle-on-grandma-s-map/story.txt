Hana spread Grandma's hand-drawn map across the kitchen table and held the corners with a spoon, a cup, and two smooth stones. The paper was soft from folding. Lines showed their lane, the post office square, the canal dock, and the common. Little pictures stood in for words: a bench, a duck, a letter box, a hill with a tiny flag. Hana's finger hovered. "What does the circle with whiskers mean?" she asked. Grandma leaned in, smelling like soap and pencils. "That is the cat who sits by the bakery door, but only in the drawing. The real cat may be on an errand. Maps remember habits."

Hana's curiosity tapped like rain. "And the blue scribble?" "The canal, drawn fast because the water would not hold still," Grandma said. "And this star is not the sky. It is the lamp by the station, so a walker knows the turn at dusk." Hana sat up. "Could we check them? Not a big journey. Just the loop you drew, with you, before lunch." Grandma was already reaching for her hat. "A map likes to be tested. Bring your own pencil. If something new is true, we can add it."

They walked the first line, which was their own pavement. Hana matched the drawing to the red door, the crack shaped like a smile, and the post box. "The letter box is fatter than you drew," she said. Grandma laughed. "It has been eating envelopes. Draw it rounder on your copy." Hana had a smaller paper clipped to a board. She drew a plump box and a tiny envelope beside it. She did not cross the street until Grandma said the way was clear. The loop was close to home, and every corner had been theirs for years.

At the canal dock they stopped where the map showed the duck. A real duck slept with its beak under a wing, near a moored boat that was not going out. The water made small patient sounds against the wood. "The duck is here," Hana whispered, so she would not startle it. "But you drew three, and I see one." Grandma looked around. "The other two may be visiting reeds. A map can show what often happens, not what this minute is doing." Hana drew one duck and, in smaller lines, two faint ducks she labeled "sometimes" in letters she could read aloud. "Is a guess allowed?" she asked. "A careful guess is a question in pencil," Grandma said.

They reached the common, where the map had a diamond that meant kite. No kite was up. The grass was dotted with daisies. Hana turned the paper. "The diamond is lonely," she said. "Should I erase it?" Grandma shook her head. "Leave it. Wind is a visitor, not a resident. Add what is true today beside it." Hana drew three daisies and a bench they sat on to rest their legs. From the bench she could see the hill lookout, small and friendly, not a place they needed to climb before lunch. "The hill flag on your map is really a scarf on the rail," she said, squinting. "I think someone tied it so the path looks cheerful."

On the way back they passed the little train fence. Hana heard no engine, only a bird. She added a note: "train comes later." She liked that a map could tell time with words. Near home, the cat from the whisker circle was indeed absent. In its place a sun-warmed step held a bowl of water. Hana drew the bowl. "New symbol," she said. "A drink for anyone small who passes." Grandma clapped softly. "That is curiosity turning into care. You did not only ask what the marks meant. You asked what the street was doing today."

Back at the table they compared papers. Grandma's map was the old song. Hana's was the new verse. Neither was wrong. "If we walk again tomorrow, the duck count may change," Hana said. "Then I will date the corner." She wrote the day in neat numbers. Grandma tapped the star that meant the station lamp. "You understand a deep thing. A map is a promise to pay attention, not a cage for the world." Hana nodded, hungry now, and happy in her questions.

They ate bread and pears with the papers safely rolled, away from jam. Hana kept asking gentle things. Why was the canal line blue if the water looked brown-green? Why did the bench scribble have four legs when this bench had six? Grandma answered until the answers became a game. "Curiosity is a good walking partner," Grandma said. "It keeps you near, because there is so much to see without going far."

The lantern by the window glowed softly over Hana's new map that evening. She did not add make-believe castles. She added the water bowl, the plump letter box, and the word "sometimes" in her best small printing. "Tomorrow I might check the scarf on the hill from the lane," she said, "just with my eyes." Grandma set a stone on the paper so it would not curl. "Then you will be a mapmaker who looks twice," she said. Hana watched the glow pool on the duck she had drawn and felt the day fold itself neatly, question by question.
