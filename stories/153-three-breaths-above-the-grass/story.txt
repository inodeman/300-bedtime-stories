Pascal stood at the fair rope line with his ticket stub in a pocket and his courage folded even smaller. The tethered balloon rose and settled, rose and settled, never higher than a grown-up's reach. Children came down smiling. Ropes stayed tight. Ms. Calder's voice was calm. Still, Pascal's stomach did a small roll. "It is only a few feet," Auntie said. "I will be in the basket the whole time. We can also just watch. Watching is a complete plan." Pascal wanted the plan that included sitting down inside the wicker, but his feet felt sewn to the grass.

When their turn came, Ms. Calder opened the little gate. "Rule one, you may say not yet," she said. "Rule two, we do not climb the ropes. Rule three, the balloon lifts a little and then we come straight back. It is a hello to the air, not a voyage." Pascal liked rules that left a door open. He stepped closer. The basket smelled like dry grass and canvas. The floor was steady because four boots of weight were not yet in it, and Ms. Calder held it. "If I sit, can I hold the rail?" he asked. "Both hands, the whole time," Auntie said. "I will be a rail too, if you want my arm."

He put one knee in, then the other, and sat. The wicker creaked a friendly creak. His heart tapped. He did not call the tap fear in a big way. He called it a drum that wanted a slower song. "I am in," he announced, as if the common needed the news. Auntie sat beside him, close and ordinary. Ms. Calder checked the tether, then looked at Pascal. "When you are ready, I will let it take us up to the mark on the rope. The mark is not high. You will still see everyone's hats." Pascal looked at the mark, a red ribbon only a short climb above the stake. "Hats," he repeated. "I can do hats."

"Count with me," Auntie said. "One." Pascal felt the rail grain under his fingers. "Two." He set his shoes flat. "Three." Ms. Calder eased the rope. The basket lightened. Grass dropped away by the height of a big step, then another. A breeze cooled Pascal's eyebrows. He saw the blanket tops, two paper flags still tied on the basket, a circle and a leaf, and the path back to the lane. Nothing spun. The common was the same common, only a little more map-like. "I am up," he said, and his voice came out surprised and steady. "You are," Auntie said. "And I am right here."

They stayed long enough to name three things. Pascal named the post box, the white tent, and Auntie's green shoe. Naming kept his eyes busy in a good way. A small child on the ground waved, and Pascal waved with one hand while the other stayed on the rail, which felt like a clever compromise. "Courage can wave," he said. Auntie laughed softly so the basket would not feel a bounce. "Courage can also sit very still and look." Ms. Calder asked, "Ready to come down, or shall we rest here for three more breaths?" Pascal chose the breaths. He wanted to be the one who decided. The breaths were easy. The view stayed kind.

The descent was a slow sit back onto the grass. The basket settled on the grass. Pascal's knees felt wobbly and proud. He climbed out when Ms. Calder said the step was ready. On the grass he turned and looked up at the balloon's round cheek. "You were not a mountain," he told it. "You were a step." Ms. Calder stamped his stub with a tiny pear-shaped mark. "For trying a safe new thing," she said. "Stamps are not prizes for being tall. They are notes that you showed up."

Pascal walked the fair path with a lighter shirt, as if the breeze had stayed in the cloth. He bought nothing. He did tell a waiting boy, "The rail is there the whole time, and your grown-up can be a second rail." The boy nodded, storing the sentence. Auntie squeezed Pascal's shoulder. "You did not have to become a different person. You became Pascal at the height of a hat." Pascal liked that. He did not need to be famous in the sky. He needed to know he could count to three and mean it.

They took the lane home past the hedge that smelled like dust and leaves. Pascal touched the stub in his pocket every few steps, not because he might forget the ride, but because the pear mark was a small hard proof. "Next time I might count faster," he said. Auntie shook her head kindly. "Or the same speed. The speed that fits your drum." Pascal thought his drum had learned a new rhythm, one with space in it.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the stub he set beneath the glass. The pear mark looked a little like a tiny balloon. Pascal did not need to ride again tonight. He only needed to remember the three breaths above the grass. "I was brave in a basket," he told Auntie. Auntie smiled. "You were brave, and you were safe, and those two things can hold hands." The glow stayed with them until the fair lights, far and small, went out one by one.
