The post office smelled like paper, pencil shavings, and the lemon oil someone used on the wooden counter. Theo liked the wall of cubbyholes, each one a little mouth waiting for a letter. A fan turned slowly and lifted the corner of a poster about stamps with birds on them. Auntie held a parcel tied with green string. "We will mail this to your cousin and then choose one stamp for our letter," she said. Theo nodded. His pocket already imagined a stamp of his own, bright as a window.

They waited behind a neighbor whose letter wore three flower stamps in a row. When she lifted her bag, one stamp slipped from the edge of the envelope and drifted down like a tiny kite. It landed near Theo's shoe, picture side up: a blue heron standing in a silver pond. Theo's hand moved before his thoughts did. The stamp was not sticky anymore. It was just a beautiful square. He could slide it into his notebook and draw the heron later. Nobody seemed to have seen it fall. The idea tugged.

The neighbor stepped to the counter and began to talk about the weather in the town where her letter was going. The clerk smiled and weighed the parcel. Theo looked at the heron, then at the envelope, which now had a pale empty corner. A letter with a missing stamp might have to wait. His notebook would be happy, but the heron had a job. Auntie glanced down and saw the square on his palm. She did not take it. "That is a choice," she said softly. Theo felt the paper's thin warmth.

He stepped sideways so he would not bump the scale. "Excuse me," he said to the neighbor. His voice was clear in the paper-smelling room. "Your heron slipped off. It is not sticky now, but it is yours." The neighbor turned, and her eyes went bright with relief. "I pressed that one on too gently," she said. "Thank you for watching the floor. I would have hunted the whole walk home." She took the stamp as if it were a small bird that needed a careful perch.

The clerk offered a fresh bit of moisture from a sponge in a dish. "One touch, then we press," she said. Theo watched the heron settle back onto the corner, wings flat, pond shining. The neighbor smoothed it with the side of her thumb. "Would you like to press the last corner?" she asked. Theo pressed, gentle and even, the way he had learned to close a book. The stamp stayed. A tiny problem that could have ridden home in a pocket was mended on the counter instead.

While Auntie's parcel was stamped, Theo studied the bird poster without taking any. He found a wren, a goose, and a heron like the one he had returned. "I can draw mine from memory," he told Auntie. "The real one needed to travel." Auntie bought their letter a stamp with a pear on it, round and yellow. Theo licked nothing; the clerk dabbed the sponge and let him press. The pear sat proudly. Their letter slid into a slot with a satisfying whisper.

The neighbor waited by the door to say one more thank you. She told Theo the letter was for her brother, who liked birds and would look for the heron first. "Then it is going to the right eyes," Theo said. Outside, the street was warm and the post office fan clicked a last slow turn behind them. Theo's hands were empty and his chest was not. He swung Auntie's hand and practiced the heron's shape in the air with one finger.

At the corner they posted nothing else, but Theo noticed a lost rubber band on the step and hooked it on a hook where someone might reuse it. "You are in a returning mood," Auntie said. Theo grinned. "The heron started it." They walked past a bakery that smelled like sugar crust. He did not feel poorer for giving the stamp back. He felt as if he had sent a piece of the day in the right direction. At home he drew the heron on a clean page, blue and silver, and he wrote the word travel underneath in careful letters. He told the story of the slip, the sponge, and the press. Uncle listened and tapped the page. "A kind choice can be as thin as paper and still carry weight," he said. Theo looked at his drawing and liked it better because the real stamp was already on its way.

Theo helped Auntie tear a strip of brown paper to cushion their parcel where the string crossed. The paper rasped softly and smelled like clean boxes. He pressed the edges flat so nothing would rub the pear stamp on the letter beside it. The clerk showed him a rubber date circle, rolling it on a pad the color of plums, then decided their letter needed only the stamp. Theo liked seeing a tool wait its turn. Not every mark had to land. Some helpful things were the ones you chose not to use.

They sat for a minute on the post office bench while Auntie retied her scarf. Theo swung his feet and listened to the slot whisper each time a letter went in. He imagined roads, boats, and a hand at the other end. The heron stamp was already out of sight and still easy to picture. A boy with a box of chalk nodded at him, and Theo nodded back. The room's lemon oil, the fan's slow turn, and the cubbyholes all felt like parts of a gentle machine that liked careful people.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the heron sketch. Theo watched the light make a small pond on the paper. Somewhere a letter was riding in a bin, flower stamps and one blue bird holding on. He hoped the brother would smile at the corner first. His eyes grew heavy in a good way. Kindness, he thought, was a stamp that stayed where it belonged.
