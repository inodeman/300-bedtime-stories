Inez was indoors when a faraway rumble rolled over the hills. The street outside was dry, and the sky above the town stayed pale and quiet. The sound was thunder, but it belonged to a distant place, not to their roof. Jovan sat on the rug with a notebook. A grown-up, Inez's dad, closed the window gently and checked the latch. "We are staying in," he said in an easy voice. "No one needs to be outside while thunder is speaking somewhere else. In here, we are cozy and safe."

Inez held her cup of warm milk with both hands. Her shoulders had given a little jump at the rumble. She almost said she was fine and did not mean it. Honesty tugged her sleeve. "Dad," she said, "that sound makes me jumpy, even though I know we are inside." Her dad nodded as if the sentence were welcome. "Thank you for telling the truth about your shoulders. A jumpy feeling is allowed. We can look at it together." Jovan slid the notebook closer, ready to help.

Dad sat on the low chair. "Thunder is the voice that follows lightning," he said. "Lightning is a bright spark in a far cloud. When the spark is far away, the voice takes time to arrive, like a friend walking a long path." Another soft rumble came. It was low and round, not sharp. Inez listened from the rug. "It sounds like a big drawer closing in another house," she said. Dad smiled. "That is a good picture. And that house is not our house today."

They made a simple counting game to see the distance. If a faint flicker showed far across the hills, they would count slowly until the rumble. Dad said a bigger count meant a farther spark. They waited. A tiny flicker touched the horizon, gentle as a blink. Inez counted with Jovan, "One, two, three, four, five, six." Then the rumble arrived, already fading. "Six is far," Dad said. "The weather is busy over there. Here, our room is steady." Inez felt her jump grow smaller because the number gave it a place to sit.

Inez wanted to be extra honest. "Part of me still wants to peek outside," she said. Dad shook his head kindly. "Curiosity can wait at the window, indoors. We do not open the door for a closer look. The safe view is this one." They looked through the glass. The lane was empty and calm. A neighbor's curtain glowed. No rain beat their street. The distant voice rolled again, softer. Jovan wrote, We stayed in. The thunder stayed far. Inez liked seeing the true story on paper. Honesty had kept her from pretending, and it had kept them careful.

They drew the day in the notebook. Jovan drew hills and a small spark far away. Inez drew their room, the rug, the cup, and the closed latch. She added her own face with round eyes, then changed the mouth into a small brave smile after she asked if that was fair. "It is fair if it is true," she said. "I am jumpy and I am learning." Dad tapped the page. "Both can be true. That is a whole kind of honesty." The lantern was not yet lit, but the room already felt warm.

Between rumbles they played a quiet clapping pattern, soft as rain that was not here. Inez noticed that waiting inside was not a punishment. It was a comfort with a reason. She told Dad the new feeling too. "I am less jumpy when I can count and draw." He squeezed her shoulder. "That is useful news. Next time, we will remember your tools." Jovan added a list. Count. Draw. Stay in. Tell the truth. The list looked friendly. A far rumble answered, and this time Inez only blinked, then checked Jovan's smile and found one of her own.

When the distant thunder faded for a long while, Dad opened the window a crack. The air that came in was mild and dry. The hills looked peaceful under a clearing sky. "The far weather is moving on," he said. "We can go out later, when the sky is quiet and the grown-ups say the time is right." Inez did not rush the door. She liked ending the indoor time with the same honesty she had started. "I feel better," she said. "And I still liked having you both here." That sentence felt like a good closing of its own.

Inez and Jovan compared their drawings and found the same safe details in both. A latched window. A cup. Hills pushed far to the edge of the page. Dad pointed out that their pictures agreed with the counting, and agreement made facts feel sturdy. Inez added a small sentence under her drawing. I told the truth and stayed in. She read it once, quietly, and her shoulders stayed loose. Outside, the pale sky did not argue. The distant weather had never asked them to come closer. Their honesty and their indoor plan had been the whole adventure, and it was enough.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as evening settled and the hills stayed calm. Inez set the notebook beside the light so the drawing of their safe room could be seen. She listened once more and heard only the house, the cup on the table, and Jovan turning a page. The faraway rumble had told the truth about distance, and Inez had told the truth about her feelings. Both truths had made the room a kinder place to be.
