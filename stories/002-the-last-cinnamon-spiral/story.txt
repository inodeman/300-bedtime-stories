The bakery woke up smelling like butter, sugar, and orange peel. Theo stood on a wooden step so he could see over the glass case. Rows of buns shone under the warm lamps. Miss Bramble slid a tray of cinnamon spirals onto the middle shelf, each one coiled like a golden shell. "Those are for the morning rush," she said. "Hands washed, smile ready." Theo washed at the little sink. The soap smelled like lemon. He dried his fingers on a striped towel and tied his blue apron with a careful bow. Outside, the street was still gray-blue, and a cart wheel clicked over a stone. Inside, the oven ticked as it cooled, a friendly sound, like a clock that baked.

People came in with cold noses and kind hurry. Theo passed paper bags. He counted coins into the wooden tray. He said, "Thank you," and meant it. A woman with a red scarf bought three rolls for her crew on the ferry. A grandfather chose a plain loaf because, he said, the crust sang when you pressed it. Theo liked that. He pressed nothing, but he listened, and the crust did sound crisp. By the time the school bell was still a while away, the case had many empty circles where buns had been. One cinnamon spiral sat alone on its paper doily, glossy with sugar, smelling like a warm blanket.

The door opened again, and a boy about Theo's size stepped in. His hair was damp from the sea air. He held a new pencil case against his coat and looked at the shelves as if they were a map he had not learned yet. "I am Jonah," he said. "We moved above the chandlery. My father said a bakery could sell me breakfast, but I think I am late." His stomach gave a small, honest growl. Jonah's ears turned pink. Theo's did too, from sympathy. Miss Bramble was in the back, starting the midday dough, humming. The case held the one spiral and a few seeded rolls that were not sweet.

Theo looked at the spiral. He had planned to eat it on the way to school, the first treat he had saved for himself all week. The sugar crystals caught the light. His mouth already knew the cinnamon. Then he looked at Jonah's hands, empty except for the pencil case, and at the way Jonah stood a little apart from the counter, not wanting to ask too much. Theo took a breath that tasted like yeast and warmth. "We can share it," he said. "There is one, and there are two of us. Half is still a real breakfast." Jonah blinked. "Are you sure?" "I am sure," Theo said, and he was.

He asked Miss Bramble first, because the spiral was shop food. She peered through the doorway, flour on her cheek, and nodded. "Kind arithmetic," she said. "Cut it fair, and wrap both halves so the sugar stays put." Theo found the soft knife and the board. He set the spiral down gently, as if it could feel the morning. The cut went through the cinnamon layers, slow and straight. Steam, just a little, rose from the middle. He slid each half onto wax paper. The smell grew stronger, sweet and spicy, and Jonah laughed once, a surprised happy sound. "It looks like a sunrise someone folded," Jonah said. Theo grinned. "Then we each get a piece of sunrise."

They ate on the step outside, not in a rush. The stone was cool under them. Gulls talked far off near the masts, but none came close, because Theo kept the paper neat and the crumbs in the wrapper. The spiral was soft at the center and a little crisp at the edge. Sugar sparkled on Theo's thumb. He licked it only after he had offered Jonah the extra corner, the one that had broken clean. "You take the corner," Theo said. "You had the longer walk." Jonah shook his head and broke the corner again. "Now it is even." They chewed. The cinnamon felt warm all the way down. A baker's fan in the window turned slowly, sending out more of that orange-butter smell.

Jonah told Theo about the new stair that creaked on the third step, and about a window that looked at brick except when you leaned and saw a slice of water. Theo told Jonah which street made the fastest walk to the little school, and which puddle was only deep at the curb. They were not old friends yet. They were new, and the spiral gave them something to hold while the newness settled. Miss Bramble brought out two small apples, shiny and cold from the pantry. "Sharing bread makes room for fruit," she said. Theo passed one apple over without thinking twice. The bite was crisp and loud. Juice touched his lip. He wiped it with the wax paper and folded the paper small.

When it was time to go, Theo hung his apron on the peg. His stomach felt comfortable, not stuffed, not empty. Jonah held his pencil case in one hand and the apple core, wrapped, in the other, ready for the bakery compost pail. "Tomorrow I can bring fig biscuits from home," Jonah said. "My aunt packed a tin. We could split those too, if you want." Theo nodded. "I want. Bring them to the step." They walked as far as the corner together. The harbor wind smelled like rope and salt, and the bakery smell stayed in Theo's sleeves. He waved when Jonah turned toward the chandlery stairs. The wave felt easy, like the second half of a good idea.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when Theo passed the bakery again after school, though the sun was still up and the glow was only a warm practice for evening. He saw the empty doily in the case and did not feel sorry. Miss Bramble raised her floury hand. Theo touched his pocket, where a single sugar crystal had hidden, and he smiled at the street. Half a spiral had been enough. It had tasted like cinnamon and like making room, and the room had filled itself with a new friend, an apple, and a plan for tomorrow's tin.
