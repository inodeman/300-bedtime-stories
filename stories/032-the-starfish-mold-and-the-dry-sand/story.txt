Nico loved the starfish mold more than any tower bucket. It was a tin shape with five rounded arms, salt-scratched and trustworthy, and when it worked it left a plump star on the beach as if the shore had signed its name. He set it down high on the dry sand because that spot had the best view of the friendly lighthouse. The sand there was warm as bread. He packed it with his fist, lifted the mold, and watched the star sigh into a crumbly pancake. A gull nearby took two polite steps back from the little landslide. "Not your fault," Nico told the mold. "The sand is thirsty."

He tried again, faster, as if speed could scare the crumbs into behaving. The second star lost an arm. The third was only a suggestion. Nico's ears warmed. Grandpa sat on the picnic blanket with a tide chart folded on his knee, not interfering. "The top of the beach is good for sitting," Grandpa said gently. "Building likes the line where the sea was a little while ago." Nico looked downslope. The damp band shone, darker, smoother, smelling richly of salt. It was farther from the lighthouse view. Patience would mean moving his plan. He was not sure he wanted to. He wanted the star here, now, tall as a cake.

He pressed one more handful because stubbornness is a loud guest. The mold made a sound like a quiet crunch and released another tired shape. Nico sat back on his heels. The gull picked up a loose feather, dropped it, and seemed content to wait for nothing in particular. "You are better at this than I am," Nico said. He thought of the tide chart's curves, how they did not rush the water. The sea was not late. It was on its own kind clock. Maybe sand was too. He stood, shook crumbs from his knees, and carried the mold down to the dark band without announcing that he had changed his mind. Grandpa saw, and smiled into the chart.

The damp sand packed differently. It held the shape of his fingers. Nico still wanted to slam it in and yank the mold off like a magic trick. He made himself stop. He filled one arm of the star at a time. He tapped the tin, waited a breath, and tapped again. A tiny crab burrow, far from his spot, put up a bubble and went on being a burrow. Nico kept his work away from it. "Slow," he coached himself. "Slow is not stuck. Slow is how arms stay on." He lifted the mold straight up, no wiggle. A starfish of sand stood complete, five arms, a little glistening, wonderful.

He did not shout, in case shouting could shake it. He grinned so hard his cheeks hurt. "Grandpa," he called softly. "The clock worked." Grandpa came to the edge of the blanket's view and nodded the serious nod of a fellow builder. "Look at that patient person," he said, meaning the star and Nico both. They made a second star beside the first, leaving a palm's width so the two would not lean into each other. Nico's hurry tried to return for the second one. He noticed it, set the mold down, shook his hands, and began again at the first arm. The second star came out even cleaner. Patience was a muscle. It liked practice.

A breeze powdered sugar-sand across the tops. Nico almost covered the stars with his body like a shield. Grandpa handed him a flat shell. "A little flag of shade," he said. Nico set the shell nearby, not on the star, just breaking the breeze. It was not a perfect fix. It was a calm one. They ate orange slices while the stars dried from shiny to matte. Nico wanted to pick one up. He did not. Sand stars were for visiting, not for pockets. He walked around them instead, admiring each arm. The lighthouse looked just fine from this lower place. The view had not been the important thing. The holding-together had.

When a small wash of water, thin as spilled milk, slid up near their feet, Nico felt the old rush. "Should we move them higher?" he asked. Grandpa checked the tide line and the chart. "This is their size of wave. It will stop a step away if we wait and see." They waited. The water touched the outer shine of the sand and stopped, polite as a library voice. Nico let out a breath he had been renting. Waiting to see had saved him from grabbing a fragile thing and breaking it in a rescue it did not need. He laughed. "I almost helped too much."

They built one last star, a small one, for the gull's amusement, though the gull only blinked and stayed wild. Nico rinsed the mold in the shallows and tapped it empty. His hands smelled like metal and sea. On the walk toward the dunes he looked back twice. The three stars were still themselves, a little family of patience on the dark sand. Tomorrow the tide might smooth them, and that was the beach's way. Today they had stood because he had moved, slowed, and trusted the damp line more than his rush.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Nico set the starfish mold on the step to finish drying. A few grains glittered on the tin in that warm circle of light. He could still feel the moment the first true star had held, the surprise of it, the quiet. Grandpa poured water. Nico drank and wiped a mustache of orange from his lip. Patience had not been a scolding. It had been a move down the beach and a slower hand, and the shore had answered with something that could stand on its own.
