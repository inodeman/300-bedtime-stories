Jasper liked being helpful early, when the yard still smelled like cool stone. His job was to carry the oat scoop from the tin to the porch table, where Hawa would fill the chicken bowls. The scoop was full and golden. Jasper turned to shut the tin with his elbow. His toe found the uneven edge of the step. The scoop tipped. Oats poured over the stone in a soft rush and slid into the dusty cracks.

For a moment the yard was only the sound of falling breakfast. Jasper's face went hot. He looked at the empty scoop, then at the lane, then at the door. A story popped into his mouth all by itself. The wind did it. A rabbit bumped me. The scoop was too heavy and decided to jump. None of those stories were true. The wind was barely moving. No rabbit was near. The scoop had fallen because his foot had missed.

He could hear Hawa humming inside. He could hear the grown-up setting cups. Jasper stood with the truth like a small stone in his palm. It was not a cruel stone. It was just heavy until it was spoken. He opened the door. "I spilled the oats," he said. "My toe missed the step. I did not mean to, and I did not want to say the wind did it." His voice shook once and then steadied, because the sentence was finished and the invented one was not.

The grown-up came out and looked at the step without a frown that would make the oats feel like a crime. "Thank you for the true start," the grown-up said. "Now we can do the right next thing." Hawa appeared with the bowls and stopped her song. "Are the chickens missing breakfast?" she asked. "Not for long," Jasper said. "These oats touched the dusty stone, so they should not go in the bowls. They can go to the compost. I can measure a new scoop."

They worked in a line that felt fair. Jasper swept the oats into a dustpan. Hawa held the compost crock open. The grown-up made sure no one slipped on a leftover grain. The dry bread bits from Bastian's lesson were already in the crock, doing their garden job, and the oats joined them. Nothing was kicked into the grass to be wasted or to confuse an animal later. Jasper washed the scoop, dried it, and filled it again, slower at the step, eyes on his feet.

The chickens received their breakfast in Hawa's dotted bowls and ate as if the morning had always been smooth. Jasper watched them and felt the hot feeling leave his ears. Honesty had not made the spill smaller. It had made the repair faster. If he had sent everyone looking for a rabbit that was never there, the chickens would have waited and the truth would have waited too, growing sour like oats left in rain.

He told Flora later, because she was writing I do not know yet on a paper and he wanted to add a matching sentence. "I do know, and I said it," he told her. Flora nodded as if that belonged in the same family as a careful question. Gideon, passing with Button at a quiet heel, said, "A true hello and a true oops are both good manners." Jasper laughed, and the laugh was clean.

Before lunch he looked at the step and memorized the uneven edge. He would carry the scoop with two hands next time. Mistakes could teach without needing a costume.

Jasper helped Hawa wash the dotted bowls and found that honest work has a rhythm. Scoop, carry, watch the step, tell the truth if the step wins. He said the rhythm out loud until Hawa could hum it without laughing too hard to hold a bowl. The chickens had already finished and were dusting as if oats had never known a tumble. Jasper did not ask them to thank him. The repair was its own thanks. When a single grain still shone in a crack, he coaxed it into the dustpan and sent it to the compost with the rest, because even a tiny leftover deserved the true ending and not a story about the wind.

Before bed Jasper looked at the oat tin and left the lid straight, not crooked like a secret. He told the grown-up he might still feel hot in the face if he spilled again, and that he would still use the true sentence first. The grown-up said a warm face and an honest mouth can belong to the same good child. Jasper smiled at the step on his way past, as if thanking it for the lesson, and went in with empty, clean hands. He set the dustpan where the next helper could see it, clean and ready, because a truthful morning should leave the tools as honest as the words.

The lantern by the window glowed softly while Jasper set the scoop back in the tin for tomorrow. He did not hide it. He did not cover the step with a story. The yard looked ordinary again, which is what honesty often returns to you: an ordinary day, mended, and friends who can trust your words the next time your hands are full.
