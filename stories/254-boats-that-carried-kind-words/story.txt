Hester wrote the word hello in tiny letters on a slip of paper no bigger than a thumb. The pencil whispered. Imani folded a white sheet into a boat at the other side of the table, creases crisp, corners aligned with the care of someone making a gift. Between them sat a wide basin of still water, filled only halfway, a small indoor pond with no current and no storm. "Message boats," Hester said. "We send kind words across to each other. That feels like friendship you can watch." Imani nodded. "And if a boat gets soggy, we fold another. Friends do not quit at the first wet edge."

They made two boats, then a third as a spare. The paper smelled dry and a little like the pencil shavings curled nearby. Hester tucked her hello into the first boat's fold. Imani tucked a slip that said thank you, because Hester had shared her best sheet of thick paper. They set the boats at opposite rims and gave them gentle pushes. Ripples walked toward the middle and met. The boats turned, slow as sleepy ducks, and drifted past each other. "We missed," Hester laughed. "The pond is being playful." Imani blew a careful breath. Her thank-you boat spun and bumped Hester's side with a soft tap. "Delivery," Imani said.

Hester opened the fold and read thank you aloud, even though she had watched Imani write it. Hearing it made the word warmer. She sent hello back in a new push, and this time it arrived. Imani read it and pressed the slip flat on the table like a tiny letter worth keeping. "Your hello has a smudge that looks like a heart by accident," she said. Hester peered. "It was my thumb. Now it can be a heart on purpose, if you like it." "I like it," Imani said. Friendship, they were learning, could be a thumb smudge that you told the truth about and then enjoyed.

They wrote more slips. One said I saved you a peach. One said your laugh is a bell. One said I will help fold if you get tired. None of the notes were showy. They were the size of real friendship, which often fits on a small paper. The boats carried them. One boat drank too much water and sagged beside the basin wall. Imani lifted it out and patted it with a towel. "This one can retire as a note holder," she said. They set it on the sill and tucked the peach note inside so it would not be lost. Then they folded a fresh boat from the spare sheet, sharing the folding, one crease each, so the new boat belonged to both of them from the first line.

The water cooled their fingertips. Light from the window lay on the surface in a shaky square. When they leaned over, their faces appeared and broke apart whenever a boat passed, which made them snort with laughter. "We look like paintings that will not sit still," Hester said. Imani sent a boat with the note sit still, paintings, and then had to explain the joke, and the explaining made it funnier. A drop splashed onto Hester's sleeve, a small dark moon. She did not mind. The basin stayed a basin. The floor stayed dry because they were careful with their elbows.

They held a last launch together, both hands on the shared boat, and put two notes inside, one from each. Hester's said stay my friend on rainy days. Imani's said rainy days are good for boats in basins. They let the boat float without pushing, just sitting in the middle, a little white bird at rest. "We do not have to race it," Imani said. "Being together in the same water is the message." Hester felt something settle happily in her chest. She did not need a louder proof. The notes, the spare boat, the shared creases, and the peach waiting in the kitchen were enough.

When they poured the water onto the garden herbs outside the door, the basil leaves shivered and shone. They brought the dry notes inside and clipped them to a string above the table, a short line of kind words. The retired soggy boat, now dry and wavy, held a pencil. The good boats sat beside it, ready for another indoor sail another day. Neither girl said she was the best boat maker. Hester said, "I liked your bell sentence." Imani said, "I liked your thumb heart." They ate the peach, juicy and bright, and saved the pit in a cup to plant someday, a maybe-tree, a patient kind of hope.

Juice sticky on their fingers, they washed up and left the notes hanging. Friendship had crossed a basin in paper hulls and come back as words they could read again. The string turned slightly whenever someone walked by, and the slips tapped each other, soft paper applause.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beneath the string of notes, lighting hello and thank you and rainy days until the letters looked golden. Hester and Imani stood under their own kind words. The boats were made, the messages delivered, and their friendship felt as sturdy as a fresh fold and as gentle as a push that did not spill.
