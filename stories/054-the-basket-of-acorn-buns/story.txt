Callum's basket of warm acorn buns bumped softly against his hip as he followed the moss path into the picnic clearing. He had baked a tall pile in his mushroom cottage, more than one person could finish, because the dough had doubled while he was humming and he had not wanted to waste a single puff of it. The buns were brown as oak bark and shiny where he had brushed them with berry syrup. "I will eat two and save the rest for tomorrow and the next day," he decided. A paper map rustled in the basket's side pocket, and the smell of baking walked with him like a friendly cloud.

The clearing was already occupied in the nicest way. A gentle fox sat by the stump table. A smaller fox peeked from the ferns. A third, with a silvered muzzle, lay in a patch of shade as if the day were a blanket. Their noses lifted together. Callum felt the basket grow heavier, not in his arm, but in his thinking. Two buns for him would leave a mountain. Tomorrow's buns would be less warm. He set the basket down and lifted the cloth. "These turned out bigger than my plans," he said. "Would you like to help them be the right size?"

The foxes did not rush. That good manners gave Callum time to mean his offer. He broke buns into pieces first, so sharing would be easy, then saw the silver-muzzled fox eyeing a whole round one with a hopeful, patient face. Callum placed an entire bun on a broad leaf and slid it over. "The smallest pieces are not the only fair ones," he said. "You get a whole bun too." He gave the peeking fox a whole bun as well, and the stump fox too, and kept a whole one for himself. The pile lowered. His tomorrow grew smaller. His today grew brighter. He sat down and took a bite. The crumb was nutty and sweet, and it tasted larger because of the company.

They ate while oak light danced on the moss. Callum poured water into acorn caps and passed them around. The silver-muzzled fox ate slowly, savoring, and Callum copied that pace instead of gobbling. "I was going to hide these under a cloth for me alone," he admitted. "Then you all looked so ready for a picnic that hiding felt like a lonely recipe." The small fox finished and carefully pushed a fallen crumb back toward Callum, a comic kind of sharing that made him laugh until he hiccupped. He patted his chest. "Keep it. Crumbs can be gifts when they are offered on purpose."

There were still four buns under the cloth. Callum thought of the hollow tree library and the fox who sorted leaves through the long afternoon. He thought of the rope footbridge, where a walker might like a bite halfway home. "A basket can have more than one destination," he said. He wrapped one bun for the library root and one for the bridge post, leaves tucked tight so the syrup would not paint the wood. The last two he left in the basket for anyone who arrived late to the clearing. He set the basket in the shade and put a pebble on the cloth as a sign that the food was meant to be found, not forgotten.

The silver-muzzled fox walked with him to the library, slow and content. Callum placed the wrapped bun where it would be seen and not stepped on. "For the reader," he called into the leafy hush. A tail-tip waved from behind a stack, which was thanks enough. At the bridge he tied the second packet to the post with a loose grass loop and a tiny paper scrap that said please enjoy, lettered in berry ink. He did not wait around to watch a stranger's face. Sharing did not need a parade. It needed a bun in a place where a need might pass by.

Back in the clearing the basket was lighter by one more bun, which meant someone had come and trusted the pebble sign. Callum felt a skip in his chest. "The wood knows what to do with an open basket," he told the remaining foxes. They rested together, full and a little drowsy. He unfolded his paper map and drew tiny bun circles at the clearing, the library, and the bridge, a silly map of kindness that still told the truth. The small fox put its chin on the map's edge and sighed. Callum did not move the chin. Maps could be pillows for a minute.

When he finally headed to his mushroom cottage, the basket swung easily. One bun remained for his evening tea, and that felt like plenty. He had thought plenty meant a tall secret pile. Now plenty meant a path with snacks at the right places and friends with syrup on their whiskers. Ferns brushed his free hand. Fireflies began to test their lamps in the shadows. Callum hummed the same tune he had hummed while the dough grew, only warmer now, with more voices in his memory.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the last acorn bun, waiting on an oak-leaf plate. Callum washed berry syrup from his fingers and watched the light make the bun look like a small sun. Somewhere a late walker might be untying a grass loop and smiling at a bridge post. He poured tea and let the steam join the lantern glow. Sharing, he thought, was a whole bun for the silver muzzle, an open basket in the shade, and the sweet discovery that tomorrow did not need everything today could give.
