Lars spread paper squares on a blanket at the edge of the fair, where the tethered balloon rested like a big friendly pear above a basket. Ropes held it to stout stakes. It lifted only a few feet when the keeper allowed, never higher than a grown-up could reach, and a grown-up always rode along. Today it was between rides, bobbing no taller than Lars's hop. Ms. Calder, the keeper, checked a rope and waved. "You may look. You may not untie anything. The balloon's job is to be safe and surprising in a small way."

Lars was not trying to ride yet. He was trying to make a hello the whole common could read. He had invented flag marks on the walk from home, which was only past the post box. A circle meant hello. A wavy line meant wind. A leaf meant please and thank you, because both were growing things. A little bench shape meant sit and stay cozy. "Words are good," he told Papa, "but flags can talk to people who are far across the grass, even if they are not far from home." Papa held the tape. "Then let us make them sturdy, and let us ask before they touch the basket."

Ms. Calder came over when Lars lifted a finished flag, a yellow square with a clear black circle. "What does it say?" she asked. "Hello," Lars said. "If you say yes, I would tie flags to the basket rail with soft ribbons. Not to the ropes. Not to the balloon skin. Only the wooden rail, and only while you watch." Ms. Calder studied the ribbon, which was cotton and short. "Yes to the rail. No to anywhere else. And the knots must be bows, so I can undo them with one pull." Lars beamed. "Bows are my best knot. Loops, not locks."

He tied four flags where riders could see them and people on the grass could too. Circle, wave, leaf, bench. The balloon's tiny lift made the flags breathe. A line of children with their grown-ups paused. "What is the leaf?" a boy asked. Lars answered without grabbing the rope. "Please and thank you. If you like a ride, the leaf is the polite part." The boy told his mother, "I will use my leaf voice." Ms. Calder laughed. "Then the flag is already working." She helped the next riders in, a grandmother and a grandchild, and the basket rose just enough to make the flags stroke the air. Feet were still near the grass. Everyone smiled at the simple height.

Lars walked a little way, still on the fair path, and looked back. The flags turned his invention into a public kindness. People pointed at the circle and waved as if answering. He waved too. "They speak circle," he told Papa. "Even without a lesson." Papa said, "A clear mark is a kind of teaching." Lars sat and made two more flags: a dot inside a ring for "I see you," and a spiral for "slow." He held them up like a tiny show. A toddler copied the spiral with a finger in the air. Lars felt creativity leave his blanket and walk around the common on other people's hands.

Ms. Calder called him back when the rides paused. "The bows held. Nothing tangled a rope. That is good inventing." She let him untie two flags so the set could rest, and she left two up for the next short lift. "May I leave the hello and the leaf?" Lars asked. "Yes," she said. "They match the way we run this balloon. A hello, and manners." Lars flushed with pride that did not need to be loud. He rolled the spare flags into a tube and slid them into Papa's basket beside a bottle of water.

They bought nothing with a logo and needed nothing. The fair's own paper cones of plain popcorn smelled buttery nearby, and Papa shared a small cone they had brought from home in a tin, which was just as good. Lars listened to the balloon's fabric tick in the breeze. "I could make a flag for home," he said. "A circle for the door, so visitors know we are glad before they knock." Papa nodded. "We can tape it to the inside of the window, where weather cannot take it."

On the walk back, Lars taught Papa the marks again. Papa drew a leaf in the air and said thank you for the afternoon. Lars said please and thank you in one leaf, which made them both grin. The common's noise thinned to birds and a distant train that stayed on its track. Lars patted the tube of flags. Ideas, he decided, liked fresh air and a person who asked permission.

The lantern by the window glowed softly behind the hello flag Papa taped to the glass. The circle shone twice, once in ink and once in light. Lars set the spare squares in a box labeled with a tiny leaf. "Tomorrow I might make a flag for the canal dock," he said, "a bench, so people remember to sit while they wait their turn." Papa smoothed a ribbon. "Then your creativity will keep greeting the neighborhood." Lars watched the glow and practiced the signs once more with quiet fingers, a language small enough to hold and kind enough to share.
