Vito tipped his box of ticket stubs onto the rug and listened to the soft paper rain. There was a stub from the little train, clipped at the corner. There was a bus stub with a smudge of pencil. There was a fair stub with a tiny pear mark from the tethered balloon line, where he had watched a basket rise only a few feet and had felt glad just to see it. For weeks the stubs had lived in the box like quiet crackers. "They are only scraps," he told Dad. "They rode, and then they stopped meaning." Dad sat cross-legged. "Scraps are how pictures start, if you give them a new job."

Vito fetched a large sheet, the back of a calendar page that had no shiny shop names left on it, only empty squares. He set the train stub near the bottom. "This can be the engine's ticket window," he said. With a pencil he drew a small green and cream train around it, not copying any famous engine, just a friendly one with round eyes he immediately changed into plain lamps, because he wanted a real-feeling train, not a toy face. "Eyes were silly," he said. "Lamps are honest." Dad nodded. "Honest lamps light the drawing."

The bus stub became a window. Vito drew the village bus around it, tall panes, a driver who was a simple shape with a kind arm. Through the stub-window he sketched what he remembered: a red door, a hedge, a kite comma. His memory was not perfect, and he decided that was allowed. "This is a memory picture, not a rule book," he said. He added himself as a dot with hair, and Dad as a taller dot. Creativity, he felt, was permission to build with what you actually had, not with what you wished you had bought.

The pear-marked stub puzzled him. A balloon was round, and the stub was a rectangle. He turned the rectangle sideways and drew the basket first, so the stub was the basket weave. Above it he drew a great pear of cloth held by ropes to stakes he made extra clear. "Only a little lift," he said, and he showed the basket near the grass, a child and an auntie safe inside, hats still close to hats. Dad smiled. "You told the truth in the picture. Safe can be part of the art." Vito colored the balloon a soft apricot with pencil, pressing lightly so the paper would not tear.

Empty squares of the calendar still showed. Vito did not like the holes. He drew a path of small arrows connecting train, bus, and balloon, a made-up day that could really happen close to home. He added the post box, the common, and the hill only as a humble bump, because he had not climbed today and would not pretend. In one square he wrote, "Scraps can be a trip." The letters wandered and then straightened. He erased a heavy line that looked like a crack and replaced it with a bench. "Benches are friendlier than cracks," he said.

Dad brought paste, the washable kind. "May the stubs stay on the picture, or do you still want them in the box?" Vito thought. If he pasted, the box would be empty, and the picture would be whole. "They can live here," he said. "The box can start a new collection tomorrow, and I will not peel these off in a grump." He pasted the edges, leaving the middles unwrinkled. His fingers got a little sticky. He did not mind. Stickiness felt like making. When the stubs sat inside the drawings, they stopped looking like leftovers and started looking like windows with jobs.

He held the sheet up to the real window to see the light through the pencil. The apricot balloon glowed faintly. "It is a lantern picture already," Vito said. Dad tilted his head. "It is a story you can walk with your eyes. Where does the eye start?" Vito pointed at the train. "Start here, ride to the bus, look out, then visit the fair, then home along the arrows." He walked his finger and made a soft train sound, then a bus sigh, then a tiny balloon breath. The sounds made him laugh. "Art can be a little noisy if the neighbors are only us."

They cleared the rug so no stub could slide under a chair. Vito kept one blank scrap aside on purpose, a stub from nothing, just paper. "This one is for a ride I have not taken," he said. "Maybe the canal boat on a slow day. I will draw it when I have really gone, so I do not invent a wave that was not there." Dad looked proud in a quiet way. "Then your creativity tells the truth and still feels new." Vito put the blank scrap in the box like a promise, not a disappointment.

The lantern by the window glowed softly behind the big sheet, turning pencil gray into warm gray and apricot into evening. Vito did not ask to hang it on a high wall yet. He let it lean where he could touch the train stub and remember the clip. "They were only scraps until I gave them a ride," he said. Dad rested a hand on the rug. "And you were the one who knew they were waiting." The glow stayed on the paper paths, a small map of near adventures, made by hand and happy to be homemade.
