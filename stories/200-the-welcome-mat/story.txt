Ekon stood at the end of the front path with a painted cardboard mat under his arm. The paint was dry, blue on one half and yellow on the other, like a small sky meeting a small sun. Dina's new house was calm and pale, with a window open and a curtain breathing in and out. Boxes made a neat stack on her porch. Ekon's hello sat in his throat like a pebble he did not want to swallow or spit. Papa waited a step behind him, holding a jar of crackers with a picture label that showed a square cracker and the words "to share."

"You can practice on me," Papa said. Ekon faced him. "Hello. I live on this side. This mat is so your step can feel expected." His voice worked on Papa. On the path, it shrank. The mat's cardboard edge was soft from sanding, a job Papa had done so it would not scrape ankles. The paint smelled faint and clean, not wet. A bird tried a song in the hedge and then listened, which felt like the whole lane was listening too. Ekon was glad the bird stopped. He needed the path to be ordinary.

They had made the mat that morning because Ekon wanted a reason to walk over that was bigger than his shyness and smaller than a speech. Papa had painted the edges. Ekon had painted the middle, a wobbly line that he decided was a path. "A mat cannot say hello by itself," Papa had told him. "It can stand with you while you do." The jar label was Ekon's too. If his voice hid, the picture could still explain the crackers. Creativity had offered him a handle. Courage still had to turn it.

Dina came to the doorway when she heard their shoes, not a loud sound, just honest ones. She was about Ekon's height, with a box clip in her hand and a curious face. Ekon lifted the mat. His mouth opened. The hello came out as a breath. Dina tilted her head, friendly, not teasing. She had not heard. Ekon's ears warmed. He looked at Papa, who did not jump in to speak the word for him. Papa's eyes said, you can try a second time. The second time is still brave. Sometimes it is braver.

Ekon set the mat down so his hands would have a job, then he stood on his own path again. He filled his chest the way he did before blowing a big bubble. "Hello," he said, and this time the word traveled. "I am Ekon. This is a welcome mat. It is cardboard, so it likes dry shoes. These crackers are for sharing, if you want." The sentence was longer than he had planned. It did not break. Dina's face opened like a window that had been waiting. "Hello," she said. "I am Dina. I was hoping the lane had a voice." She stepped onto the mat, dry shoes, and the wobbly painted path sat under her like it had meant to.

They sat on the step with the jar between them. The crackers were plain and salty and a little dusty in the way good crackers are. Dina told him she had a favorite cup somewhere in a box and did not need it yet. Ekon told her the hedge bird was shy too. They both laughed, quiet laughs that fit a calm house. Papa stayed on the path, near enough to help with the jar lid, far enough to let the hello belong to the children. The lid came free with an easy twist. The label faced up, picture and words matching, which felt like honesty keeping courage company.

Dina traced the painted line on the mat. "It looks like a path that decided to lie down," she said. Ekon liked that. He told her about the first hello that had been only air. She nodded as if she had a drawer full of air-hellos herself. "The second one reached me," she said. "That is the one I will keep." They saved the last two crackers for later, a reason to meet again that did not depend on anybody feeling bold all day. The boxes on the porch were still boxes. The house was already less new.

When Ekon walked home, the mat stayed, blue and yellow, doing its slow job. His chest felt wide. Papa did not make a big speech either. He said, "I heard you," which was the right size of praise. The jar was lighter. The label had a crumb on the cracker drawing, and Ekon brushed it off so the picture would stay clear. The hedge bird tried its song again. This time Ekon did not need it to stop. He could walk beside a sound and still feel brave.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in Ekon's house and, a moment later, in Dina's, two small lights along the same lane. The welcome mat on her step held the last of the color, a painted path that had done its work. Courage had sounded like a quiet hello said twice, and the evening was calm enough to let that hello rest between the houses, warm and enough.
