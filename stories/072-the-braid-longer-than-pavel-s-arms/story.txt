The braid came out of the bread oven longer than Pavel's arms. It was glossy with honey, dotted with orange peel, and dusted with cinnamon. It smelled like the whole Saturday market had moved indoors. Emile stared. Nkechi stared. The picnic basket they had planned to use sat on the shared table, honest and too small.

"It will not fit," Pavel said. "If I bend it, the pretty ropes will crack."

Emile measured the basket with his hands, then measured the braid. The braid won by a lot. "We could bake it smaller next time," he said. "This time it is already this big, and it is beautiful."

Nkechi smiled. "Big can be a sharing shape. One basket was the old plan. The loaf did not read our plan."

They let the braid rest, because hot bread needs a pause before it is moved. Patience kept them from stuffing it. While they waited, they brought a second basket from the hook by the door and a wide plate for the shared table. The soup pots burbled, full of extra for later. Nobody was trying to stretch a tiny meal. They were trying to let a generous loaf be generous.

When the crust had settled into a firm, friendly shell, they eased the braid onto a board. Pavel held one end. Emile held the other. Nkechi walked in the middle like a guide. They did not drop it. They did not giggle so hard that their hands bounced, though a giggle did try.

"Where does a braid like to open?" Nkechi asked.

"At the places it was folded," Pavel said. "If we follow the ropes, it will share itself."

They found the soft joins and loosened them with buttery fingers, slow as untying a gift. The braid became three smaller braids, each still handsome, each still warm. One fit in the first basket with a cloth around it. One fit in the second basket. The third, the middle piece with the most cinnamon freckles, stayed on the plate.

"The middle is for here," Emile said. "So the table gets a share, not only the walks."

Pavel nodded. "And the baskets can go to the stall and the grocer's bench. Two trips, one loaf, no squashing."

They tasted the end piece together so they would know the flavor they were giving away. The crumb was soft. The honey had turned the crust into a quiet shine. Orange peel popped like sunshine in the middle of a bite. Nkechi closed her eyes. "This would have been sad if we had cracked it to force a fit," she said. "Now it gets to be three hellos."

At the market stall they set one basket where shoppers could see the braid and smell the cinnamon. They did not hover as if the bread might vanish. There was more at the bakery. There was more on the table. Pavel told a neighbor the story in one sentence. The loaf was big, so they shared the shape. The neighbor laughed and chose a piece, and another piece remained.

Back at the shared table, Emile poured water. Nkechi set out honey in case anyone wanted even more sweetness, which was funny, because the braid was already sweet, and still the jar was there, because extra kindness is not a mess. They ate the middle piece in torn, even portions and left the heels for later, wrapped and waiting.

Pavel wrote on a recipe card. If the braid grows longer than the basket, do not bend it. Rest it. Follow the ropes. Use two baskets and one plate. Big bread is a chance to share, not a problem to squash.

They read the card aloud and tucked it under the honey jar. The ovens were quiet. The market was loud in a happy way. Flour dusted their sleeves. Their hands smelled like orange and toast.

"Same dough," Emile said. "More doors."

"Same friends," Nkechi said. "More baskets."

Pavel looked at the empty board and the full feeling in the room. "Next time I might braid it long on purpose."

The baskets were already lined with the softest cloths so the smaller braids could travel like guests, not packages. Pavel had tucked a peel curl beside each loaf as a bright flag. Emile had slipped a tiny card under a handle, just an arrow and the word share, so the carrier would remember the middle piece stayed home. Nkechi had tested the weight, and each basket was liftable with one arm, which the long braid had never been. They laughed again at the memory of staring at the oven door, arms wide, loaf wider. The resting time had saved the crust from cracks. The gentle opening along the ropes had saved the shape. Even with the board empty, a little shine of honey seemed to linger where the middle piece had been. Big had become welcoming. That was the size they liked best.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the two basket cloths folded side by side. Pavel, Emile, and Nkechi sat at the shared table with cinnamon on their lips and room in their plans. The loaf had been too big for one container and just right for a morning that knew how to share.
