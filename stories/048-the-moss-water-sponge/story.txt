Arlo knelt in the fern hollow where the moss grew thick as a green sponge and pressed his palm into it. Cool water welled up around his fingers, clear and smelling of leaves and stone-shadow, though he could see no stones bigger than acorns. He had come with a purpose. On the sunny edge of the oak path, a young tree's root-moss had gone pale and dusty, thirsty in a way that made Arlo's own throat feel dry. He lifted a handful of the wet moss, then put most of it back. "Only a little," he told the hollow. "You need to stay plump too. I will carry a drink, not empty your bed."

He shaped the damp moss into a round moss water-sponge and set it in a wide oak-leaf bowl so the water would not drip away on the walk. A gentle fox watched from the ferns, head cocked. Arlo showed the bowl. "This is for the young oak by the picnic side, the one whose feet look tired," he said. "Will you walk with me so I remember to go slowly? Hurry would spill the drink." The fox rose and matched his steps. Together they followed the path past the rope footbridge, where the air was warmer and the light lay in bright patches on the planks.

At the young oak, Arlo saw what he had noticed yesterday. The leaves were still green, which was a relief, but the moss at the roots was thin and grayish, and the soil looked tight. He did not peel anything off. He set the water-sponge against the base and squeezed with a gentle, even hand. Water slid into the root bed, darkening it from dust color to a rich brown. The tree did not say thank you in words, but a single leaf turned and flashed its paler underside, and Arlo chose to believe that was a nod. "There," he said. "A drink for your quiet work."

The fox sniffed the wet ring and sneezed a tiny sneeze that made Arlo grin. "Bless your whiskers," he said. He looked up through the young branches and then back toward the older oaks, whose roots were wrapped in healthy moss like warm socks. "The big ones make shade that keeps water from flying away," he told the fox. "This little one stands in more sun, so it needs a friend with a sponge now and then." He touched the bark, rough and young, and felt how the tree was busy inside, even when it looked only like a post holding up leaves.

They made a second trip, and Arlo was even more careful about taking only a palmful from a different part of the fern hollow so no bald patch would show. He paused at the hollow tree library to rest his arms and to look at a leaf-page that showed drawings of roots. The drawings were simple, made by someone who had paid attention. Arlo added nothing to the page. He only whispered, "I am trying to pay attention too." The fox sat in the doorway, a red-gold shape in the shade, and kept watch over the oak-leaf bowl as if it were a picnic basket. That made the water feel even more important.

Back at the young oak, Arlo poured more slowly. He noticed a curl of fern already growing in the new damp, a volunteer shade-maker for the days ahead. "You can stay," he told the fern. "Shade is a gift." He placed three acorn caps upside down nearby, not as toys, but as tiny roofs to slow the sun on the wettest spot. The fox nudged one cap straighter. Arlo sat back on his heels and wiped his damp hands on his sleeves. The soil smelled awake now, like after a gentle mist. He breathed it in and felt his shoulders settle.

He took out his paper map and marked the young oak with a small blue-ish dot of berry ink mixed thin with water. Beside it he drew a sponge shape so he would remember to visit again another bright day. "Nature is not a picture we only look at," he said. "It is a neighborhood of thirst and shade and roots." The fox blinked in agreement, or at least in comfort. They shared an acorn biscuit under the larger trees, where the moss was already doing its water-keeping job without any help. Helping did not mean the woods were helpless. It meant joining a kindness the moss had started long ago.

Before leaving, he checked that he had not taken too much from the fern hollow. The bed there still looked thick, and the squeezed place had already sprung halfway back. "Good," he said. "You have your own springiness." He bowed a little to the hollow, then to the young oak, feeling only slightly shy about bowing to plants. The fox bowed in its own way, a stretch with front paws out and back curved. Arlo laughed. "That is a better bow than mine." They turned toward home along the path, the empty leaf bowl light in his hands, the afternoon warm on their backs.

The lantern by the window glowed softly as Arlo set the oak-leaf bowl on his mushroom cottage sill to dry. He could still smell the fern hollow on his fingers, green and cool. Outside, the young oak was a darker stroke against the bright edge of the wood, no longer dusty at the roots. Arlo poured himself a cup of water and drank while the lantern painted a gold rim on the bowl. Caring for the wood, he thought, was a walk with a sponge, a promise to take only a little, and a second trip because one drink was a start and not the whole story.
