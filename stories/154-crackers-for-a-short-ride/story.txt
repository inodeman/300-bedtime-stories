Quinn boarded the little train with a tin of crackers under one arm and Mum's hand on the other. They were riding a single stop to the Meadow platform, where the Saturday flower stalls stood, and then they would ride straight home. The carriage was nearly empty and smelled like warm metal and oranges. Quinn chose a seat with a table the size of a book. "I will not open the tin until we are moving," Quinn said. "Crackers are a traveling food." Mum laughed. "Then they will feel important."

Across the aisle, a family settled. There was a father, a small child named Noor, and an older child named Esti. Esti held a paper bag of bread rolls with great ceremony. The train jerked politely as it started, a cat-stretch of a jerk. The bag tipped. Rolls did not scatter everywhere, but one broke, and a handful of crumbs made a constellation on the table. Noor looked at the crumbs as if they were a ruined drawing. "Our snack went to pieces," Esti said, embarrassed, already cupping the bigger bits into a napkin. "We still have apples at home. Just not here."

Quinn felt the tin's weight. It was full. The old pinch of mine showed up and then sat down, because the carriage was a shared room on rails. "We have crackers," Quinn said. "They are square and a little salty, and there are enough for a ride this short. Would you like some?" Mum nodded, glad to be asked with eyes before the tin opened. Esti hesitated. "Only if you still have your own." "I counted," Quinn said, which was almost true and then became true as Quinn counted aloud. "Eight for you and Noor and your dad, eight for us. The tin is a fair."

They ate as the hedges slid by. Crumbs of the broken roll were wrapped, not brushed onto the floor. The father offered orange slices from a little box that had survived the jolt. "Sharing goes both ways on this train," he said. Juice made Quinn's fingers shine. Noor held up a cracker like a ticket. "Square ticket to cheese land," Noor said, though there was no cheese, and everyone smiled anyway. The conductor passed, saw the neat napkin, and gave them a thumbs-up that was really a thank-you for keeping the carriage kind.

Quinn watched the slice of orange on the napkin and thought about how the tin felt lighter and the table felt more like a table for friends. "If I had hidden the crackers, we would be quiet and still hungry in different ways," Quinn said to Mum. Mum dabbed a sticky finger. "Hunger of the 'I wish I had' kind. You skipped that." Esti heard and said, "You also skipped us having a sulk. Sulks are heavy luggage." They giggled. The train hummed a steady note. Fields turned like pages. Nobody needed the ride to be longer than it was.

At Meadow platform the flower stalls were bright and close to the exit, so nobody could wander. They looked with their eyes and with one gentle nose-lean toward the mint pots. Quinn did not pick stems. The stall keeper gave each child a fallen mint leaf that had already left its stalk. "For the ride back," she said. Quinn tucked the leaf into the tin, now holding a green smell where crackers had been. Esti tucked theirs into the napkin bundle. "We can remember the train by a smell," Esti said. "And by squares."

On the return, they sat together on purpose. The tables held fewer snacks and more talk. Noor counted bridges, and there was only one small footbridge, so the counting was easy and funny. Quinn told them about the cracker plan. "I will pack a few extra next time, not a mountain, just a bridge between tins." The father said, "Then our oranges will try to be a bridge too." It was not a trade that kept score. It was a habit trying to be born. Mum looked content, the way she looked when a day needed nothing added.

When they stepped off at their own platform, the stub in Quinn's pocket was clipped neat. Esti waved from the window until the little train curved away, still slow, still safe. "The wheels shared the trip, and we shared the food," Quinn told Mum on the lane. "Maybe sharing is round, like a wheel." Mum swung the empty-ish tin. "Round and rolling toward somebody. I like that." The flower smell of mint followed them farther than Quinn expected, a small green reminder of the stall, only a scent that knew the way home.

The lantern by the window glowed softly on the tin Quinn left open so the mint leaf could dry flat. Eight crackers had become a conversation, and the conversation had become a wave at a window. "Next Saturday I might bring squares again," Quinn said. Mum set two cups on the sill, not too close to the edge. "Then the little train will be a table as well as a ride." Quinn watched the glow touch the leaf's veins and felt full in the way that has nothing to do with finishing the last crumb.
