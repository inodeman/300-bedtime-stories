Dalia knew the clock tower's chime the way she knew her own shoes. Every hour it spoke once for the number, low and round, like a spoon tapped on a good cup. People in the plaza did not jump. They smiled, or they finished a sentence, or they counted along. That afternoon she sat by the fountain with a grown-up, peeling a tangerine into one long curl, when the chime came and sounded wrong. It was muffled, as if the bell had put on a wool hat. "That is not its voice," Dalia said.

Her grown-up listened to the next soft attempt as the minute passed. "You are right. It sounds sleepy in a way it should not." Dalia stood. "May we tell the clock keeper? If we only wonder, the plaza will keep hearing a hat on the bell." They crossed the tiles. The keeper, a grown-up with a ring of keys that did not jangle sharply, met them at the tower door. "You noticed," the keeper said, already kind. "I was in the back room labeling oil. Come up with me. The stairs are bright, and we will use the rail."

The stair was wide and the walls were the color of cream. Dalia held the rail and did not try to race. At the landing she saw a small triangle of cedar wood on the step, spicy-smelling, with a worn edge. "Is this supposed to be here?" she asked. The keeper paused. "No. That is the wedge. It sits under the hammer rest so the hammer does not lean on the bell between chimes. If it is gone, the hammer rests too close, and the sound comes out wearing a blanket." Dalia picked up the wedge with two hands. It was light and warm from the stair.

In the clock room the great bell shone like a calm moon. The hammer, padded in felt, drooped nearer than it should. Nothing looked dangerous, only crowded. "The wedge fell when I brushed the shelf," the keeper said. "I did not hear it leave." Dalia held it out. "It wanted to be found." Together they set the wedge back in its notch, the cedar snug, the hammer lifted to a polite distance. "May I listen from close, and then from the plaza?" Dalia asked. "Yes," said the keeper. They waited. The chime spoke. Close up it was larger, but still kind, a round note with air around it.

They went down, not faster than they had come up, and stood by the fountain again. The next small test note, which the keeper sent for them, floated over the water without the wool hat. A child counting paper boats looked up and grinned. Dalia let out a breath. "It was only a little piece of wood," she said, "but the whole plaza hears it." Her grown-up nodded. "Little pieces matter when they are the difference between a clear hello and a mumble." Dalia tucked the tangerine curl into the peel bag so the plaza stayed neat.

The keeper waved from the door. "Thank you for saying the sound had changed. I might have called it a tired day and left the wedge on the stair." Dalia waved back. She thought of how easy it would have been to say nothing, to decide she was fussy. Speaking up had not been a complaint. It had been care for a voice the town loved. The fountain kept its bright coins of light, not money, only sparkles, dancing on the tiles.

She sat again and shared a segment of tangerine with her grown-up. The taste was sharp and sunny. When the true hour came, the chime counted itself clearly, and people around the plaza answered with little nods, the way neighbors do when a familiar friend walks in. Dalia counted too, under her breath, and each number sounded like itself. She felt the cedar smell still on her fingers, a small proof that she had carried the missing piece.

On the way past the tower door, Dalia paused so her grown-up could tie a loose lace. She used the pause to listen once more, the way you check a cup you have just set down. The bell was quiet between hours, which was correct, and the quiet was not the wool-hat quiet. It was a resting quiet. "If it sounds stuffed again, I will say so," she told her grown-up. "Even if I am in the middle of a tangerine." Her grown-up said that was fair. A neighbor waved from the bridge, as if the clear air itself had been mended, and Dalia waved back with sticky, happy fingers.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the tower's lower window as evening leaned toward the plaza, early and gentle, not a darkening to fear. Dalia watched that gold square and listened to the bell rest. Noticing the muffled chime, and telling the keeper kindly, had put the wooden wedge home. The town's kind voice was clear again, and the fountain seemed to glitter in time with it.
