Orla liked to walk the bridge with one hand free and one hand counting. A sea-blue cloth pocket was tied to the rail, and inside it lived ten smooth stones, each painted with dots from one to ten. Small children borrowed them to count their steps and then put them back. Today the pocket's mouth gaped like a surprised fish. Orla looked down, not over the water, but into the dry groove beside the plank. The stones were there, scattered, safe, and out of reach of the river. "They spilled," she said. "The dots are having a muddle."

The bridge mender, her grown-up for the afternoon, knelt to see. "The stitching tired out. If we leave them, the next counter will find an empty pocket and feel the bridge is not for them." Orla set her basket down well away from the rail. "I can gather. I will not lean out. The groove is on the walking side." She picked up the stone with one dot, then two, then the five which had rolled farthest, still dry. Each stone was cool and friendly. "Ten," she said at last, cupping them. "No one went for a swim."

"Good," said the mender. "Now we close the pocket so they can live there again." The mender showed a simple knot: over, through, and snug, with the cloth folded so the mouth could still open for a small hand but not for a sneeze of wind. Orla tried. Her first knot slipped into a loose bow that would not last. "It looks like a knot and acts like a ribbon," she said. "Try again, slower at the through," said the mender. Orla folded the cloth, went over, found the true hole, and pulled until the knot sat like a small pebble of thread. She tugged kindly. It held.

She slipped the stones back in order, one dot at the front, ten at the back, so a child could meet them in a line. The pocket accepted them and did not sag open. "May I test it the way a counter would?" Orla asked. She took out three, walked three steps, and put them back. The knot stayed. She took out all ten, counted them in her palm, and returned them. "It works," she said. A real small child arrived with a grown-up and looked shyly at the pocket. "Are they for me too?" the child asked. "Yes," said Orla. "They were lost for a little, and now they are home. Start with the one-dot if you like."

The child walked ten steps, placing each stone in a careful pile at the end, then Orla helped put them back without taking over. "You did the count," she said. "I only held the pocket open." The child smiled as if the bridge had spoken a welcome. Orla felt the kindness land. She had not built the bridge. She had put back the small things that made a child brave enough to cross while counting. The mender trimmed a thread end. "A pocket is a little room," the mender said. "You made the room trustworthy again."

Orla checked the other side of the bridge, in case a second pocket had gaped. It was firm. She liked checking. Guessing that one mend fixed every cousin of the problem was how stones spilled twice. The river moved under the planks, bright and uninterested in dots. Painted wooden fish along the rail faced the market, all agreeing. Orla touched the blue cloth once more. "Stay shut unless a hand asks," she told it, and then felt a bit silly, and then decided the pocket did not mind.

They sat on the bench at the market end and drank water. Orla's fingers remembered the knot: over, through, snug. She practiced once on a spare bit of cord so the memory would be in her hands tomorrow. The mender said she could show another child the knot if a pocket ever yawned. Orla said she would show it slowly, the way it had been shown to her. Teaching, she thought, was gathering of a different kind. You gathered a person into knowing.

When they stood, the clock tower chimed, and Orla counted the notes on her fingers without needing the stones. The stones could rest. They had been spilled and gathered and ordered and tested. The bridge felt like itself again, a place with a small blue kindness at the rail. She looked down the groove and saw only clean wood. That emptiness was the right kind.

Orla patted the sea-blue pocket and listened to the stones click softly, a satisfied little sound. "Ten are home," she said. The mender smiled. "And the knot is a knot, not a ribbon pretending." Orla laughed. She liked the bridge best when its small rooms were trustworthy, and she had helped make this one so. A child could borrow a count and give it back. That was enough work for one kind afternoon.

The lantern by the window glowed softly in the bridge shed window, beside a hook where spare blue cloth waited. Orla hung the practice cord on a peg. Gathering the counting stones and closing the pocket with a true knot had made the bridge welcoming for the next small counter. She walked toward the market lamps with her grown-up, the ten dots safe and in order behind her.
