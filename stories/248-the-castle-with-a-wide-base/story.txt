Oona set a big cardboard box in the middle of the rug and smelled the dry, papery scent that always made her think of parcels and possibilities. Piotr slid two smaller boxes beside it. Tape, crayons, and a sheet of red paper waited in a basket. Papa had helped cut one window yesterday, a neat square with safe edges, and then put the scissors away. Today the children would fold, tape, draw, and build. "A castle," Oona said, and her voice came out smaller than the word. Tall things sometimes fell when she built them. Her stomach felt fluttery, like a moth that was not ready to land.

"We can start wide," Piotr said. "Wide is a kind of brave. It gives the height a friend." He set the big box as the hall and taped a smaller box to its side for a tower, pressing the tape until it sang a sticky sound. Oona drew stones on the walls, round gray shapes with her crayon whispering. She liked the sound. It made her shoulders drop. Then she tried to stand a third box on top for a lookout. It wobbled. It tipped. It slid off and bumped the rug with a hollow thump, not a wild crash, just a cardboard sigh. Oona's eyes stung. "I knew it," she whispered. "Tall is not for me."

Piotr did not laugh. He set the box upright again and sat on his heels. "The thump was information," he said, a word he had heard Papa use, and then he made it simpler. "It told us the top needs a wider friend or a tape hug. Want to try one new way before we decide?" Oona swallowed. Courage, she thought, might be a small nod. She nodded. They cut no cardboard. They tucked a folded paper bridge inside the seam and wrapped tape all the way around, Oona pulling, Piotr holding the box still. The tape smelled faintly sharp. When they let go, the lookout stayed. Oona put one finger on it. It did not flee.

She let out a laugh that surprised her. "It stayed." "You stayed with it," Piotr said. They made a door from a flap that already existed, bending it so it could open and close. Oona drew a friendly path of yellow dashes from the door to the edge of the rug. Piotr colored a red paper flag and taped it to a paper straw Papa had given them. The straw bent. The flag drooped. Oona felt the old flutter. This time she spoke before the flutter could grow. "I am going to try a shorter straw piece and a sturdier tape collar." Her hands shook a little. She did it anyway. The flag stood, a red leaf above the lookout.

They crawled inside the hall box. It was dim and warm and smelled like crayon and cardboard. Light came through the cut window in a square of gold. "We need a welcome," Oona said. She wrote, in careful letters on a card, "Come sit. There is room." Piotr added a drawn cup of tea, a simple smile-shaped cup, nothing fancy. They were not trying to make the castle look like it belonged to a king in a film. It belonged to anyone who was gentle with the tape. When Mama looked in, Oona felt the wish to hide the crooked stone she had drawn. Instead she crawled out and said, "The tower fell once. We fixed the hug. Would you like to see the window light?"

Mama knelt and peeked. "It glows," she said. "And the flag is trying its best in the best way." Oona felt pride sit down beside her instead of jumping up and down. She did not say the castle was better than any other. She said, "I was scared of the thump, and I tried again." Piotr bumped her shoulder kindly. "That is the bravest brick in the wall." They invited Mama's teacup to rest on a box ledge, a real cup this time, empty and light, just to see if the ledge held. It held. The cardboard creaked and then went quiet, doing its job.

They added a pebble path outside, smooth stones from the dish by the door, placed not thrown. Each pebble clicked softly. Oona made a tiny curtain from a scrap of blue cloth taped above the window so it could be tied back. Piotr tested the door ten times, and on the eleventh it stuck. They loosened one tape loop, a kindly repair, and the door swung again. "Finished," Oona said, and the word felt steady. Her moth-stomach had landed. She could hear the house around them, a clock, a bird, the small sound of Mama setting the kettle, a homey hiss far away in the kitchen.

The castle sat in the middle of the rug, gray stones drawn by hand, red flag up, window full of day. Oona and Piotr sat outside it like neighbors who had done a good chore. Their fingers were tacky from tape. They peeled a bit of tape off a thumb and laughed when it made a tiny rip sound. Nothing about the castle was perfect. Everything about it was theirs, mended where it had slipped, and brave in the way only a second try can be.

The lantern by the window glowed softly across the cardboard stones and turned the red flag into a deeper rose. Oona watched the light and felt tall on the inside, even while she stayed sitting. Piotr rested his chin on his knees. The castle was finished, the door moved, and courage had sounded a lot like tape, a nod, and a thump that did not get the last word.
