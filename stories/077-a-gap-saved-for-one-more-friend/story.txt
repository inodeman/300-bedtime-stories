The shared table looked full before Tessa arrived. Picnic baskets sat on the benches. Honey jars made a glittering fence down the middle. A platter of cinnamon rolls left only a narrow path for cups. Reina and Pavel had saved their favorite spots by the window, and the spots seemed to be the last flat places. Outside, the Saturday market bustled. Inside the neighborhood bakery, bread ovens hummed and soup pots steamed, abundant and warm.

Tessa stood in the doorway with a small bunch of orange peels and a hopeful face. "Is there room for one more bun and one more friend?" she asked.

Reina looked at the baskets and felt a pinch of worry. "We wanted you here," she said. "The table dressed itself in too many things."

Pavel lifted a basket and peered under the bench. The space below was empty and clean. "The baskets are sitting in the seats that friends need," he said. "They can sit on the floor instead. Baskets do not need a view."

They made a plan, the way friends do when a problem is small and the care is big. Reina slid her basket underneath, slow so the rolls inside would not tumble. Pavel moved the honey jars closer together without crowding their labels. They folded an extra cloth and turned it into a cushion of space rather than a pile. Patience kept them from shoving. A jar clinked and stayed whole.

"Try now," Pavel said.

Tessa sat. Her knees fit. Her peels fit on a little dish. The window light reached her hands. She laughed. "I thought full meant finished. Full meant we had not looked underneath."

Reina passed her a cinnamon roll, still soft, still plenty on the platter. "We were saving the view and forgetting the welcome," she said. "Friendship can rearrange a table."

They invented a gentle game while the soup was ladled. Who could shape a bit of extra dough into the roundest bun? The dough was leftover from the morning, soft and more than enough, set aside for play and then for baking, not for waste. Tessa rolled a neat moon. Reina's was a little lopsided and made them grin. Pavel's was round as a marble and then got a fingerprint dimple, which he decided was a window. Nobody won with a prize that left someone out. They decided every bun was round enough to be a friend. Into a small pan they went, and the oven took them with a hum.

While they waited, they talked. Tessa told them the peels were for a glaze if the new buns wanted shine. Reina admitted she liked the end seat because the lantern would be near later, and she had not known how to say there was room in her liking for more people. Pavel said he sometimes stacked things high when he felt excited, and excitement could look like a wall. They nodded. The talk felt as warm as the cups.

The little buns came out golden. They brushed them with honey and orange. The smell rose in a bright cloud. They put the buns in the middle, not in a private corner. The big platter of rolls was still generous. The soup bowls were full. Under the bench, the baskets rested like quiet animals who had found a better nap.

"Sit by the lantern side with me," Reina said to Tessa.

"And leave a gap for the next knock," Tessa said. She shifted a cup to mark a future place. The gap was small and real.

Pavel wrote a note on a recipe card. If the table looks full, look underneath. Baskets can move. Friends should not have to. Then share the round buns, dimples included. He tucked the card beside the honey. A friendly hello floated from the market path, and they waved, saving the gap in case that neighbor turned toward the door. The wave was part of the new habit.

They ate until they were content, with crumbs like confetti and peels like confetti too. The ovens ticked. The pots settled. Reina's favorite view was still there. It was simply wider.

"I like this shape better," Pavel said, looking at the open middle.

"Round enough," Tessa said, and they knew she meant the buns and the welcome.

The honey shine on the buns had settled into the crumb, sweet and steady. Tessa turned the last round bun so its dimple faced the window. Reina said the dimple looked like a seat. Pavel said every bun could have a seat, which was the point of the morning. They shifted the jars a finger's width farther apart and checked the gap they had saved. A basket under the bench did not complain. The soup bowls still sent up a faint orange-scented steam. Cinnamon from the rolls mixed with it until the table smelled like both the oven and the fruit bowl. Tessa's peels had become glaze and decoration, a small gift that fit because they had made space. "Full used to mean packed," she said. "Now it means friends, bread, and a place left open." They smiled at the gap as if it were another bun.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the new gap and the honey-bright buns. Reina, Pavel, and Tessa sat close, but not so close that another chair would sigh. Friendship had moved a basket, made a moon of dough, and kept the shared table open to the next kind knock.
