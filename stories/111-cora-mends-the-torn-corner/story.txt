A tiny tear smiled from the corner of a picture book on the town library table, a white nick in a page about rabbits and garden rows. Cora saw it when she turned the page and heard a small gritty sound, not a loud rip, just a warning. She stopped her hand. The paper felt thin there, like a leaf that had dried too fast. Ada sat beside her on the reading rug with a spool of mending paper and a glue stick they kept in the care basket. The library smelled like sun on dust and the orange someone had peeled at the desk earlier. Cora held the page open like a bird that needed a quiet lap.

"It wants a bandage," Cora said. "If we turn past it, the tear might grow, and the rabbit's ear would split in two." Ada leaned in. Her breath was soft and did not flutter the page. "We can make a hinge on the back, a little strip that holds the corner and still lets the page turn." She had learned that from a picture in the care basket itself, drawn by kids with arrows and smiles. Cora nodded. Kindness to a book was still kindness. The next reader was someone they might never meet, and that person mattered.

They slid a clean card under the torn page so glue would not mark the rabbit on the other side. The card was cool and smooth. Ada tore, not cut, a narrow piece of mending paper so the edges would be soft. The tear of the mending paper was fuzzy and white. "Little pieces," she said. "A flood of glue would wrinkle the story." Cora dotted the strip with glue that smelled faintly sweet, then they counted to ten together while it got tacky. Their counting was a whisper. Ten arrived. They set the strip behind the tear, pressed with two fingertips, and waited again. Pressing too soon would slide the repair. Waiting was part of being gentle.

Cora tested the turn. The page rose and settled, and the rabbit's ear stayed one ear. The corner did not catch her nail. "It works," she said, and her smile felt like a light being turned up. Ada smoothed the whole page with the side of her hand, a slow iron of skin. They found one more weak spot near the gutter, not torn yet, only creased. They did not cover the words. They left that crease, because not everything needed fixing, and the crease showed the book was loved. "We mend what can grow into a problem," Cora said. "We let the loved marks stay."

They wrote a tiny note on the pocket card. "Corner hugged back together. Please turn me slowly." Cora drew a small bandage that looked like a smile. Ada added, "Thank you for reading with clean hands." They were helping the next pair of hands. The glue smell faded as the sun warmed the table. Outside the window, a tree shadow moved across the rug like a slow fish. Cora read the rabbit page aloud now that it could be turned, her voice round and calm. Ada pointed at the carrots, the watering can, the rabbit's whiskers, each one whole.

Stefan paused nearby, holding a seed jar carefully against his shirt. "Is the story all right?" he asked. Cora showed the neat corner. "It had a small ouch, and we gave it a hinge," she said. Stefan's eyes softened. "I will turn it with two fingers when it is my turn." He did not grab. He put his name, in his mind, next in line, and went to set the jar on the window shelf. Cora liked how kindness made a line of careful people without anyone having to be told twice. The care basket looked proud, if a basket could.

They checked the other books on the table with quick gentle flips, listening for that gritty warning sound. Most pages sang a normal paper song. One book had a loose signature stitch, too tricky for glue, so they set it on the helper shelf with a note that said, "Needs a grown person's needle, please, when one is here." They did not pretend they could do every repair. Honesty kept the book safer. Cora washed a smear of glue from her thumb. The water was cool. Ada capped the glue stick so it would not dry out and be useless for the next tear.

Cora hugged the rabbit book lightly, then put it on the low shelf where small hands could reach, repaired corner facing out so people would see it was ready, not hidden. "A mended thing can still be the one you choose," she said. Ada agreed. "Sometimes it is the one you choose because you know it was cared for." They sat back on the rug, knees warm, and let the room be quiet enough to hear pages behaving.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the care basket, and the mended corner caught a little of that glow. Cora watched it, glad they had stopped at the first small sound, and glad the rabbit could hop through the story in one piece.
