Sylvain stepped onto the balcony in quiet slippers and bent toward the clay pot of mint. The leaves brushed his nose before his eyes had finished looking, and the smell was cool and sweet, like green candy the night had made. A cricket sang inside the shade of the pot, a creaking little rhythm, then paused as if listening back. Sylvain did not poke the leaves apart. He set his hands on his knees and let the garden introduce itself. Behind him the window lamp painted a gold path to the door. Hot milk waited on the indoor table. The moon rested above the rail like a calm visitor.

Grandma joined him with the quilt over one arm, in case the air felt chilly, and she spoke softly. "Night gardens are awake in their own manners. We can be guests." Sylvain nodded. He noticed dew beginning to silver the mint edges. He noticed the soil was cool and damp, not thirsty. He noticed the cricket sang again once their voices settled. "How does it make that sound?" he asked. Grandma answered gently. "It rubs a part of its wing the way you hum in your throat. It is music, not a fuss. We will leave the player in its little hall."

Sylvain liked knowing. He also liked not catching. He fetched the small cup they used for plants and gave the mint three careful sips of water, even though the soil was already kind, because Grandma said a little drink at dusk helped the leaves stay soft. He poured slowly so no flood would bother the cricket. One drop rolled down a stem and winked in the lamplight. "The plant is drinking with its feet," he whispered. Grandma smiled. "Roots are quiet drinkers. They do their work underground while we look at the moon."

They looked. Sylvain lifted the cardboard telescope, a simple tube with a paper ring, and rested it on the rail. The moon filled the circle. He told Grandma he could see smudges like thumbprints of flour. She said those were wide stony places, far away and peaceful to watch. Then she helped him find the Big Dipper. "A spoon for a giant's bedtime milk," Sylvain said, and the idea made the stars feel neighborly. The cricket added a note at the end of his sentence, which made them both laugh without being loud.

Back inside, Sylvain washed the mint smell from his fingers only a little, so a hint would stay. He drank his hot milk under the quilt and listened through the open door. The song continued. He drew a leaf in the corner of a notepad and a small cricket shape that was friendly, round, and smiling. He stuck a glow-in-the-dark sticker next to the drawing so the leaf could have a moon of its own when the lamp was turned down. Paper stars on the window clicked faintly against the glass when the breeze changed.

Sylvain still had one more nature job he had chosen. A fallen mint leaf lay on the balcony board, clean and green. He could have crumbled it. Instead he carried it in on a square of paper and pressed it inside a heavy book of constellation pictures, between two smooth pages. "So I can remember the smell's shape," he said. Grandma wrote the word mint in pencil beside it. They did not pick a handful. One leaf was a souvenir. The plant kept the rest for growing and for the cricket's roof.

He stood once more at the door and said goodnight to the pot, the singer, and the spoon of stars. The garden did not need him to own it. It needed him to notice and to be careful. His slippers were damp at the toes from the boards, so he set them near the lamp to warm. The room smelled faintly of milk and leaves. Sylvain felt wide awake in his heart and sleepy in his bones, which is a pleasant way to end a night.

He told Grandma the best part. It was not the telescope and not the milk, though those were lovely. It was the moment the cricket sang back after they had become quiet. "We made room," he said. Grandma patted the top of his hair. "That is how people belong in nature. They make room and they listen." Sylvain pulled the quilt up and let the song be his last story, told in creaks instead of words.

Sylvain opened the constellation book just enough to peek at the pressed leaf, then closed it again so the leaf could stay flat. He set a paper star as a bookmark, a yellow point sticking out like a little flag. "Nature can sit beside our paper things without either one being boss," he said. Grandma agreed and poured a last splash of hot milk. They stood at the window and matched one glowing sticker on the glass to one real star as a game, not a contest. The sticker was near and green. The star was far and white. Both were worth a goodnight. Sylvain yawned and decided the mint smell on his fingers was the best souvenir of all.

The lantern by the window glowed softly beside the book that held one mint leaf. Outside, the clay pot kept its small singer, and the moon kept the balcony in silver. Sylvain closed his eyes while the crickets played on, glad to be a gentle guest in a garden that was beautifully awake.
