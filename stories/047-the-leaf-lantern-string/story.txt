Noor stood between two wide oaks with a string of leaf lanterns pooled at her feet like a green necklace. Each lantern was an open cup of stiff oak leaf, stitched with grass so a firefly could rest inside if it wished and leave whenever it liked. She wanted them hung before warm dusk, high enough to catch the first lights and low enough that no one had to climb. The string was longer than her arms. When she lifted one end, the other end slithered away across the moss. "I need more paws than these," she said, and she did not sound cross. She sounded ready.

A gentle fox came from the ferns, then a second fox with a paler chest, both sniffing the leafy cups with interest. Noor showed them the plan on her paper map, two dots for the oaks and a dotted line between. "If you hold the ends, I can tie the middle to this low branch," she said. "Nobody has to leap. We will keep the string off the ground so dew does not soak the cups." The first fox picked up an end in a soft mouth, careful not to bite through. The second fox took the other end. The string rose, wobbling, beautiful, and a little silly.

Noor laughed as the middle drooped and brushed her hair. "A bit higher, please." The foxes backed up in tiny steps. Their tails stayed low and helpful. Noor stood on a sturdy root, not a risky perch, and looped the string over a branch she could reach with both feet still sure. She tied a knot she trusted, then hopped down and checked the sag. One lantern cupped the air like a green bell. Another tilted sideways. "That tilt needs a friend," she said. The pale-chested fox nudged the tilted cup until it sat open to the sky. Teamwork, Noor thought, was a lot of small nudges.

They worked along the whole string. Noor talked through each task so the foxes could follow. "Hold. Step. Wait. Good." Her voice stayed warm, never sharp, even when a knot slipped and they had to begin that loop again. The second try was neater. She praised the try, not only the success. "We learned how the grass likes to sit," she said. From the rope footbridge path, the string would look like a row of little boats resting in the leaves. Noor wanted walkers to smile, and she wanted fireflies to have a choice, never a trap. Every cup stayed open on purpose.

A third fox, smaller and younger, arrived with an acorn cap stuck playfully on one ear. Noor giggled and lifted the cap off, then showed the young fox how to carry a spare leaf cup without crumpling it. "You can be the cup passer," she said. The young fox took the job seriously, placing each spare cup by Noor's boot and waiting for a nod. The older foxes kept the string steady. Four sets of efforts made one straight line. When Noor stepped back, the lanterns hung in a gentle swoop, none of them shut, all of them ready for any light that wanted a rest.

They tested the work by resting themselves. Noor poured berry water into three acorn caps and one for herself, a picnic on the moss under the new string. "Holding is as important as tying," she told them. "If you had run off to chase sunbeams, I would still be tangled." The foxes drank and flicked water drops from their whiskers. One drop landed on Noor's wrist, cool as a joke. She did not mind. She unfolded the paper map and drew the dotted line darker, a sign that the plan had become a real thing between real trees. The young fox patted the map with a dry paw, adding nothing but approval.

As the afternoon softened, the first firefly woke early and drifted toward the string. It circled one cup, landed, and glowed a shy gold that made the leaf look lit from inside. Noor put a finger to her lips, not to scold, but to share the quiet. The firefly lifted off again, free as ever, then chose a second cup just because it could. "See?" Noor whispered. "Open doors. Open cups. That is how we invite without grabbing." The foxes watched with shining eyes. Above them the oaks knitted their branches, and the lantern string looked like it had always belonged.

Noor gathered the leftover grass and the unused leaf into her basket so the moss would stay clear for paws. She thanked each fox with a scratch under the chin only if the fox leaned in first. The young one leaned in at once. The pale-chested fox preferred a polite nod. Noor gave both. On the way to her mushroom cottage she could still picture the swoop of green cups and the single firefly trying them out. Her arms were tired in a satisfied way. The work had been too wide for one person, and that had turned out to be the best part.

The lantern by the window glowed softly, a bigger cousin to the little leaf cups still hanging between the oaks. Noor set her basket down and looked out to where a few more fireflies were beginning their evening drift. She hoped they would find the string and also hoped they would ignore it if they preferred ferns. Both choices were good. She sipped cool water and flexed her knot-tired fingers. Teamwork, she thought, was foxes holding ends, a young paw passing cups, and a person who asked for help before the string slithered away into the moss.
