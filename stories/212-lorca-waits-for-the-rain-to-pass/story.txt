Lorca had the light picnic list in one hand and the small bottle in the other. Corin had already checked the basket: apple tin, cloth, two cups, and Moss's little hay net, nothing extra trying to sneak aboard. Moss stood by the gate, ears forward, ready for a lane that felt fair. Then the sky changed its mind. A soft rain began, silver and steady, tapping the leaves and darkening the path one polite drop at a time.

Lorca's shoulders rose toward the plan. "It is only rain," Lorca said. "We can go between the drops." Corin looked at the shining stones. The grown-up looked at Moss's dry hooves and at the children's canvas shoes. "We can go after the drops," the grown-up said. "The meadow will keep. A slippery lane is not a better adventure. Waiting is a way of caring for ankles and for donkeys."

Lorca sat on the porch bench with a thump that was almost a protest and then, because the thump sounded ruder than the feeling, smoothed it into an ordinary sit. The basket waited. Moss was led only as far as his open shelter, where rain could not needle his back, and the lead came off so he could stand as he liked. He could see the porch. He blinked through the gray air and did not seem heartbroken. Lorca tried to borrow that calm.

They played a quiet clap game. Corin clapped a pattern. Lorca answered. The grown-up kept score with pebbles in a cup, one pebble for each matched pattern, no prize except the pleasure of getting it right. Rain threaded the garden. Under the hedge, two rabbits stayed dry and washed their ears. Lorca watched them and did not call them out. "They know how to wait too," Lorca said. The sentence helped. Patience looked less like doing nothing and more like doing the right small things while the big thing rested.

Emeka would have counted breaths. Lorca counted rain sounds instead. Taps on the bucket. Taps on the sheet Kira had folded away. A softer hush out in the meadow grass. The hum of bees had paused, which was wise of them. Lorca's wish to hurry paced around the porch and then sat down. "I think I can let the path drink first," Lorca said. Corin passed a pebble into the cup as if that sentence had matched a pattern too.

The rain grew thin. The sky lifted from gray to a pale gold. Drips fell from the eaves one by one, like a clock finishing its chore. The grown-up touched the path with a shoe and nodded. "Kind footing now." They greeted Moss, settled the same light basket, and walked out. The lane shone. It did not grab at hooves. The meadow smelled bigger, like wet mint and clean dust. Every leaf held a bead, and Lorca did not need to rush to see them because they waited in plain sight.

They ate under the oak on the cloth, which was only a little damp at the corner. Moss chewed hay in the brightened shade. A heron stood far off at the pond, unbothered, as if rain and not-rain were both ordinary parts of a patient day. Lorca lifted a cup. "To waiting," Lorca said, and Corin laughed and agreed.

Lorca helped wipe Moss's water bucket, though it was not very wet, because waiting had left a leftover helpfulness in both hands. The donkey leaned into a scratch along his neck and then returned to dozing. Lorca looked at the lane, now only damp in the shady parts, and felt proud of the feet that had stayed on the porch. "We did not miss the meadow," Lorca told Corin. "We met it when it was ready to be kind under hooves." Corin agreed and shook a last drop from the cloth.

They set the clap-game pebbles back in the garden, each pebble a tiny medal nobody needed to wear. The rabbits had come out from the hedge to nibble wet grass tips. Lorca watched without calling them. Patience had stretched from the rain into the way they looked at neighbors. Inside, the apple tin was rinsed and upside down. The day had not been shortened by the wait. It had been shaped by it, rounder and safer, with gold light on every rinsed leaf.

Lorca stood with the grown-up at the gate and named three things the wait had protected: Moss's steady feet, the children's dry shoes, and the rabbits' choice to stay under the hedge until the grass glittered instead of poured. Nothing on the list was dramatic. That was why it mattered. A patient plan does not need a trumpet. It needs a dry path and friends who are glad they did not rush the sky.

The lantern by the window glowed softly when they returned with dry enough shoes and a donkey who had not been asked to prove anything on a slick lane. Lorca pinned the picnic list back on its nail and added four small words at the bottom: after the rain, if needed. Patience had not stolen the picnic. It had handed the picnic back, safer and sweeter, with the meadow rinsed and ready.
