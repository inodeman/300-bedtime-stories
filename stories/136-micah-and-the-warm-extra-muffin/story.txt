The kitchen smelled like cinnamon and orange zest. Micah stood on a stool at the counter while Grandpa slid a tray of muffins onto a rack. There were six, golden on top and pale at the sides, steam lifting in little ribbons. The rule was to wait until they relaxed, so nobody bit a too-hot middle. Micah could wait for that. What he could hardly wait for was the corner muffin, the one with an extra dome, which Grandpa had said could be his. "Corners are for the baker's helper," Grandpa said. Micah's chest felt shiny with the title.

He set out plates the color of cream and a small dish of butter that softened just by being in the room. Rain had stopped, and the window showed a shiny path. The muffins ticked as they cooled, a tiny sound Micah liked. He leaned in and smelled the dome. It promised a soft middle and a cinnamon top. Then a knock came, polite and not very loud. Grandpa wiped his hands. "See who it is, and I will be right behind you," he said. Micah opened the door to the length of the chain they used for visitors they knew, then wider when he saw the neighbor child holding a clean blue bowl.

"Mama said to return this," the child said. The bowl had held soup last week. The child's cheeks were pink from the walk, and the path behind was wet enough to shine. "I am Ellis." Micah remembered Ellis from the garden fence, a waver of hands, not yet a kitchen guest. The corner muffin waited on the rack like a secret he had not told. He could say thank you and close the door on a nice smell. Ellis's eyes flicked, not greedy, just hopeful in the way warm kitchens make people hopeful. Micah felt the shiny feeling turn into a question. Helper, Grandpa had called him. Helpers share the warm part.

"Come in while Grandpa finds the lid," Micah said. Ellis stepped onto the mat and dried shoes with care. The kitchen wrapped them in cinnamon. Micah pointed at the rack. "They need another minute, and then there is one I was going to keep. We can split the domed one, or you can have it and I will take a side." He heard himself offer the better choice second. He let it stand. Ellis blinked. "A side is plenty if we sit. I mostly came for the bowl." Grandpa, arriving with the lid, nodded as if the offer were a recipe he trusted.

They waited the last minute together, watching the steam thin. Micah told Ellis about zest rubbed on sugar. Ellis told Micah about a spoon that had sung against the bowl on the walk, a silly song. When Grandpa said ready, Micah lifted the domed muffin with a napkin and broke it. The middle was pale, tender, and not too hot. He gave Ellis the taller half and kept the shorter, and the giving did not pinch after the first breath. Butter melted into both pieces and made gold maps. They ate at the table with milk in small cups. Crumbs gathered like sand on the cream plates.

"This is the best return trip a bowl ever had," Ellis said. Micah laughed and wiped a cinnamon speck from his thumb. Grandpa packed two side muffins into a little tin for Ellis to carry home, so the kindness would have a second chapter. Micah did not argue. The tray still held enough for their own supper. He walked Ellis to the door and held the tin until shoes were on. The path shone. "Come wave at the fence," Micah said. "I will wave with whatever is on my hands, even zest." Ellis promised. The blue bowl stayed, clean and at home on the shelf.

Micah washed the plates and felt the water warm as muffins had been. He told Grandpa he had almost closed the door on the smell. Grandpa said almost is a draft, and the open door was the weather they chose. Micah liked that. He set the domed muffin's napkin, now empty, in the wash. The problem had been a treasure on a rack and a knock at the right time. Sharing the dome had made the kitchen bigger than six muffins. The cinnamon lingered in his sleeves.

At the table later he drew a muffin with a line down the middle and two smiles above it. Grandpa pinned the drawing to the recipe tin. The evening was quiet enough to hear the rack cool, though the rack was already cool. Micah practiced the wave he would use at the fence. It was a small wave, the kind that means you are welcome again. He was glad the bowl had needed returning on a baking day.

Micah checked the rack once more and found a single crumb shining like a seed. He ate it, laughing at the tiny end of the batch, and wiped the counter until it showed the window's pale square. Grandpa set the timer aside, its bell quiet now that the work was done. The kitchen felt like a story with the bowls washed and the welcome still in the air. Micah practiced saying Ellis's name so tomorrow's wave would come with a word attached. The cinnamon smell thinned into something softer, a reminder rather than a cloud, and he liked reminders.

He put the butter dish in the cool cupboard and listened to the lid settle with a gentle click. The click sounded like a period at the end of a friendly sentence. Micah thought of Ellis walking the shiny path with a tin held in both hands, careful as a person carrying a small sun. The house could spare that sun. There would be breakfast enough, and breakfast is patient. He smoothed the towel on its bar until the stripes hung straight, a little order after a happy mess of crumbs.

The lantern by the window glowed softly over the recipe tin and the drawing of the split muffin. Micah watched the light and smelled cinnamon even after the tray was washed. Somewhere down the path a tin of side muffins was arriving at another table. He hoped Ellis would tell the spoon story again. Kindness, he thought, was a dome broken fairly. He let the kitchen's warmth tuck in the day.
