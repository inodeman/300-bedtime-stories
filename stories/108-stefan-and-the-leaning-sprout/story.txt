Sunlight made a bright square on the town library floor, and in that square Stefan set three jars of seeds from the school science table. The glass was cool at the bottom and warm at the rim. A bean stem in the nearest jar leaned toward the window, its leaves still folded like hands about to clap very softly. Nola sat beside him with a picture book open to a page of green stems. The book smelled like paper and the wooden cart. Outside, the town trees moved only a little, leaves showing their pale backs and then their dark faces.

"Why does it bend that way?" Stefan asked. He did not poke the stem. He let his eyes do the walking. Nola turned the book so the drawing faced the real plant. "This page says leaves drink light the way we drink water at the fountain," she said. "The plant leans because the light is a kind of lunch." Stefan liked that. He could see the difference. The jar farther from the window had a shorter, straighter sprout. The close jar had a curve like a question that had found a direction. "Nature is answering, even without a mouth," he said.

They decided to watch for a few quiet minutes, which is a long time when you are full of wiggles, so they gave the minutes a job. Stefan would notice colors. Nola would notice shapes. He saw the green was not one green. Near the soil it was pale, almost yellow, and at the leaf tip it was deeper, like the chalk the class used for grass. Nola saw an oval leaf, a heart-ish leaf, and a leaf still stuck in its seed coat like a hat it had not taken off. They whispered their notices. The rug under their knees smelled faintly of soap and sun.

A smaller noticing almost slipped by. Tiny droplets clung inside the glass, caught from the damp towel. Stefan fogged the outside with one careful breath and watched the fog shrink back. "The jar makes its own little weather," he said. "Not a storm. Just a sip of wet air." Nola nodded. "And we leave it. The plant knows what to do with that sip." They resisted the wish to wipe the glass perfectly clear. Some of the wonder lived in the blur. Stefan wrote the word light on a paper label and drew an arrow toward the window, so the next friend would see the lean and understand.

Then Stefan wanted the plants to have company from the real outside, not only from drawings. Nola pointed through the glass, not at a far away place, but at the library's low bushes by the step. "We can look with our hands behind our backs," she said. "Looking is a way of visiting that does not pick the garden bare." They stepped out into the bright air. It smelled like warm leaves and the stone of the step. Stefan found a fallen leaf already brown at the edges, a gift the bush had finished using. He held it beside the picture in the book when they came back in. The veins matched like roads on two maps.

"The jar plant is practicing to be this," he said, laying the fallen leaf on a napkin, not on the book. "And the bush was once a small lean toward the light too." Nola smiled. "Everything green has had a first day." They returned the fallen leaf to the soil under the bush so it could crumble into food, and they wiped their hands on their pants, feeling the faint grit of the outdoors. Back at the jars, Stefan turned the far jar a little so its sprout could have a fairer share of window. He did it slowly, two hands, no rush. Nola read one sentence from the book aloud, the one about patience and sun, and her voice matched the quiet of the room.

They made a simple chart on scrap paper. Not a score. A kindness chart. One column said "what we saw." One said "what we left alone." Stefan wrote "leaning stem" and "droplets" and "fallen leaf put back." Nola wrote "did not pick the bush" and "did not shake the jars." The pencil whispered. Stefan felt a calm pride, the kind that sits in the chest like a warm drink. Nature was not a thing to grab. It was a neighbor to notice.

Before they carried the jars back toward school, they stood at the window and let the sun land on their own faces. It felt gentle on Stefan's eyelids when he closed them for a blink. He understood the lean a little more. Light was pleasant. Of course a stem would turn toward it. Nola tapped the picture book shut with a soft papery sound and hugged it to her chest. "We can tell the science table what the library light showed us," she said. Stefan agreed. The jars clinked once, carefully, in his careful hands.

The lantern by the window glowed softly next to the bright square of sun, two kinds of light keeping company. Stefan looked from the flame-shaped glow to the living stem and smiled. The plant had been teaching all morning, and he had finally stood still enough to learn.
